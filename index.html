LESSON 56
Data Deletion & the Right to Be Forgotten: Erasing a User Across an Immutable, Sprawling System
For fifty-five lessons we made data spread and survive — cached everywhere (L48), replicated to 300 edge PoPs (L55), copied into a warehouse (L29), projected into read models (L37), and written to an append-only log we promised never to change (L38). Then a user clicks "Delete my account" and every durability decision becomes a liability. Trace one request — erase user U‑8842, "Alice," from a 200M-user platform. Estimate the copies: she lives in ~15 systems plus 35 nightly backups, so the primary-DB row is ~1‑2% — and truly rewriting the immutable tier is ~35 exabytes/month of restore-and-reseal (impossible), while destroying one 32-byte key erases every copy at once (6.4 GB of keys for all 200M users). Model the kit: a data map (you can't delete what you can't find), two mechanisms chosen by "is this store rewritable?" — true deletion where mutable, crypto-shredding (throw away the per-user key, L30) where not — a suppression tombstone so a restore or replay can't resurrect her, and an idempotent orchestrator (L13/L32/L46) to fan out with retries. Trace the trivial mutable delete (the 1%), the immutable event log where you destroy the key because you can't touch the log (the event survives; its PII payload becomes noise), and the backup/warehouse/edge/third-party sprawl where crypto-shred + retention age-out + forwarded requests (L45) do the work. First wall = structural: you can't delete what you can't find (sprawl) or rewrite what you made immutable (L38), so privacy-by-deletion becomes privacy-by-encryption — born-encrypted per user, "forget this person" collapses to "destroy this one key everywhere." Bounded by key management as the new crown jewel and by legal holds (L25) that make erasure partial, not absolute. Four traps: "DELETE the row" is deletion; rewriting immutable stores per user; forgetting the resurrection path; crypto-shred but a copy of the key survives. Trade named throughout: compliance & privacy vs immutability & derived-data sprawl.
LESSON 55
Edge Computing: Running Code Milliseconds From the User
Lesson 14 pushed cached bytes to hundreds of PoPs; Lesson 50 steered traffic to them. This lesson pushes code — running a small function at the PoP nearest the user so auth, routing, personalization, and filtering execute milliseconds away instead of an ocean from a single origin. Take a global app on 300 edge PoPs fronting a Virginia origin at 40k req/s. Estimate the prize (a ~200 ms round trip skipped — ~215 ms → ~15 ms for a Sydney user) and the trap: split 40k/s across 300 PoPs and 1,000 functions and each function sees ~0.13 req/s (one request every ~7.5 s), so functions keep going cold — and a cold start must stay under the round trip or the edge is slower than the origin. Model why that makes isolates (~5 ms, ~3 MB) non-negotiable where containers (~250 ms) invert the win, and the one rule for state: local-only, eventually-consistent edge KV, or forward home — chosen by how stale the data may be. Trace a request the edge fully answers in ~15 ms (origin untouched), a cold start that wins on an isolate and loses on a container from the same code, and a "Buy" the edge authenticates near the user but forwards home because the truth isn't at the edge. First wall = state: you can push code to 300 places but not a strongly-consistent database (L11/L23), so the edge runs the decision and the origin owns the truth — kept survivable by a thin edge and canary rollouts across the 300-PoP blast radius (L31/L36). Four traps: containers at the edge; authoritative state at the edge; the thick edge; ignoring the 300-PoP blast radius. Trade named throughout: latency & offload vs consistency & operational reach.
LESSON 54
Billing & Metering: Turning a Usage Firehose into a Bill Correct to the Cent
Lesson 53 fed a fraud model the right numbers to guard the money; this lesson follows the money into the bill. A pay-as-you-go platform charges for what customers use — every API call, GB stored, GB shipped — and at month's end billions of tiny usage events must become an invoice correct to the cent: no event counted twice, none dropped, each priced by the plan in force when it happened, every figure provable against the ledger. This is where Lesson 21's friendly "approximate is fine" becomes a liability — a rounding error here is money moved wrongly, and it hurts both ways (undercount → silent revenue leakage; overcount → overbilling, disputes, churn). Take one pipeline metering 50 billion events/day. Estimate the firehose (~579,000/s, 10 TB/day of raw events you must keep, 3.65 PB/yr for audit) and the danger: a quiet 0.5% error on a $50M/month book is $3M/yr mischarged — invisible on a dashboard, seven figures on an invoice. Model the four stages — ingest (dedup an at-least-once firehose to counted-once, L13), aggregate (exact counters, never sketches, L21), rate (tiered + point-in-time pricing: 12.5M calls → $5.50 block by block), reconcile (recompute every derived total against the raw log & ledger, L25) — and the four guards: counted-once, summed-exactly, priced-as-of-then, provable. Trace one event to a line item, a retried duplicate the atomic event_id claim drops before it double-charges, and a month-end close held behind a watermark so stragglers bill to the right cycle. First wall = the dedup memory: promising "never counted twice" means remembering every event id for the whole retry horizon (5.6 TB) — you may cut the lookups with a Bloom filter (whose only error, a false positive, costs an extra check, never a wrong bill) but you may not make the dedup itself approximate. Four traps: counting every arrival; approximating the billed number; rating at today's price; closing on processing-time and trusting the total. Trade named throughout: billing accuracy vs metering cost & latency.
LESSON 53
Feature Stores & ML Serving: Feeding a Model the Same Numbers It Trained On
Lesson 52's moderation funnel leaned on a classifier; Lesson 16's search on a ranker; fraud scorers and recommenders all lean on the same thing. A model is a function, and a function is only as good as its inputs — the features: computed signals like "how many times did this card pay in the last minute" or "this user's average order over 30 days." The glamorous part is the model; the part that decides whether it works in production is getting the right feature values to it, fast, computed the exact same way as in training. Take one fraud scorer approving a payment in ~50 ms. Estimate why you can't compute features at request time: ~200 features × 10,000 txns/s = 2,000,000 aggregations/s (400× a database's safe rate) and ~1,000 ms of latency (20× the budget) — impossible on both axes; precomputed, the answers are just 160 GB served as a 2 ms lookup. Model the feature store: an online store (KV cache, latest-per-entity, fast) + an offline store (warehouse, full history, for training), fed by one shared definition, held to two rules — train-serve consistency (serve the number you trained on) and point-in-time correctness (build each training row from values as they were then, never now). Trace a 2 ms serving read, the per-feature freshness choice (batch for a 30-day average, streaming for a 1-minute count that is the fraud signal), and the training build where a naive join to current values leaks the future → a model that scores 0.99 offline and flatlines live. First wall = skew between the two stores: even one definition can't stop online and offline drifting at the fast-moving moments that matter → log the exact served feature vector and train on it (one number, recorded once). Four traps: computing at request time; training on current values (leakage); two code paths per feature (skew); one freshness for everything. Trade named throughout: feature freshness vs serving latency & training-serving consistency.
LESSON 52
Content Moderation & Abuse Systems: Reading a Firehose No Human Could
Fifty-one lessons built the infrastructure; now we turn to the systems that run on top of it, starting with the one every user-content platform needs or it drowns: content moderation. A firehose of posts must be sorted into spam, fraud, and harmful — faster and cheaper than any room of humans could read it — and getting it wrong costs in both directions: miss real abuse (a user is harmed) or wrongly remove a good post (an innocent is silenced). Take a stream of 500M posts/day. Estimate why humans-read-everything is a non-starter: ~231,000 moderators, ~$9.3B/yr, still slow — then a cheap-model-first funnel (Lessons 16/29's narrow-before-expensive) settles the confident 99.6% in machines and leaves humans just the 0.4% (~926 moderators, 250× fewer). Model the funnel (blocklist → cheap classifier → big model → human), the precision/recall dial where one threshold can't win (at 1% bad: aggressive = 80% recall / 40% precision → 6M innocents/day; conservative = 95% precision / 38% recall), and the two-threshold fix — auto-decide the confident ends, escalate the uncertain band, set per category (favor precision for spam, recall for imminent harm). Trace a near-free clean post, hash-caught spam, an ambiguous post escalated to a human (whose verdict trains the next model), and a false positive rescued by an appeal. First wall = the human review queue: it doesn't scale, so order it by severity × reach not FIFO (a viral post reaches 600k before a FIFO line gets to it), and it's fed by an adversary who makes recall silently decay (fuzzy hashing + a continuous feedback loop). Four traps: one global threshold; a FIFO queue; treating the classifier as "done"; auto-removing with no appeal. Trade named throughout: safety coverage vs false-positive harm & review cost.
LESSON 51
Chaos Engineering & Fault Injection: Breaking It on Purpose to Prove It Holds
Fifty lessons built defenses — timeouts and circuit breakers (Lesson 7), health checks and failover (Lessons 34/42/50), cells (Lesson 36), quorums (Lessons 10/11). Every one is a claim: "when this breaks, the system stays up." But a defense you've never triggered is a hypothesis, not a fact — a timeout might be set to 30 s instead of 1, a breaker might never trip, a "graceful degrade" path might throw — and you find out which on the day it really breaks, at 3 AM, at 100% blast. Chaos engineering moves that discovery earlier and smaller: deliberately inject the fault in a controlled experiment and check the system behaves as drawn. Take a checkout flow across payment / inventory / recommendation services. Estimate the stakes: ~50 resilience mechanisms each ~10% likely silently broken → 0.9050 ≈ 0.5% chance all work → a ~99.5% chance one is already broken; find it in a 1%, 30-s experiment (~1,500 requests) or a 100%, 30-min outage (~9,000,000) = 6,000× less damage. Model the experiment as the scientific method: a steady-state hypothesis in output terms (checkout success ≥ 99.9%), a single fault (with latency the most revealing — slow is harder than dead), a tiny blast radius + control group (Lessons 31/36), and an automatic abort. Trace a passing kill-experiment, the payoff — a latency test that finds a missing timeout letting a nice-to-have sink the critical path (caught on 1% in 28 s), the experiment that goes wrong (the abort shares fate with the partition it injects — Lesson 36's trap in the tooling), and the one that lies (staging passes everything, proves nothing). First wall = the safety of the experiment itself: a small shrinkable blast radius, a safety plane independent of the target, detection faster than harm, and a blameless culture without which no one runs the experiment that finds the bug. Four traps: no hypothesis; unbounded blast radius; a safety plane that shares fate; proving it only in staging. Trade named throughout: confidence in resilience vs the risk of the experiment.
LESSON 50
Global Traffic Management: Steering Every User to the Nearest Healthy Region
Lesson 49 put one front door in front of one fleet; Lesson 23 ran the system live in more than one region. This lesson sits above both: before a request reaches any load balancer, the user's device must be handed an IP to connect to — so which region's address does a shopper in Sydney get, and how fast can that answer change when a region catches fire? A single fixed IP fails twice: it drags everyone to one far region (Lesson 14's speed-of-light tax at continent scale) and, when that region dies, takes down users a healthy region could serve. Take a service in three regions (Virginia, Frankfurt, Singapore). Estimate the win: Sydney→Singapore ~63 ms floor vs Sydney→Virginia ~160 ms → 3 × 97 ≈ ~290 ms off a cold load, and a fixed IP = a 100% outage when its region dies (the spare regions you paid for, unreachable). Model the two places you can steer — DNS-based (GeoDNS/latency: rich control, but bounded by a cached TTL) vs anycast (one IP announced by BGP from many places: seconds-fast failover, but coarse and rough on live TCP) — gated by a health check that is Lesson 34's readiness at region scale. Trace the steady state, a GeoDNS region death (failover bounded by detection + TTL ≈ 90 s), the same death under anycast (reconverges in seconds, at the cost of connection resets), and the false failover where a partition makes a live region look dead — a routing hiccup at worst, a split-brain at worst-worst (Lessons 10/11). First wall = DNS caching: a drain is only as fast as a TTL and caches you don't control (lower TTL 60→20 s shrinks the window but triples DNS QPS) → stop fighting DNS and layer anycast for the fast path. Four traps: a fixed un-steered address; treating DNS failover as instant; health-checking a region from one place; letting routing (AP) decide write-promotion (CP). Trade named throughout: proximity & availability vs routing staleness & complexity.
LESSON 49
API Gateways & the Edge: One Front Door for Many Backends
Lesson 42 balanced load across the fleet; this lesson asks what the single front door in front of it all should do. Once a system is 30 microservices instead of one monolith, a pile of work has no home — checking who the caller is, enforcing the rate limit (Lesson 8), terminating TLS (Lesson 30), matching a URL to a service, stitching six services' answers into one screen for a phone. Push it into every service and you write it 30 times, 30 chances to leave a hole; pull it out to a shared API gateway and you write it once — but now 100% of traffic crosses one box. Take a mobile home screen over 40,000 req/s to 30 services. Estimate the win, each a cost flipped over: cross-cutting security written once vs 30 codebases (30 deploys → 1, no forgotten hole); a 6-call home screen collapsed from 3×100 = 300 ms to 100 + 3×2 = 106 ms by a BFF; and 20% × 40,000 = 8,000 req/s of junk rejected at the door for a token check (Lesson 28's shield). Model the gateway's seven jobs and the identity trick that keeps services simple — authenticate once, forward a signed identity header over mTLS, let each service authorize locally — plus the lines that keep it from swallowing the system: north-south (gateway) vs east-west (service mesh, Lesson 34), one gateway vs per-client BFFs. Trace a clean request, the aggregating home screen, junk killed at the edge, and the partial failure that is aggregation's bill — one slow service sinks the screen unless the gateway holds a per-call timeout + fallback (Lesson 7). First wall = the front door itself, failing two ways: the fattest SPOF you own (fixed as a stateless, disposable herd behind Lesson 42's LB, N+1, drained on deploy) and a deploy bottleneck (fixed by keeping it thin — cross-cutting only, logic pushed out to BFFs, routes as per-team config, or it becomes the ESB reborn). Four traps: the god gateway; treating it as infra that "just works"; re-authenticating at every hop or trusting internal calls blindly; aggregating without timeouts. Trade named throughout: centralized cross-cutting concerns vs a fragile shared choke point.
LESSON 48
Distributed Caching & Invalidation: Keeping Many Caches Honest
Lesson 2 put a cache in front of one database; Lesson 14 pushed copies to the edge. Both times the job was hits. This lesson takes the real shape — many app servers sharing one cache tier over a database whose data keeps changing — where the hard problem isn't the hit, it's coherence: a cache is a copy, and the instant the source of truth moves, every copy is a small lie. Take a product catalog: 50,000 reads/s over 10M products against a DB that safely serves ~5,000/s, changing ~500 times/s. Estimate why one shared tier beats a private cache per server: 286 private caches mean up to 286 stale copies to chase on every write (and 5.72 TB to hold the catalog 286×), while a shared tier holds one copy per key (routed by consistent hashing, Lesson 4) — 20 GB once, one delete to invalidate — and must run at a 99% hit ratio because the DB physically can't serve peak without it. Model the write, where caching gets hard: cache-aside (write DB, invalidate — the honest default) vs write-through (always fresh, slow write) vs write-behind (fast, durability risk), and the sharper fork of delete (idempotent, race-free, but a miss) vs versioned overwrite (no miss, needs compare-and-set, Lesson 6). Trace a hit (the 99%), a miss (populate + TTL backstop), a clean write (source of truth first, then invalidate), and the stale-repopulation race — a slow reader writes an old value back after a newer write, bounded only by a version stamp or a TTL. First wall = adding a per-server near cache to beat the network hop brings N copies back: invalidation becomes a best-effort pub/sub fan-out (500 × 286 = 143,000 drops/s) whose only hard guarantee is a short TTL, and deleting one hot key (10,000/s × 0.010 s = 100 simultaneous misses, Lesson 13) reignites Lesson 2's thundering herd — tamed by single-flight, serve-stale, or versioned overwrite. Four traps: invalidating before writing the DB; overwriting without a version; trusting the fan-out to be reliable; deleting a hot key and expecting the DB to absorb it. Trade named throughout: freshness vs cache hit rate & load.
LESSON 47
Storage Engines: LSM-Trees vs B-Trees — the Write Tax Under Every Database
For 46 lessons a "database" was a black box you put rows into. Lesson 12 cracked it open for reads — the B-tree, fan-out 500, any of 100M rows in ~3 hops — but every write-heavy system since (the ledger of Lesson 25, the event log of Lesson 38, the metrics of Lesson 44) paid a hidden tax: a B-tree keeps data sorted on disk, so inserting a scattered key means a random write, the one thing disks do worst. Take Lesson 44's firehose — 1,000,000 writes/s, 16 B each = a mere 16 MB/s of data, but randomly interleaved keys. Estimate why the B-tree detonates: each 16 B insert forces a read-modify-write of the whole 8 KB page (8,192 ÷ 16 = 512× write amplification), and scattered keys defeat page-batching, so it needs 16 MB/s × 512 = 8 GB/s ≈ 16× the disk's entire sequential bandwidth — or, in ops, ~1,000,000 random writes/s against a disk's few-hundred-to-tens-of-thousands budget (20–10,000× short); no same-class faster disk closes that. Model the write-optimized answer, the LSM-tree (RocksDB, Cassandra, LevelDB): append each write to a sequential WAL + a RAM memtable (16 MB/s = 3.2% of disk), flush the full memtable to disk as one immutable sorted SSTable, and let background compaction merge-sort files down, dropping shadowed values and tombstones — random writes become a sequential trickle. Trace a write, a point read (would probe every SSTable = read amplification, rescued to ~1 file by a per-SSTable Bloom filter, Lesson 21, ~1% false positives at 10 bits/key), a range read the Bloom filter can't help, and a delete that is itself a write. First wall = compaction is a second write workload on the same disk: the sustainable ingest ceiling is bandwidth ÷ write-amplification ≈ 500 ÷ 20 = 25 MB/s, not the WAL's apparent 500 — cross it and the engine stalls writes (Lessons 27/28 inside one database). Four traps: a B-tree under a write-heavy load; assuming the LSM made writes free; expecting Bloom filters to speed range scans; ignoring tombstones & space amplification. Trade named throughout: write throughput vs read amplification & space.
LESSON 46
Distributed Job Scheduling: A Million Cron Jobs, Each Firing Once, On Time
Lesson 45 delivered events the instant they happened; this one delivers them on a clock — not "when the order ships" but "at 3 AM," "every hour on the hour." A scheduled job is work bound to a time (a subscription renewal, a nightly report, a five-minute sweep). Running one on a laptop is the humble cron line; running a million across a fleet so each fires exactly once, on time — even when a box dies mid-tick, even when the whole cluster was down at midnight — breaks on two words that fight: once and on time. Take our platform: 1,000,000 jobs, ~23 fires/s average but wildly spiky. Estimate why one cron box gives you neither: it's a SPOF (a midnight outage silently drops every midnight fire, with no retrier — it's the thing that's down), a throughput ceiling (200,000 × 0.5 ms = 100 s to dispatch the midnight batch serially → two minutes late on the happy path), and a herd (200,000 jobs written 0 0 * * * come due in one tick = ~8,600× the average) → shard the dispatcher (Lessons 3/4/10) and hand work to a queue + workers so the tick never runs a job inline (Lessons 5/9). Model firing safety across machines: a dispatcher that only decides-and-enqueues, a per-shard lease for failover (Lesson 22), an atomic claim (compare-and-set, Lesson 6) so two racing dispatchers enqueue one run not two, and — because a GC pause always lets a paused owner overrun its lease — an idempotent job keyed on (job_id, scheduled_time) that makes the unavoidable double-fire a no-op (Lessons 13/22): the lease bounds liveness, the idempotent job guarantees the effect. Trace a clean fire, the double-fire race (the DB serializes the claim → one run) vs the GC-pause double-fire (two runs → idempotency saves it), and the crash-then-recover where 200,000 overdue jobs force a per-job choice between coalesce (fire once) and backfill (fire each missed occurrence). First wall = the herd the schedule itself makes → jitter the fire times (200,000 over 60 s → ~3,333/s instead of an 8,600× wall, Lessons 7/26) — plus clock skew (Lesson 35) that makes "now" disagree across nodes, fixed by comparing due-ness against one authoritative clock and refusing to promise precision finer than the skew. Four traps: one scheduler / inline execution; assuming a lease gives exactly-once; no missed-fire policy; the midnight herd + trusting local clocks. Trade named throughout: timeliness vs exactly-once execution.
LESSON 45
Webhooks & Outbound Delivery: Telling Ten Thousand Strangers' Servers, Reliably
Every delivery lesson so far pushed a message to a server we own — Lesson 9's queue, Lesson 26's phone, Lesson 33's outbox feeding our own search. This one pushes it to a server we don't own and can't fix: a merchant's endpoint that may be slow, dead for six hours, or an impostor's. A webhook is an HTTP callback — when order.shipped happens we POST the event to a URL the customer registered, so they learn about it without polling. That reversal (we call them) changes every rule. Take our platform: Lesson 33's outbox emits 5,000 events/s, ~2 subscriptions each → 10,000 outbound POSTs/s to 50,000 endpoints we neither control nor trust. Estimate why delivering inline hands your uptime away: a dead endpoint hangs to a 30 s timeout, so by Little's Law just 1% down ties up 100/s × 30 s = 3,000 threads that never drain — your checkout pipeline dies because a stranger let a TLS cert expire → decouple through a durable queue + a separate worker fleet (Lessons 5/9). Model the four things a stranger forces on you: at-least-once (retry till 2xx — a silent drop is invisible to them), an idempotency key (stable event_id so their dedup makes your unavoidable duplicate a no-op, Lesson 13), an HMAC signature over body+timestamp (so they can prove it's you and reject forgeries/replays, Lesson 30), and exponential backoff + jitter over 24 h (~35 patient attempts, spread so a recovering fleet isn't a thundering herd, Lessons 7/26) ending in a dead-letter queue with dashboard replay. Trace a clean delivery, a failing one retried then dead-lettered, and the ambiguous timeout (request lost? response lost? — you can't tell) that makes the idempotency key non-negotiable. First wall = one dead endpoint at 500 deliveries/s × 30 s = 15,000 in-flight slots (5× the healthy fleet) — head-of-line blocking + noisy neighbor at once → per-endpoint circuit breaker (dead endpoint → ~0 slots) + bulkhead (Lessons 7/40), plus giving up strict ordering for self-describing, versioned events. Four traps: inline delivery; no signature (or a static token); assuming exactly-once / a fresh id per retry; one shared pool with no per-endpoint isolation. Trade named throughout: delivery reliability vs the burden pushed onto the receiver.
LESSON 44
Time-Series & Metrics Storage: A Million Points a Second, Kept for Years
Lesson 17 drew the dashboards that say something is slow; this lesson builds the database under the graphs. The fleet emits metrics by the million per second, forever, and the data is just (what, when, value) triples that only ever get appended — a shape that's wonderfully compressible and treacherous in exactly one way. Estimate why "one row per reading" is a half-petabyte-a-year mistake: inline labels cost ~216 MB/s ≈ 18.7 TB/day (the same label string written a million times a second), so first split the series from the samples (store each label set once → a bare 16 B timestamp+value), then compress the samples for what they are — delta-of-delta timestamps (a regular 10-s interval → ~1 bit each) and XOR values (a slow-moving gauge → ~1.3 B each) crush 16 B to ~1.5 B/point (Facebook's Gorilla reports ~1.37), landing at 130 GB/day ≈ 47 TB/year, an ~11× cut. Model the two halves: the series live in an inverted label index (Lessons 12/16) that intersects posting lists to pick which series a query touches; the samples live in time-ordered chunks that go immutable once their window passes — which is what lets old chunks compress hard, roll up (raw 10 s → 1 min → 1 hour, ~90× per level) and get dropped whole at retention (O(1), not a billion-row delete — Lessons 24/39). Trace a sample landing (creating a series is the costly event; sampling an existing one is a couple of bytes and bits) and "p99 latency for search, last 6 h" narrowing by label index → pruning by time partition (Lesson 29) → decoding only a few MB. First wall = the counter-intuitive heart: the volume of points is bounded and cheap, but the cardinality of series is a product of label values, so one innocent user_id label over 10M users explodes 750k series into 7.5 trillion (~750 TB of index + unbounded RAM) while the sample rate still looks fine — Lesson 17's "ids on traces, never on metric labels" made physical. Four traps: a high-cardinality label; metrics in a row store (forfeits the 11× compression); one resolution/retention for everything; rolling up before you know the questions (a lossy, irreversible trade). Trade named throughout: resolution & retention vs storage cost.
LESSON 43
Gossip & Anti-Entropy: Agreeing Without a Coordinator
Every "who's alive?" answer so far leaned on a center — Lesson 34's registry, Lesson 10's elected leader. This lesson removes the center entirely: can 1,000 peer nodes agree on who is alive and what the data is with no coordinator? The answer is gossip — nodes spread news like a rumor through a crowd. Estimate why the obvious "everyone pings everyone" melts: 1,000 × 999 ≈ 1,000,000 messages/second, quadratic, and a central registry or consensus just rebuilds the SPOF you're removing. Gossip has each node contact only 3 random peers/round (3,000 msgs, ~333× less, linear) — yet a fact reaches all 1,000 in ~5 rounds (log₄1000), exponential reach for linear cost. Model the two jobs by shape: membership uses SWIM — O(1) failure detection (ping one peer, then k indirect pings before believing a death), suspicion + incarnation refutation so a GC-paused node isn't wrongly evicted (the flap of Lessons 27/34), news piggybacked on the pings; data uses Merkle-tree anti-entropy — one 32-byte root hash proves 100 GB identical, a mismatch chased down only the divergent branch (~1 KB of hashes to locate, ~1 MB to fix) so cost scales with divergence, not dataset size (~100 million× less than a naive diff), repaired two ways: read-repair for hot keys, background sweep for cold ones. Trace a real death converging in seconds, a paused node saving itself with a higher incarnation number, and a stale replica repaired on-read and by Merkle sweep. First wall = the property baked in: gossip is eventually consistent (Lesson 11 AP), so there's always a window where nodes disagree — shrink it with faster/wider gossip, never to zero, because zero is the coordination gossip exists to avoid. Four traps: using gossip where you need consensus (a lock, a commit — Lessons 10/22/25); diffing the whole dataset instead of a Merkle tree; hard timeouts with no suspicion (flap/churn storm); relying on read-repair alone (cold keys never converge). Trade named throughout: convergence speed vs message overhead & staleness.
LESSON 42
Load Balancing: Steering 40k req/s Across a Churning Fleet
For 41 lessons we built the fleet — servers that scale out (Lesson 27), churn in and out (Lesson 34), and deploy without an outage (Lesson 31). We never asked the question at the front door: when 40,000 requests/second arrive, which of the 286 servers serves each one? The thing that decides is a load balancer, and "spread them evenly" gets deceptively hard the moment servers stop being identical. Estimate why the fairest-sounding rule breaks first: round-robin distributes request counts evenly but is blind to backend state, so one server slowed 10× (400 ms/req, real capacity 8 cores / 0.4 s = 20 req/s) still gets fed its full 140 req/s — an unbounded backlog (Lesson 28) that fails ~0.35% of all traffic, because it counts turns, not trouble. Model the two orthogonal choices: the layer (L4 forwards whole connections cheaply but pins a multiplexed HTTP/2 stream to one box; L7 reads each request, spreads them, can retry — at the cost of parsing + TLS) and the algorithm ladder — round-robin → least-connections → least-latency → power-of-two-choices (near-perfect balance from two random picks, no global scan, no herd; imbalance drops from growing like ln N/ln ln N to a near-constant ln ln N) → consistent-hash for stickiness (Lessons 2, 4), which a hot key can melt. Trace a healthy P2C request, a sick box that a load-aware algorithm heals in milliseconds and a health check ejects in seconds, and a deploy that drops zero requests because the retiring box deregisters, drains, then dies (Lessons 31, 34). First wall = the balancer itself: every request crosses it, so it's the purest single point of failure in the system (Lessons 7, 26) — and making it redundant (anycast, VIP failover, DNS) destroys the global view of load, which is exactly why coordination-free randomized algorithms win at scale. Four traps: blind algorithms in front of servers that can slow down; a health check probing a shared dependency (one DB blip ejects the whole fleet); L4 in front of multiplexed/long-lived connections; and treating the LB as infrastructure that "just works." Trade named throughout: even load distribution vs stickiness & simplicity.
LESSON 41
Graph & Relationship Systems: Traversing a Billion-Edge Graph
Every table we've built holds rows and asked about one row at a time. This lesson makes the relationships between rows the query. Take a social network: 1 billion people, ~200 friends each (200 billion edges), and one innocent question — "who are my friends' friends?", the 2-hop pool behind every friend suggestion. That question is where the row-at-a-time database falls off a cliff. Estimate why a relational JOIN explodes at depth: a self-join does d^k index seeks and re-pays a log N B-tree descent on each, so 1 hop is trivial, 2 hops is Lesson 12's N+1 (~20 ms), 3 hops is 8M seeks (~4 s), 5 hops is the whole planet — each hop multiplies work by d = 200. Model the fix: an adjacency list (a node's neighbors stored together) and index-free adjacency (a node points straight at its neighbors, so one hop is O(1) and its cost is independent of graph size) — trading the relational model's generality for one thing done superbly. Trace the 2-hop FoF three ways: a sub-millisecond in-memory pointer-chase on one machine; the same query once 3.2 TB of edges are sharded by hash(user_id) across 100 machines, where ~99% of edges are cut and every hop becomes a network round trip; and a supernode (a celebrity with 100M followers) that fans one edge out to 100M nodes and becomes a hot shard — Lesson 15 and Lesson 3 reincarnated as graph structure. First wall = the deepest in the course: you cannot cut connected data into independent shards — an edge is a dependency — so you buy the best imperfect partition: replicate the hot subgraph (TAO), partition by community not by hash, vertex-cut the supernodes, and cap depth + precompute because d^k is the answer's own size. Four traps: JOINs for deep traversal, sharding a graph like a table, trusting the average degree (it hides the 100M celebrity), and traversing arbitrarily deep. Trade named throughout: traversal speed vs the partitionability of connected data.
LESSON 40
Multi-Tenancy & Noisy Neighbors: Many Customers on One Shared System
Every lesson so far designed a system for one workload — but almost every SaaS runs one shared system for thousands of paying customers at once. That's the business model: pack many tenants onto the fleet you already built (Lesson 27) and the database you already sharded (Lesson 3), so each costs pennies. The catch is one word — shared: a shared connection pool, cache, or queue is a pot all tenants draw from, so the instant one tenant misbehaves it can drain it and starve everyone else. That's the noisy neighbor. Take a B2B SaaS: 50,000 tenants, one fleet, one Postgres cluster with a 300-connection pool. Estimate the damage: normal load fits in ~60,000 q/s of headroom, but one tenant's 200-query, 2-second-each export grabs 200 of 300 connections, dropping everyone else to 20,000 q/s below 40,000 demand — a 100% blast radius from 0.002% of the tenants, no rule broken. Model the isolation spectrum (shared table → schema → database → a whole cell per tenant, Lesson 36) and three fairness controls on the shared pot: per-tenant rate limits (Lesson 8, one bucket per tenant_id), concurrency bulkheads (Lesson 7), and fair queuing (a line per tenant, not one FIFO). Trace a normal request, the spike without isolation (the 100% outage), the same spike with a 30-connection bulkhead (270 left → 54,000 q/s → zero impact, the greed paid by the greedy), and a genuine whale routed out to its own database. First wall = a limit counts requests, not work: a tenant inside every count-based cap still sends 10 un-indexed scans at ~40 s each → fix by metering the scarce resource itself (CPU-seconds, rows, bytes) plus per-query guardrails, then shuffle-shard the shared tier (Lesson 36), route hot tenants out (Lesson 3/19), and wall the async tier and cache too — not just the front door. Four traps: counting requests not cost, one global limit instead of per-tenant, forcing one isolation model on all sizes, and walling the front door while the back doors stay open. Trade named throughout: density & cost efficiency vs isolation & fairness.
LESSON 39
Tiered Storage & Data Lifecycle: When the Log Outgrows Fast Disk
Three lessons in a row handed us a pile of bytes that only ever grows — Lesson 20's 100 PB object store, Lesson 29's re-scanned warehouse, and Lesson 38's event log that grows ~130 GB/day and, being immutable, never shrinks. Each ended on the same worry: we can't keep all of it on fast disk forever. The answer rests on one fact from Lesson 2: reads are wildly skewed toward new data — yesterday's event is read constantly, a three-year-old one a thousand times less — yet you pay the same fast-SSD price for both. So split storage into tiers (RAM → SSD → HDD → object store → glacier) that get ~5× cheaper and ~10× slower each step down, and let each byte fall downhill as it cools, governed by a lifecycle policy. Take Lesson 38's event log (5,000 events/s × 300 B), kept 7 years for compliance. Estimate the bill: ~330 TB all on hot SSD is ~$33,000/month, but 90% of reads touch the last 7 days (~1 TB, 0.3% of the data), so slicing by age across HOT→WARM→COLD→ARCHIVE drops it to ~$744/month — a ~44× cut, with the biggest slice of bytes being the smallest slice of the bill. Model the tier ladder, the temperature that picks a record's rung (age is only a proxy), and the background policy that demotes as data cools and expires at 7 years — keeping tiering (a cost move, still retrievable) strictly separate from expiration (a compliance move, gone forever), and the small index hot (Lesson 20) while only payloads cool. Trace a write (always lands hot), a hot read (~2 ms, the 90%), a cold/archive read (issue a restore job, wait minutes), and the throttled sweep (copy-new-then-free-old, delete at the end). First wall = the retrieval-latency cliff: archive reads take minutes, not milliseconds — fixed not by flattening the ladder (that forfeits the 44×) but by tiering to each slice's retrieval SLA and temperature, keeping the index hot, and packing small objects. Four traps: tiering by age when age isn't the temperature, confusing tiering with expiration, putting the index on a cold tier, and the small-object retrieval tax. Trade named throughout: storage cost vs retrieval latency & operational policy.
LESSON 38
Event Sourcing: When the Log of Changes IS the Truth
Two lessons already flirted with this and stopped short. Lesson 25 rejected a mutable balance column, stored an append-only stream of ledger entries, and derived the balance as their SUM. Lesson 33 committed a change to the write model and an event to an outbox in one transaction. In both, the current state was the truth and the events were a side effect. Event sourcing inverts that: store the stream of events as the one source of truth, and treat current state as the side effect — something you compute by folding the events, exactly as Lesson 25 folded entries into a balance, but for every aggregate. The outbox becomes the database. Take the digital wallet (Lesson 25), re-built event-sourced. Estimate two storage walls: a log that grows ~130 GB/day ≈ 47 TB/year and never shrinks (vs a state DB's flat ~100 GB), and a fold whose cost grows with an account's history — ~20 s to load Lesson 25's 100M-event hot account. Model four parts: immutable past-tense events (corrected by appending, never editing), the aggregate as fold-and-invariant boundary, the fold (state = events.reduce(apply, empty), walked $0→$100), and the guarded append that enforces a rule with Lesson 6's compare-and-set instead of a lock. Trace a command (fold-to-decide, append-if-version-matches — no lock, no double-spend), a query (served from a projected read model, because you can't SELECT over a log — which is why event sourcing needs CQRS), and a replay (any new view, or "the balance last Tuesday," free because every fact was kept). First wall = folding a long-lived aggregate on every command → snapshot (Lesson 25's snapshot generalized to "a cached fold," a ~100,000× cut, safe because it's derived and rebuildable). Then: project to answer queries (CQRS), version-and-upcast events because their schema is forever, and accept an unbounded log as the price of total history. Four traps: mutating/deleting an event, folding the whole log per read, a too-big aggregate (Lesson 6/22 contention), and events coupled to current code. Trade named throughout: perfect auditability & replayability vs query complexity and storage.
LESSON 37
Read/Write Splitting & CQRS: Two Shapes of the Same Data
Half the course already built a second copy of the data beside the first without naming the pattern: Lesson 2's cache, Lesson 15's precomputed feed, Lesson 16's search index, Lesson 29's warehouse, Lesson 33's outbox/CDC stream feeding them all. Each was the same admission — the shape that's good for writing the data isn't the shape that's good for reading it. CQRS makes it a design: keep one write model optimized for correct, consistent writes (Lessons 6/25), and build as many read models as you have read shapes, each fed from the write model's change stream. Take the orders service (5,000 writes/s, 50,000 reads/s) and watch one normalized table fail four reads at once. Estimate why it's shape, not just volume: the detail page is a 6-way join ≈ ~2.4 ms = 48 cores at 20k/s, but a denormalized read model does it in one 0.4 ms lookup = 8 cores (6×) — yet the very denormalization that makes the read cheap is what makes a write dangerous, so no one table wins both (search wants an inverted index, dashboards a columnar store, status a cache). Model the three parts: a write model that owns the truth (normalized, ACID), read models each shaped for one query (document/search/columnar/cache), and the projection that connects them by consuming Lesson 33's durable stream idempotently (Lesson 13) — plus why a plain read replica scales the same shape where CQRS changes the shape. Trace a command (commit order + outbox event in ONE transaction, ack, project async) and four queries (each on its own store, unable to starve each other). First wall = the eventual-consistency gap: the write is acked before any read model reflects it, so read-your-writes looks like data loss until you special-case it — and that same gap marks where CQRS becomes accidental complexity (if reads share the write's shape and fit one DB, you bought two models and a pipeline for nothing). Climb the ladder — one model → replica (volume) → materialized view (one shape) → full CQRS (divergent shapes) — only as far as the reads force you. Four traps: dual-writing instead of projecting (Lesson 33's trap), a non-idempotent projection, trusting a read model as the truth, and hiding the gap from the UX. Trade named throughout: read/write optimization vs the consistency gap between the two sides.
LESSON 36
Cell-Based Architecture: Containing a Failure to 1/N of Your Users
Almost every failure lesson pointed at the same escape hatch without naming it: Lesson 7's bulkhead, Lesson 28's bounded queues, Lesson 31's canary — all the instinct to contain the damage, applied one layer at a time. This lesson makes it structural and total. Take Lesson 27's fleet (286 servers, 40,000 req/s) serving 8,000,000 tenants as one shared stack, and a single poison query, bad deploy, or hot tenant is everyone's problem — 8M × 5 min = 40,000,000 tenant-minutes, a 100% blast radius, for every such incident. Cut the whole stack (LB + app + data) into N independent cells that no request may leave, send each tenant to one, and the same failure drops to 1/N: eight cells turn it into 12.5% — 8× less damage, because 7/8 of the fleet is physically unreachable from the fault. Model the one hard rule (no cross-cell calls, or you've rebuilt the monolith) and the one part you can't cell — the thin router that maps tenant → cell, which must stay dumb + HA because it's the only shared fate (a router bug is the one bug with 100% blast radius). Size cells against an overhead floor (quorum + headroom): near the floor, halving the blast radius roughly doubles the fixed cost — 64 cells would need 64×8 = 512 nodes, ~2× the fleet. Trace a bad deploy (the cell IS Lesson 31's canary unit) and a hot tenant (cells cap the rest of the system, but the 1,000,000 cell-mates still share its fate — the wall). The fix: shuffle sharding — give each tenant a random subset of cells, and a noisy neighbour fully knocks out another only if their whole subsets coincide: C(8,2)=28 shrinks the co-victim group from 12.5% to 3.6%, and C(100,5)≈75,000,000 to 1-in-75-million. Four traps: a hidden shared component (DB/cache/config) re-coupling the cells, a fat/fragile router, cells sized wrong, and a global operation that skips the cell boundary. Trade named throughout: blast-radius isolation vs cross-cell coordination & overhead.
LESSON 35
Logical Clocks & Causal Ordering: Putting Events in Order When No Clock Can Be Trusted
Three lessons quietly borrowed a clock they never earned — Lesson 22's lease expired "when the clock passed the TTL", Lesson 23's last-write-wins picked "whose timestamp is bigger", Lesson 34's registry evicted "when the clock said the lease ran out" — but each clock lives on a different machine, and machines disagree about the time. Meet the bug head-on with one painfully common symptom: a group chat where a reply prints ABOVE the question it answers. Nobody's code is wrong; Frankfurt's server clock just runs 50 ms behind Virginia's, so Bob's reply (sent 20 ms after he read Alice's question) gets a wall stamp 30 ms BEFORE the question — and every client sorting by timestamp inverts them. Estimate the two sides of the one inequality that causes it: clocks disagree by tens of ms (drift ~4.3 s/day unchecked; NTP pins only to ~ms and can step backward), while causally linked events are microseconds to milliseconds apart — so skew > gap, and the inversion is routine, not rare. The fix is to stop counting seconds and start counting causality: define happened-before from information flow alone (same-process order, send-before-receive, transitivity), then stamp it. Lamport clocks (one counter, max+1 on receive) give an O(1) total order that never sorts an effect before its cause — but the arrow runs one way, so they can't detect concurrency. Vector clocks (one counter per process, elementwise-max) can tell concurrent from causal — at O(N) size (Lesson 23's version vectors). Trace the reply (a real cause both clocks order right) and Carol's unrelated message (truly concurrent — only the vector clock refuses to invent an order, which is exactly what a conflict resolver needs). First wall: O(N) is fine when N is servers and fatal when N is users — 1M × 8 B = 8 MB of metadata to order one keystroke, and it never shrinks → hybrid logical clocks (O(1), causal, AND close to real wall-clock time — the honest repair for the clocks L22/23/34 trusted). Four traps: sorting by wall clock across machines, reading a Lamport/HLC order as causality, per-client vectors at scale, and thinking a logical clock removes the need for real time. Trade named throughout: ordering precision vs metadata size & coordination.
LESSON 34
Service Discovery & Health Checking: Finding a Live Instance in a Fleet That Comes and Goes Every Minute
Every lesson so far assumed a step it never justified: a caller "calls the search service." But Lesson 27's fleet autoscales and Lesson 31 rolls deploys through it, so the search service isn't at an address — it's at 200 addresses that change every few seconds. Which IP does the order service dial, and how does it avoid one that died two seconds ago? Price the naive config file: a rolling deploy alone changes an address ~once every 6 s, and one crash inside a 60 s refresh window black-holes 200 req/s × 60 s = 12,000 requests before the caller notices — stale in both directions (dead addresses linger, fresh capacity stays invisible). The fix is to stop treating membership as config and make it live self-maintaining state: a registry where each instance registers on boot and heartbeats every 3 s to renew a 9 s TTL lease (Lessons 10/22/26), so a crash is just an entry that expires — death detected in ≤3 missed beats, no reply needed. Plus the one distinction that prevents an outage: liveness ("is it alive?" → restart) vs readiness ("should it get traffic now?" → remove from pool, don't restart — the drain off-ramp, Lesson 31). Choose who does the lookup — DNS (cheap, stale), client-side (fresh, code everywhere), or a sidecar mesh (fresh + uniform, operational weight). Trace a boot (register only when READY, so capacity appears when it can serve), a crash (TTL expiry bounds the black hole, a Lesson 7 retry papers over the gap), and a graceful shutdown (deregister first, then drain — the ordering a crash gets wrong). First wall: the registry is now everyone's single dependency — it must fail-static (cache the last good list, choose AP — Lesson 11 — opposite of Lesson 10's CP election), run as a replicated consensus cluster (etcd/Consul/ZK), and shed heartbeat load that scales with the whole platform. Four traps: conflating liveness/readiness, a health check that probes a shared DB (one blip ejects the whole fleet), a strongly-consistent registry on the hot path, and a mistuned TTL that flaps. Trade named throughout: routing freshness vs lookup cost & staleness.
LESSON 33
Change Data Capture & the Outbox: Getting Data Out of One Database Into Everyone Else's Without the Dual-Write Lie
Lesson 32 ended on a poisonous "and": each saga step had to "commit locally and emit an event." Committing to your DB is one system; publishing to a broker is another — and there's no shared transaction across two systems. So "write the order row and publish OrderPlaced" is a dual write, and it lies in either order: write-then-publish → a crash leaves a real order nobody was told about (not searchable, not in the warehouse, no confirmation); publish-then-write → a crash announces an order that never saved. There's no safe order — the gap is the bug (Lesson 32's wall, in miniature, under every "when an order is placed, update search/cache/warehouse/notify"). Price it: at 5,000 writes/s even a 99.9% publish rate drips 5/s = 432,000 silently-invisible orders a day, and one 30-second broker blip orphans 150,000 at once — and no retry closes the crash-in-the-gap window. The fix is to stop having two writes: the transactional outbox inserts an event row in the same transaction as the order (Lessons 13/25), so the announcement is a byproduct of the one commit, shipped out-of-band by an at-least-once relay — a crash can only make the event late, never lost. Or change data capture skips the table and tails the DB's own commit log (WAL/binlog), emitting every committed change with no app code and the lowest lag (raw row events vs the outbox's clean domain events — combine them by CDC-tailing the outbox). Trace a clean run, a crash right after the commit (outbox row survives → only late), and a relay that republishes (at-least-once → duplicate → made safe by idempotent consumers keyed on event_id, L13). First wall: everything now flows through one relay — its lag is every downstream system's staleness (L09), it grows the outbox 86 GB/day unless compacted (L20/L24), and ordering at scale needs partition-by-key (L09). Trade named throughout: freshness/consistency vs pipeline complexity.
LESSON 32
Distributed Transactions & Sagas: Making Many Services Commit All-or-Nothing Without a Shared Transaction
Lesson 31 shipped one service safely; now one user action spans four — "book my trip" = reserve flight + hotel + car + charge the card, all-or-nothing. But each lives in its own database, and a transaction stops at the edge of its own database — so the atomicity you got free inside one box you now must build. Price the obvious "hold a lock across all four" (that's two-phase commit): every contended row is held 50/5 = 10× longer than a local commit (a hot flight drops ~200 → ~20 bookings/sec), the commit is only as available as the product of its participants (0.999⁴ ≈ 99.6% ≈ 35 h/yr unbookable), and a coordinator crash between the two phases leaves participants in-doubt — frozen holding locks (Lesson 10's coordinator, darker). The saga is the AP answer: commit each step locally and immediately (no cross-service lock ever held), and on failure walk backward running compensating transactions — ordered so everything before the pivot (the charge) is undoable and everything after is retriable, driven by a central orchestrator or by choreographed events (L09). Trace a clean booking, a sold-out car (compensate the holds, no charge made), and a declined card (why the charge goes last), and the wall appears: a saga has no isolation — its half-done state is really visible, so a second saga can dirty-read a soon-to-be-cancelled hold or double-book the last seat → fixed not by re-adding the lock but by a semantic lock (HELD, not SOLD) + idempotent, retriable compensations (L13). Trade named throughout: atomicity vs availability across service boundaries.
LESSON 31
Deployments & Progressive Rollout: Shipping New Code to a Live Fleet Without Making Every User Your Test Subject
Thirty lessons built a system; now we have to change it — push a new version to Lesson 27's live fleet (286 servers, 40,000 req/s) while users are mid-request. The simplest move, a big-bang deploy, is the release-form of the all-at-once blast radius Lesson 30 just shrank: a bug meets 40,000 × 300 s = 12,000,000 requests — 100% of users — before a ~5-min rollback lands. Ship the same bug as a 1% canary and it meets ~24,000 (a 500× cut) and never reaches the rest. Model the space as two dials — shrink the exposed slice or shrink the time to undo: rolling (cheap, gradual, slow rollback, capacity dip), blue-green (instant flip-back, 2× transient fleet, all-at-once at the flip), canary + health-gated promotion (smallest blast radius, at the cost of metrics + bake time), and the orthogonal feature flag that decouples deploy (code running) from release (behavior reaching users) so rollback is a millisecond flag flip. Trace a healthy ramp (1% → 5% → 25% → 100%, drain before retiring v1) against a poisoned one (spike at 1% → gate fires → auto-rollback, 99% untouched), and the wall appears: old and new run simultaneously over one shared database the load balancer's fast flip can't reverse → so rollback is only as fast as your slowest-to-reverse layer, and safety demands backward/forward compatibility (expand → deploy → bake → contract, L24). Four traps: coupling the migration to the deploy, a thin canary that proves nothing, dropping in-flight work instead of draining (L26), and feature flags left to rot into test debt. Trade named throughout: release velocity vs blast radius.
LESSON 30
Secrets, Keys & Encryption: Protecting the Data Without the Key Becoming the Single Point of Failure
Lesson 29's warehouse concentrated three years of payments and every customer record into one ~11 TB vault — a gift to an attacker. The obvious defense, "encrypt it," hides two traps. Encrypt all 11 TB under one key and a single leak exposes 100% of the vault, while rotating that key means moving 22 TB → ~12 hours of churn. The fix is envelope encryption: bulk data under cheap per-file DEKs (AES-256-GCM, so confidentiality and tamper-detection), each DEK wrapped by a master KEK that lives only inside a KMS and never touches a host. Now a leaked DEK costs one file (1/100,000), and rotating the KEK re-wraps just ~10 MB of DEKs while the terabytes never move (~2,000,000× less data touched). The KMS is Lesson 10's coordination point in a security hat — powerful, so it must be HA, access-controlled, audited — and TLS/mTLS guards the wire (terminate it everywhere, not just the edge). Trace a write (generate → encrypt → wrap → store the envelope → wipe the DEK) and a read (fetch → unwrap → decrypt-or-refuse-on-tamper), and the wall appears: the unwrap is a KMS round trip on every read (40,000/s = a hot, rate-limited SPOF) → fixed by caching the unwrapped DEK for a bounded TTL (a ~6,000× cut), where the TTL is a dial between KMS load and how long a stolen or un-revoked DEK stays live. Four traps: reusing a GCM nonce, encrypting without authenticating, secrets in code/config/logs (down to secret-zero), and terminating TLS too early. Trade named throughout: security blast-radius vs operational friction.
LESSON 29
Data Warehousing & OLAP: Asking One Big Question of the Whole Firehose
For 28 lessons we protected the serving path. Now the opposite question: not "what is order #12345?" but "revenue by region by day for last quarter — across all 11 billion orders (~11 TB)?" Run it on the row-oriented serving DB and it drags whole 1 KB rows to read 2 of 50 columns → 900 GB → ~30 minutes, while also starving live customers (L07/L27). Store the same data by column instead and that query reads only the 2 columns it names, prunes to 90 of 1,095 day-partitions (11 TB → 900 GB), projects (→ 9 GB), and compresses (→ ~2.5 GB) → a 5 s scan, ~0.5 s across 10 nodes — a 360× cut bought purely by reading fewer bytes, not a faster disk. Model the system: a separate columnar warehouse (analytics and serving have opposite shapes and failure modes — never share one DB), partitioned for pruning and denormalized into a star schema (precompute the join, L15 — normalize for writes, denormalize for reads), fed by an ETL/ELT pipeline that makes it a copy, and so stale. Trace the load (an order is queryable in ms but enters analytics only at the next batch) and the scan (prune → project → scan → merge, L21's partial-then-merge). First wall: the warehouse is stale, and "batch more often" loses (cost scales with history × frequency) → the lambda (two layers, duplicated logic) vs kappa (one replayable stream) reconciliation — guarded by three traps: point lookups on a scan engine (L12's selectivity trap reversed), over-partitioning into a small-file swamp (L20), and late events needing a watermark (L17). Trade named throughout: query freshness vs cost & complexity.
LESSON 28
Backpressure & Flow Control: Saying No Gracefully When Scaling Can't Keep Up
Lesson 27 proved a 2× spike outruns the 3-minute boot time, so for those ~3 minutes the fleet is offered 80,000 req/s but can finish only 40,000 — something has to give. The tempting answer, "just queue the overflow," is exactly what turns a slowdown into a total outage: an unbounded queue grows at 40,000/s and hits ~144 GB in 3 minutes (40,000/s × 180 s × 20 KB) → OOM crash, and even before that goodput collapses to ~0 while throughput stays a deceptive 40,000/s — the server pegged, finishing requests whose clients already timed out. The fix is one idea: bound every queue, sized from the client timeout (depth < timeout × rate = 40,000 → pick 20,000 → max wait 0.5 s, memory a fixed 400 MB, goodput back to full). Then choose how a full queue says no — block the producer (credit-based flow control: TCP window, HTTP/2, request(n)) when you control it, or drop (an honest 429, L07) when you don't. Trace three responses and watch the wasted work: unbounded (crash + retry-storm metastability), bounded-but-shed-deep (survives, but every reject already burned edge + API-tier CPU — ~200 stolen cores), and bounded-plus-edge admission control (downstream fullness travels upstream as a credit signal → 429 the excess for near-free → goodput preserved at L27's knee). First wall: where the "no" goes, guarded by three traps — an unbounded queue hiding at any hop, blocking a shared thread pool (L07 freeze), and blind shedding (drop retries/low-tier/stale head first — FIFO→LIFO — never health checks). Trade named throughout: throughput vs tail latency & stability.
LESSON 27
Autoscaling & Capacity Planning: Sizing a Fleet Before the Spike Hits
Lesson 26 leaned on a word without earning it — headroom. Now earn it for the API tier behind those gateways: 40,000 req/s at peak, 40 ms of CPU each, 8 cores per server. Size the fleet two ways that must agree: Little's Law (L13) fixes the floor — 40,000 × 0.04 s = 1,600 busy cores = 200 servers at an impossible 100% — and the utilization knee (T = S/(1−ρ): a 40 ms request is 133 ms at 70% but 4,000 ms at 99%) sets the real target at 286 servers, the extra 43% (~$377k/yr) buying nothing but distance from the cliff. Model the autoscaler as a feedback loop (measure → compare to 70% → desired = load ÷ (C × ρ*) → act → cool down), scale-out fast / scale-in slow so it doesn't flap (L10). Trace three loads and watch the one thing that decides everything — does load rise slower or faster than a server boots: a gentle morning ramp reactive scaling handles for free; a sudden 2× spike it cannot catch (fleet at 140% for ~3 min, ~4.1M requests backed up); a known 8:30 surge you beat only by pre-scaling ahead. Then the wall: the ~3-minute cold-start lag — a control loop's dead time, minutes against seconds — fought not by deleting boot time but by shrinking the gap: spike-buffer headroom, warm pools, predictive pre-scaling, faster warm-up (and scale-to-zero as the opposite extreme). Trade named throughout: cost vs headroom for spikes.
LESSON 26
Real-Time Delivery: Telling 10 Million Phones "You Got Paid" the Instant It Happens
Lesson 25 settled the money; now tell Bob's phone the instant his balance moves — in ~100 ms, without him asking. LESSON 25
Payments & Ledgers: Moving $50 So It Can Never Be Lost, Doubled, or Un-Audited
Lesson 24 made the orders table safe to reshape. Now make the money in it trustworthy: Alice pays Bob $50, and forever after the $50 must never vanish or double, a retry must move it once, and an auditor must prove every balance to the penny. The tempting UPDATE balance = balance + 50 fails all three — it races (L06's lost update), keeps no history to audit, and one bad write silently corrupts the number with no way to recompute it. The fix is the oldest idea in accounting: the double-entry ledger, where money is never edited, only appended as paired immutable entries that sum to zero — so a balance is a SUM you derive, not a number you store, and the whole ledger always totals zero (money is conserved via a bank system account). Trace a clean transfer (two entries in one atomic commit, or $50 vanishes), a duplicate retry stopped cold by a unique idempotency key before any entry is written (L13's exactly-once effect, now moving dollars), and a refund that appends a reversal instead of deleting — which is what keeps the books auditable. Then the wall: a hot merchant's balance = SUM of 100M entries takes ~3.2 s → snapshot the running balance (100,000× cut) and shard the account (L21), both kept as derived accelerators over an untouched ledger. Trade named throughout: correctness & auditability vs throughput.

Distributed Locks & Leases: Two Workers, One Billing Job, and the Fencing Token
Lesson 21 left a hazard: two workers both believe they hold the lock on the nightly billing job, and both charge the customer $50. Trace it end to end. A lock is a claim to be the only writer, but a process can freeze — a stop-the-world GC pause, a paused VM — for longer than its lease lives: at t=6s worker A freezes, at t=15s its 15s lease expires, at t=16s worker B acquires and charges, and at t=28s A wakes still believing it holds the lock and charges again. Size the lease TTL (heartbeat 5s → TTL 15s → ≤15s failover) and watch a too-short TTL flap a live-but-slow holder. The lease alone can't save you — it caused the overlap. The real fix is a fencing token: a monotonic number (41, 42) the ledger uses to reject any write from a superseded grant — A's token-41 write is fenced out at the resource, no clock needed. Then the wall: one shared lock serializes to 200 ops/s (13.9 h for 10M charges), is a SPOF, and leans on clocks — which is why a distributed lock is a last resort, behind idempotency and single-writer routing. Trade named throughout: safety vs liveness.
