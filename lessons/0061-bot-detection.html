Bot Detection & Traffic Authenticity: Telling Humans from Scripts at the Edge

Lesson 60 delivered the page to the screen as fast and cheap as the shared/personal split allows — and quietly assumed a real human on the other end. That assumption is often wrong. On any popular site a large fraction of requests come from automated clients — some you welcome (search crawlers), many you don't (scrapers, credential-stuffers, scalpers) — and serving, rendering, and rate-limiting them as if they were people wastes compute (Lesson 59) and enables abuse (Lesson 52). This lesson takes one concrete front door — the orders platform at 40,000 req/s (Lessons 27/49/55/60), plus its login endpoint — and asks the question the edge must answer before it renders or rate-limits anything: is this a person or a program? We'll estimate how much of the traffic is automated and what it costs, and watch a distributed attack hide from the obvious defense. We'll model detection as a funnel of increasingly expensive checks — passive signals, active challenges, and the choice between rate-based and behavioral detection — every one of which has two ways to be wrong. We'll trace a loud scraper, a low-and-slow credential-stuffing botnet, and the graduated response that avoids blocking real customers. The first bottleneck decides the whole design: you cannot separate bots from humans perfectly — every signal is forgeable and the two error types trade off — so the real goal is not detection but making abuse cost more than it's worth, without taxing the humans. The trade underneath: abuse reduction vs friction & false positives.

The one problem we'll design

Two hundred thousand people an hour browse our product pages and, now and then, log in. The front door serves 40,000 req/s at peak (Lesson 27's fleet, behind Lesson 49's gateway and Lesson 55's edge). But not every request is a person. Some are search-engine crawlers we want (they bring shoppers). Some are scrapers copying our whole catalog and prices for a competitor. Some are credential-stuffers trying millions of stolen username/password pairs against the login endpoint, hoping a few still work. Some are scalpers racing to buy every unit of a limited drop the instant it goes live. The naive instinct is "just block the bad ones." But a request arrives as a bare HTTP call — it does not come stamped human or robot — and a determined program can be made to look almost exactly like a browser. Deciding who to serve, who to challenge, and who to turn away, in a few milliseconds, without turning away real customers, is a system-design problem — and the two ways of getting it wrong cost very different amounts.

Bot detection is not a yes/no gate you put in front of the site. It is a funnel: cheap passive signals score every request, a graduated set of active challenges taxes the suspicious ones, and only the confidently-bad are blocked. The design question is where to draw the lines — because every line you draw both lets some bots through (a false negative) and stops some humans (a false positive), and those two mistakes do not cost the same.

Words we'll earn before we lean on them

  • Bot — any client driven by a program, not a person typing in a browser. Ranges from a one-line script to a real browser automated by code.
  • Good bot vs bad bot — automation you welcome (search crawlers, uptime monitors, link-preview fetchers) versus automation that harms you (scrapers, credential-stuffers, scalpers, spam). The same techniques serve both; intent is what differs.
  • Scraping — a bot systematically copying your content (catalog, prices, reviews) at machine speed, often for a competitor or a reseller.
  • Credential stuffing — trying a large list of stolen username/password pairs (leaked from some other breached site) against your login, betting that people reuse passwords.
  • Scalping / inventory hoarding — bots buying up limited stock (sneakers, tickets, GPUs) the instant it drops, to resell.
  • Signal — anything a request reveals about itself without being asked — its IP, its request rate, the exact shape of its headers and TLS handshake, how it moves through the site.
  • Fingerprint — a signature built from those low-level details. A TLS fingerprint (JA3) hashes the exact order of cipher-suites and extensions in the connection's opening message; real browsers and script libraries produce different ones.
  • Challenge — an active test that costs the client something to pass: a CAPTCHA (a puzzle easy for humans, hard for programs), a JavaScript challenge (can the client run JS at all?), or a proof-of-work (make the client burn measurable CPU).
  • Proof-of-work (PoW) — force the client to compute a small but non-trivial puzzle (find a number whose hash starts with N zero bits) before it's served. Cheap once, expensive a million times.
  • Rate-based detection — flag a client that makes too many requests per unit time (Lesson 08's rate limiter, used as a bot signal).
  • Behavioral detection — flag a client whose pattern (navigation, timing, aggregate failure rate) doesn't look like a human's, even at a low request rate.
  • False positive / false negative — a false positive blocks a real human (a lost customer); a false negative lets a bot through (wasted compute or abuse). You cannot drive both to zero.
  • Graduated response — instead of block-or-allow, choose an action proportional to suspicion: serve, challenge, or block. The middle rung is what keeps friction off real users.

Move 1 — Estimate: how much traffic isn't human, and what it costs

Before designing a defense, size the problem. Two numbers matter: how much of the load is automated, and what the two kinds of mistake cost.

PieceValueWhere it comes from
Peak front-door traffic40,000 req/sLesson 27's fleet

Leaked credential pairs (one attack)10,000,000a dump from someone else's breach
Attacker's proxy IPs (botnet)10,000rented residential / mobile proxies

What bots cost you just by being served. If 40% of 40k req/s are bots, that's 16,000 bot requests every second hitting the same render path as real users. Priced the Lesson 59/60 way: 0.030 s × 16,000 = 480 core-seconds every second = 480 CPU cores ≈ 60 servers doing nothing but rendering pages for programs. Before a single account is stolen or a single price is scraped, automated traffic is already a ~60-server line item on the bill (Lesson 59) — the cheapest bot to handle is the one you never render for.

Why the obvious defense fails — the low-and-slow attack. A credential-stuffer has 10M stolen pairs to test against our login. The loud way — one machine firing 2,000 login attempts/s — is trivially caught by a per-IP rate limit (Lesson 08). So the attacker spreads the work across a botnet of 10,000 rented IPs, each making just 1 attempt per minute. Per IP that is 1/60 ≈ 0.017 req/s — slower than a real person who mistypes a password twice. A per-IP limit of even 30/min never fires. Yet the aggregate is 10,000 ÷ 60 ≈ 167 attempts/s, grinding through all 10M pairs in 10,000,000 ÷ 167 ≈ 59,880 s ≈ 16.6 hours. Each IP looks perfectly human; the population is an attack. Rate-limiting the thing you can see per-client is blind to an attack that is only visible when you add up all the clients.

The asymmetry that governs everything — the two mistakes cost differently. Suppose we tune a detector aggressive enough to block 95% of those 16,000 bot req/s (15,200 stopped — good), but at that sensitivity it also wrongly blocks 0.2% of humans: 24,000 × 0.002 = 48 real people per second hitting a wall, ≈ 4.1 million wrongly-blocked human requests per day. And here's the trap: a blocked bot is silent and a blocked human is invisible too — they don't appear in your success metrics, they just leave. A false negative costs a little compute; a false positive costs a customer you never knew you lost. That asymmetry — cheap, measurable mistakes on one side; expensive, unmeasured mistakes on the other — is why you cannot simply "turn the detector up to maximum."

Name the trade-off — abuse reduction vs friction & false positives. Every knob in this lesson moves along one dial. Tighten detection (block more, challenge more) and you stop more abuse — paid for in friction on real users and in humans wrongly turned away. Loosen it and the humans sail through — paid for in wasted compute and successful attacks. There is no setting that gives you both, because a program can be built to look arbitrarily human, so the honest goal is not "perfect separation" but "make abuse cost the attacker more than it earns them, while keeping the tax on real users near zero."

Move 2 — Model: a funnel of signals, challenges, and two detection styles

Model the defense as three layers, each more expensive than the last, applied to fewer requests as the cheap layers filter the obvious cases out. This is the same "cheap filter first, expensive check only on survivors" funnel as the search re-ranker (Lesson 16) and the CDN's shield (Lesson 14).

Layer 1 — passive signals (free, ask nothing)

A request reveals a lot without being asked. IP reputation: is it from a residential ISP (probably a person) or a datacenter/known-proxy range (probably a program)? Rate per client: Lesson 08's counter, now read as a bot signal. Fingerprint: the exact order of HTTP headers, the User-Agent, and especially the TLS handshake (JA3) — a real Chrome offers its cipher-suites and extensions in a characteristic order; a generic HTTP library offers a different one, and a User-Agent claiming "Chrome" over a non-Chrome TLS fingerprint is a lie you can catch. These cost nothing (they're already in the request) and filter the lazy majority of bots — but every one of them is forgeable by an attacker who bothers to mimic a real browser.

Layer 2 — active challenges (raise the cost of faking)

When signals are ambiguous, make the client prove something. A JavaScript challenge asks the client to run a snippet and return the answer — a bare HTTP script that can't execute JS fails instantly, and even one that can pays a round trip. A CAPTCHA asks for something still hard to automate (though machine vision keeps eroding this). A proof-of-work makes the client find a nonce whose hash starts with N zero bits — pure CPU, no human needed, but metered. A challenge doesn't identify a bot; it changes the economics. It's nearly free for a human doing one login and expensive for a program doing ten million.

Proof-of-work, with numbers. Require the client to find a nonce so hash(challenge‖nonce) starts with 20 zero bits. Expected tries = 2²⁰ ≈ 1,050,000 hashes ≈ ~0.5 s of a phone's CPU. For a real user logging in once, that half-second hides behind the network round trips — unnoticed. For the credential-stuffer testing 10M pairs: 10,000,000 × 0.5 s = 5,000,000 CPU-seconds ≈ 58 CPU-days of work to grind the list, where before it was essentially free. Proof-of-work flips the attack from "free at any scale" to "priced per attempt" — but it taxes low-power legit devices too, and a botnet running on stolen CPU has cycles to burn, so PoW raises the cost, it doesn't build a wall.

Layer 3 — the two ways to detect, and why you need both

Rate-based detection counts requests per key (IP, account, API token) and flags the ones over a threshold. It's cheap, stateless, and instantly catches the loud — the single IP at 2,000 req/s. Its blind spot is the whole low-and-slow attack above: spread thin enough, every individual key stays under the limit. Behavioral detection instead learns what normal looks like and flags deviation — not "how many requests" but "what pattern": a client that hits 500 product pages in perfect sequence with no mouse movement and identical 200 ms gaps, or an endpoint whose failed-login ratio jumps from 3% to 47%. Rate-based catches volume; behavioral catches shape. The distributed attack is invisible to the first and glaring to the second — which is exactly why a real defense runs both.

The model in one line: score every request with free passive signals; challenge the ambiguous middle to make faking expensive; and detect abuse two ways at once — by rate (catches the loud single client) and by behavior (catches the quiet distributed crowd). No single layer is trustworthy alone, because each is forgeable or blind in some way; the strength is in stacking many weak, independent checks — defense in depth — so an attacker has to defeat all of them at once.

  The trap: to stop Path B you could CAPTCHA every login. But a hard

Path A shows the easy case: a loud, lazy bot is caught free at the cheapest layer — rate plus a fingerprint that contradicts its own User-Agent — and tarpitting it wastes the attacker's time instead of ours. Path B is the case that decides the architecture: a distributed low-and-slow attack is invisible to rate-based and fingerprint checks (each request looks human), and only the population-level behavioral signal — the endpoint's failed-login ratio jumping to 47% — reveals it; the fix isn't to identify the bad IPs (you can't) but to raise the cost on the whole path with proof-of-work and account-level step-up. Path C is how you pay for all this without driving humans away: score instead of gate, and respond in proportion, so the 99% who look human sail through untouched and only the doubtful few are taxed.

Move 4 — Find the first bottleneck: perfect separation is impossible, so change the goal

Two walls shape every bot-detection design, and both push you away from "detect the bots" toward "price the abuse."

First wall — you cannot separate bots from humans perfectly, and the two errors trade off. Every signal is forgeable: an attacker willing to spend a little runs a real browser (headless Chrome with a genuine TLS fingerprint, real JS execution, even simulated mouse jitter), so no client-side signal is proof of anything. That means detection is always a probability, and wherever you set the threshold you make both mistakes at once — a false negative (a bot served: a little wasted compute) and a false positive (a human blocked: a customer lost, and lost silently — they never show up in your metrics). These costs are wildly asymmetric and one of them is unmeasured, so cranking sensitivity to "catch everything" quietly destroys the business it protects. The resolution is to stop aiming for separation and aim for economics: make each abusive attempt cost the attacker more (proof-of-work, step-up challenges, tarpits) than the attempt earns them, while keeping the cost on a real user near zero. Bots don't stop because you detected them; they stop because you made the attack unprofitable. That reframing — from a detection problem to a cost problem — is the whole craft, and the graduated response (score, then serve / challenge / block) is how you apply the cost where the suspicion is and nowhere else.

Second wall — the adversary adapts, so no fixed signal stays good. This is Lesson 52's lesson in a new setting: the moment you key your defense on one tell — a datacenter IP, a python-requests fingerprint, a missing JS engine — the attacker learns it and mimics a real browser, and that signal goes dead. So no single strong signal survives contact; the durable defense is defense in depth — many weak, independent signals combined, so the attacker has to defeat all of them simultaneously, and the cost of doing so is itself the deterrent. Crucially, mix signals the attacker can see and forge (client-side: fingerprint, JS, mouse) with signals they cannot see (server-side aggregate: this account's failure rate across all IPs, this endpoint's population behavior, this IP-range's reputation history). A bot can perfectly fake its own request; it cannot fake the pattern that only emerges when your server adds up thousands of requests it has no view into. And because the adversary keeps moving, the models must be retrained continuously — bot detection is never "done," it's a standing arms race you keep paying to stay ahead of.

So the honest design for our front door is not a bot filter but a layered, economic defense: score every request with free passive signals (rate, IP reputation, TLS/header fingerprint) and catch the lazy majority for nothing; challenge the ambiguous middle with an invisible JS or proof-of-work test first and a CAPTCHA only as a last resort, so faking gets expensive without taxing humans; detect the distributed attacks behaviorally, at the account and endpoint level, where the population reveals what no single client does; and respond in proportion so real users almost never feel it. Each choice is made knowing its price — a tighter threshold buys less abuse and more lost customers; a challenge buys deterrence and a little friction; every signal you add buys robustness and a little latency.

The deepest point: "bot detection" sounds like a wall you build once, and it is really a market you keep tilting. You will never label every request correctly, because the other side can always spend more to look human — so the winning move is not perfect identification but making abuse cost more than it pays, applied precisely to the requests that look suspicious and never to the ones that don't. And the same asymmetry runs underneath: a served bot is a cheap, visible mistake; a blocked human is an expensive, invisible one — which is why the safest default is to doubt your own detector and let the graduated middle, not the block, do most of the work. The standing tension: abuse reduction vs friction & false positives.

The four traps

  • Trap 1 — relying on per-IP rate limiting alone. It catches the loud scraper and is completely blind to a 10,000-IP botnet doing 1 req/min each. A per-client limit can't see an attack that's only visible when you sum the clients — add behavioral / aggregate detection at the account and endpoint level.
  • Trap 2 — cranking sensitivity to "catch everything." The false positive (a blocked human) is an expensive, invisible lost customer, while the false negative (a served bot) is cheap compute. Maximizing detection quietly maximizes the costlier, unmeasured mistake. Doubt the detector; let the graduated middle absorb the ambiguity.
  • Trap 3 — trusting any single client-side signal. A fingerprint, a JS check, even mouse movement can all be forged by an attacker running a real browser. No client signal is proof; combine many independent ones (defense in depth) and lean on server-side aggregate signals the attacker can't see or fake.
  • Trap 4 — CAPTCHA-ing everyone to be safe. A hard challenge is abandoned by 15–30% of real users, so a blanket challenge costs more humans than the bots ever would. Challenge only the suspicious middle, invisibly first (JS/proof-of-work), and save the CAPTCHA for last.

Where we landed

Telling humans from programs at the front door is an economics problem wearing a detection costume. Estimate found that ~40% of a 40k req/s front door can be automated — ~16k req/s, a ~60-server render bill (Lesson 59) before any abuse — and watched a credential-stuffer defeat the obvious defense by spreading 10M stolen pairs across 10,000 IPs at 1 attempt/minute each, invisible to any per-IP limit yet grinding the whole list in ~16.6 hours. Model built a funnel: free passive signals (rate, IP reputation, TLS/header fingerprint), active challenges (JS test, CAPTCHA, and proof-of-work that turns a free attack into ~58 CPU-days), and two detection styles run together — rate-based for the loud, behavioral for the quiet distributed crowd. Tracing caught a loud scraper for free (and tarpitted it), watched a low-and-slow botnet slip past rate and fingerprint checks only to be exposed by a population-level signal (a 3%→47% failed-login spike), and used a graduated response — score, then serve / challenge / block — to keep friction off the 99%. The first bottleneck reframes the whole thing: perfect separation is impossible because a program can be built to look arbitrarily human and the two error types trade off — a served bot is cheap and visible, a blocked human is expensive and invisible — so the goal isn't detection, it's making abuse cost more than it earns while taxing real users near zero; and because the adversary adapts (Lesson 52), the defense must be many weak independent signals (defense in depth), including server-side aggregates the attacker can't see.
