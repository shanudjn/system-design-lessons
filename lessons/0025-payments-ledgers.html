Payments & Ledgers: Moving $50 So It Can Never Be Lost, Doubled, or Un-Audited

Lesson 24 made the orders table safe to reshape. Now we make the money inside it trustworthy. The system is a digital wallet: Alice pays Bob $50, and afterwards three things must be true forever — the $50 left Alice and arrived at Bob (never vanished, never doubled), a retry of the same request moves the money once (Lesson 13, now load-bearing for dollars), and an auditor six months later can prove every balance to the penny. The tempting design — a balance column you UPDATE balance = balance + 50 — fails all three: it races (Lesson 06's lost update), it keeps no history to audit, and a single bad write silently corrupts the number with no way to recompute it. The fix is the oldest idea in accounting, the double-entry ledger: money is never edited, only appended as paired entries that sum to zero. We'll estimate the scale and the cost of getting it wrong, model the ledger (accounts, immutable entries, transactions), trace a transfer, a duplicate retry, and a refund, and hit the wall: computing a hot account's balance by summing millions of entries. The trade shifts from Lesson 24's migration-safety-vs-velocity to a new one: correctness & auditability vs throughput.

The one system we'll design

A digital wallet. Users hold a balance, top it up from a bank, and pay each other. We handle about 10 million transfers per day (~116/sec average, ~1,200/sec at peak). Every dollar amount is stored in integer cents — never a float, because 0.1 + 0.2 in floating point is 0.30000000000000004, and money that doesn't add up exactly is a bug you can't ship. The single most concrete operation, the one we trace all lesson, is: Alice pays Bob $50.00 — that is, 5000 cents.

The naive design keeps one row per user with a mutable balance column and, on a transfer, runs two updates: balance = balance − 5000 for Alice and balance = balance + 5000 for Bob. It looks obviously right and is quietly disastrous. So instead of editing balances, we record movements and never touch them again. Here is the whole idea in one picture.

  DOUBLE-ENTRY LEDGER  —  one transfer, two entries that sum to zero

  Alice pays Bob $50.00   (5000 cents)

  transactions (the request)        entries (append-only: never UPDATE, never DELETE)
  +----------------------+          +-----+----------+----------+
  | txn_id   T1          |          | txn | account  |  amount  |
  | idem_key ab-9f       |  ----->  | T1  | Alice    |   -5000  |
  | status   posted      |          | T1  | Bob      |   +5000  |
  +----------------------+          +-----+----------+----------+
                                                 sum of T1 = 0   <-- the invariant

  balance(account) = SUM(amount) over ALL its entries        (derived, not stored)
     balance(Alice) drops by 50      balance(Bob) rises by 50
     SUM of EVERY entry in the whole ledger = 0   (money is only moved, never made)

A balance is not a number you edit. It is the running total of an append-only list of movements. You never change money that already happened — you only add a new entry. That one rule buys you correctness, auditability, and safe retries all at once.

Words we'll earn before we lean on them

  • Ledger — the append-only record of every money movement. The source of truth; everything else (balances, reports) is derived from it.
  • Account — a bucket money can sit in: a user's wallet, a merchant, a fee account, or a system account that represents the outside world (the bank). A balance belongs to an account.
  • Entry (a.k.a. a posting) — one line: "this account changed by this signed amount." Entries are immutable — once written, never updated or deleted.
  • Transaction (ledger sense) — a group of entries that belong together and must be applied all-or-nothing. Its entries always sum to zero.
  • Debit / credit — accountants call the two sides of an entry a debit and a credit. We'll use plain signed amounts and keep only the rule that matters: the entries of one transaction sum to zero. That's double-entry in one sentence.
  • Immutable / append-only — the ledger only grows. To "change" the past you append a correcting entry, so the original stays visible for the auditor.
  • Balance — SUM(amount) over all of an account's entries. Derived, not stored as a truth of its own.
  • Idempotency key — a client-chosen id (Lesson 13) attached to a transfer so that retrying it moves money at most once.
  • Reconciliation — checking the books balance: every account's stored/snapshot balance equals the sum of its entries, and the whole ledger still sums to zero.
  • Compensating transaction — a new transaction that reverses an earlier one (a refund), instead of deleting the original.

Move 1 — Estimate: the scale, and the price of a single wrong move

Two numbers set the stage: how much the ledger grows, and how much one mistake costs. The growth is easy; the cost of error is the reason every design choice below leans toward safety.

Worked: how fast the ledger grows. Double-entry means each transfer writes 2 entries (one per side). At 10M transfers/day:

• entries/day = 10,000,000 × 2 = 20,000,000 rows.

• entries/year = 20,000,000 × 365 ≈ 7.3 billion rows.

• at ~100 bytes/entry that's 7.3 × 10⁹ × 100 B ≈ 730 GB/year. Modest for a database — the append-only ledger is cheap to keep, which is exactly why we never throw a movement away. Storage is not the problem; correctness is.

Worked: what one duplicate costs. Suppose 2% of requests get retried after a timeout (Lesson 07 — a timeout is a partial failure; the money may already have moved). If the design isn't exactly-once, every retry moves the money again:

• duplicated moves/day = 10,000,000 × 2% = 200,000.

• at $50 each = 200,000 × $50 = $10,000,000/day of money moved that shouldn't have been.

That is the same $10M/day from Lesson 13 — but here it isn't an over-charge you refund, it's wrong balances across the whole system, and once balances are wrong you don't ship a fix, you run a forensic audit. In payments, a bug isn't a bad pixel — it's money that moved wrong, and someone has to find every cent. This is why the ledger is built to make wrong moves structurally hard, not just unlikely.

Name the trade-off — correctness & auditability vs throughput. A single mutable balance column is the fastest possible design: one row, one write, done. The ledger is slower and larger — two immutable rows per move, balances computed instead of read, nothing ever edited in place. We pay that throughput and storage to buy something a fast counter can never give back: a complete, tamper-evident history where every balance can be re-derived and proven. The rest of the lesson is spending throughput to buy correctness — and then, at the wall, buying some of the throughput back.

Move 2 — Model: accounts, immutable entries, transactions that sum to zero

Three tables. Accounts (who can hold money). Transactions (one row per money-moving request, carrying the idempotency key and a status). Entries (the append-only movements; two or more per transaction, always summing to zero). Balances are not a table — they're a query over entries.

  THE THREE TABLES

  accounts                 transactions                      entries (APPEND-ONLY)
  +---------+-------+       +-----+----------+--------+       +----+---------+--------+
  | id      | kind  |       | id  | idem_key | status |       | id | txn/acct| amount |
  +---------+-------+       +-----+----------+--------+       +----+---------+--------+
  | Alice   | user  |       | T1  | ab-9f    | posted |       | e1 | T1/Alice|  -5000 |
  | Bob     | user  |       +-----+----------+--------+       | e2 | T1/Bob  |  +5000 |
  | bank    | system|         ^ UNIQUE(idem_key)             +----+---------+--------+
  | fees    | system|           = exactly-once (L13)            ^ never updated/deleted
  +---------+-------+

  RULE 1 (atomicity):  all entries of a transaction commit together, or none do.
  RULE 2 (balance):    SUM(amount) within one transaction = 0.
  => therefore SUM(amount) over the WHOLE ledger is ALWAYS 0. Money is conserved.

Where does money enter the system? It can't appear from nowhere without breaking Rule 2. So the outside world gets an account too — a system account named bank. A top-up of $100 into Alice's wallet is still a zero-sum transaction: bank −10000, Alice +10000. The bank account's (negative) balance is exactly "how much real money we owe the world," and the ledger stays a closed, self-checking system whose grand total never leaves zero. A platform fee works the same way: Alice pays Bob $50 with a $2 fee becomes three entries — Alice −5000, Bob +4800, fees +200 — still summing to zero.

Name the trade-off — derived balance vs stored balance. By making the balance a SUM over immutable entries instead of a stored number, we get a balance that is always re-derivable and impossible to silently corrupt: there is no single number a bad write can wreck, because the number doesn't exist until you add it up. The cost is that reading a balance is now work (a sum), not a lookup — and that bill comes due in Move 4 on a hot account. Truth you can rebuild vs a number you can read cheaply: we chose rebuildable truth, and we'll buy back the cheap read carefully.

Move 3 — Trace the paths

Three journeys prove the model: a clean transfer, a duplicate retry (exactly-once), and a refund (immutability). Watch the same invariant hold every time — every transaction sums to zero, and nothing already written is ever changed.

Path A — the happy transfer (and why the two entries commit as one)

  Alice pays Bob $50.  Request carries idem_key = ab-9f.

  BEGIN transaction (one atomic database commit)
    INSERT transactions (T1, idem_key=ab-9f, status=posted)   -- UNIQUE(idem_key)
    INSERT entries (T1, Alice, -5000)
    INSERT entries (T1, Bob,   +5000)
    -- optional guard: check balance(Alice) >= 5000 before commit
  COMMIT                                        <-- both entries land, or neither

  After: balance(Alice) -= 50,  balance(Bob) += 50,  sum(T1) = 0  OK

The two entries go in one database transaction (Lesson 13's "commit the effect and the record together"). This is not optional. If you wrote Alice −5000, then crashed before Bob +5000, $50 would have vanished — the ledger's total would no longer be zero and the books would never balance again. Atomicity is what makes "sum to zero" a guarantee instead of a hope. The optional balance check (don't let Alice go negative) rides inside the same commit so it can't race — it's the same single-transaction discipline, now protecting an invariant (balance ≥ 0), exactly the un-mergeable invariant from Lesson 23.

Path B — the duplicate retry (exactly-once money movement, recap L13)

  Alice's phone times out waiting for T1 and retries — SAME idem_key ab-9f.

  BEGIN
    INSERT transactions (T2, idem_key=ab-9f, ...)
      -> UNIQUE(idem_key) VIOLATION  (ab-9f already used by T1)
  ROLLBACK
    -> look up the existing transaction for ab-9f, return ITS result.
       No new entries written. Money moved exactly once.

  Balances unchanged by the retry. sum still 0.

The idempotency key is a unique constraint on the transactions table. The first request claims ab-9f; the retry's insert hits the constraint and fails before any entry is written — so the second attempt cannot move money. This is the atomic "claim the key" from Lesson 13, now the thing standing between a network blip and a doubled balance. Exactly-once delivery is impossible on a network (Lesson 09), but exactly-once effect is easy: let the database's unique index be the one place a duplicate is caught.

Path C — the refund (you don't delete money, you append its reversal)

  Bob refunds Alice's $50.  DO NOT delete or edit T1's entries.

  Append a NEW compensating transaction:
    INSERT transactions (T3, idem_key=refund-ab-9f, reverses=T1, status=posted)
    INSERT entries (T3, Bob,   -5000)
    INSERT entries (T3, Alice, +5000)                sum(T3) = 0

  History now reads:  T1  Alice->Bob $50   (still there, untouched)
                      T3  Bob->Alice $50   (the reversal)
  Net effect: zero. Audit trail: complete. Alice's balance: back to start.

Refunds, corrections, chargebacks — all of them are new transactions, never edits. Deleting T1 would make the money "un-happen," and an auditor asking "what occurred on the 3rd?" would get a lie. Appending T3 tells the truth: the payment happened and was reversed, both visible. This is why immutability isn't fussiness — it's the property that makes the ledger auditable. The general reconciliation check falls straight out of it: sum every entry in the whole ledger; if the total isn't exactly 0, money leaked and you have a bug to hunt. Because entries never change, that check is always valid and always reproducible.

Name the trade-off — append-forever vs edit-in-place. Never editing means the ledger only grows (Move 1's 730 GB/year) and a "simple" correction becomes a whole new transaction. In return, the past is fixed: no write can rewrite history, every state is reconstructable by replaying entries, and every dispute has a paper trail. Storage and a little ceremony, traded for a history you can stand behind in a courtroom.

Move 4 — Find the first bottleneck: a hot account's balance is a SUM of millions of rows

Reads are where "balance = derived" bites. For Alice it's nothing — a handful of entries. But the bank account and popular merchants sit on one side of a huge fraction of all transfers, so their entry list is enormous, and SUM over it is O(n).

Worked: the merchant whose balance takes seconds to read. A busy merchant has been on the platform three years and has 100 million entries. Reading them through an index (Lesson 12: ~16 bytes per index entry) to add them up means scanning:

• 100,000,000 × 16 B = 1.6 GB of index, at ~500 MB/s ≈ 1.6 GB ÷ 0.5 GB/s ≈ 3.2 s.

A 3.2-second balance read, on every page load, for your best customers. Worse, the bank account touches every top-up and withdrawal, so it grows fastest of all. Computing truth from scratch is correct but does not scale. The very thing that made the balance incorruptible — deriving it from all history — is what makes it slow to read.

The fix — snapshot the running balance. Periodically write a checkpoint: "as of entry #100,000,000, this account's balance is X." A balance read becomes snapshot + SUM(entries since the snapshot):

• if 1,000 entries have landed since the last checkpoint, the read sums 1,000 rows, not 100M — a 100,000× cut, back to sub-millisecond.

Two properties keep the snapshot honest, and they're the whole reason it's safe:

  • The snapshot is a cache, not a new source of truth. The entries remain the ground truth; the snapshot is a derived convenience (Lesson 12's covering index / Lesson 02's cache, applied to money). If a snapshot is ever doubted, you delete it and recompute from entries — the truth was never lost. Contrast the mutable balance column we rejected: that was the only copy, so corrupting it lost the truth. A snapshot corrupted is just a stale cache.
  • Reconciliation still works. The nightly audit sums raw entries (ignoring snapshots) and checks the ledger totals zero and each snapshot matches its recomputed sum. Snapshots make reads fast without becoming something you have to trust blindly.

Behind the slow read hides a second wall — the same one from Lessons 06, 08, and 23. The bank account isn't just slow to read; it's a single row every top-up must write to, so it serializes writes and becomes a hot row (contention, lost-update pressure). The fix is the one we keep meeting: shard the account into N sub-accounts (bank#0 … bank#7), spread writes across them, and define the real balance as the sum of the shards (Lesson 21's mergeable counter). Trade named: an exact single-row balance vs a sharded balance that scales writes but must be summed to read.

Name the core trade-off — correctness & auditability vs throughput. Everything in this lesson spent throughput to buy correctness: two immutable entries instead of one mutable update, balances derived instead of stored, nothing ever edited. Move 4 buys throughput back without giving up correctness — snapshots and sharded accounts make hot balances fast, but only as derived accelerators over an untouched append-only ledger. That's the discipline of payments systems: make truth cheap to read, but never let the fast path become the truth. The ledger is always the source; the fast numbers are always rebuildable from it.

Where we landed

We moved $50 from Alice to Bob in a way that can never lose it, double it, or hide it. Estimate sized the ledger (2 entries/transfer → ~7.3B rows/year ≈ 730 GB — cheap) and priced a single mistake (2% retries × 10M/day × $50 = $10M/day of wrongly-moved money, which in payments means a forensic audit, not a hotfix) — so the design leans everywhere toward safety. Model gave the double-entry ledger: immutable entries grouped into transactions that sum to zero, balances derived as SUM(amount) rather than stored, and the outside world represented by a bank system account so the whole ledger always totals zero — money is conserved, never created. Tracing proved three things: the two entries commit in one atomic transaction (or $50 vanishes and the books never balance again); a duplicate retry is stopped cold by a unique idempotency key before any entry is written (Lesson 13's exactly-once effect, now moving dollars); and a refund is a new compensating transaction, never a deletion — which is exactly what keeps the ledger auditable and makes "sum every entry, expect 0" a reconciliation check you can always run. The first bottleneck was the hot account whose balance is a SUM of 100M entries (~3.2 s per read) → snapshot the running balance (read snapshot + SUM(since), a 100,000× cut) and shard the account for write throughput (Lesson 21), with both kept strictly as derived accelerators over an untouched ledger. The real lesson: a balance is not a number you store and edit — it's the running total of an append-only list of movements you never change. That single rule gives you correctness, safe retries, and a provable audit trail at once; the price is throughput, which you buy back with derived snapshots that never get to be the truth. The trade is correctness & auditability vs throughput.
