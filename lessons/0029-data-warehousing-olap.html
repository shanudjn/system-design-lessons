Data Warehousing & OLAP: Asking One Big Question of the Whole Firehose

For 28 lessons we protected the serving path — accept a request, do a little work, answer in milliseconds. Every one of those systems emits a firehose of events, and eventually someone asks a completely different kind of question: not "what is order #12345?" but "what was revenue by region by day for the last quarter — across all 11 billion orders?" Run that on the production database and you get a 30-minute table scan that also starves every live customer. This lesson takes that one query and traces why it needs a different machine. We'll estimate the scan two ways — the row-store's 30 minutes vs the column-store's 5 seconds — model the two ideas that buy that 360× (columnar storage + partition pruning) and the OLTP-vs-OLAP split that keeps analytics off the serving box, trace how events flow from the live system into a warehouse, and hit the wall that defines the whole domain: the warehouse is stale, and closing that gap forces the lambda-vs-kappa (batch vs streaming) reconciliation. The trade shifts from Lesson 28's throughput-vs-stability to the one every analytics system is really deciding: query freshness vs cost & complexity.

The one system we'll design

Take the order events from earlier lessons — the ledger of Lesson 25, the orders table of Lesson 12. The business does 10 million orders/day and keeps 3 years of history: 10M × 1,095 days ≈ 11 billion rows. Each order is a wide record — ~50 columns (id, timestamp, customer, region, item, price, tax, shipping, status, …), about 1 KB each — so the whole thing is 11B × 1 KB ≈ 11 TB. Now an analyst asks one question: total revenue by region, by day, for the last quarter (90 days). That question touches just 2 columns (region, amount) over 90 of the 1,095 days. Here is the shape, and why the storage layout is the whole game.

  OLAP  —  asking ONE big aggregate question of the whole firehose

  11 billion order rows · 50 columns · ~11 TB.   Question:
  "revenue by region, by day, last quarter"  ->  touches 2 columns, 90 of 1,095 days.

  ROW STORE  (OLTP, L12/L25)                 COLUMN STORE  (OLAP)
  each row's 50 columns stored together      each COLUMN stored together
   +--------------------------------+         id: [.....................]
   | id | ts | region | amount |... |         ts: [.....................]
   | id | ts | region | amount |... |         region: [us|eu|us|ap|...]  <- read this
   | id | ts | region | amount |... |         amount: [ 50| 12| 88|...]  <- and this
   +--------------------------------+         ...48 other columns untouched
   want 2 cols -> must drag all 50            read ONLY the 2 you asked for
   off disk row by row                        (and skip 48; skip 91.8% of days)
   = ~900 GB read = ~30 min                   = ~2.5 GB read = ~5 s

  THE FUNNEL — narrow the bytes BEFORE you compute (L14/L16 again):
    11 TB  --prune to 90 of 1,095 day-partitions-->  900 GB   (12x less)
           --project 2 of 50 columns------------->      9 GB   (100x less)
           --columnar compression (~3.6x)-------->    ~2.5 GB
           --scan @ 500 MB/s across 10 nodes----->    ~0.5 s

The serving database (Lesson 12) stores each row's columns physically next to each other, because it answers "give me everything about order #12345" — one row, all its columns, one seek. An analytics query is the mirror image: it wants two columns across a billion rows. Store the table by column instead of by row and that same query reads only the two columns it asked for, leaves the other 48 on disk untouched, and compresses far better — because a column is a billion similar values, not a jumble of 50 different types. Same data, opposite physical layout, chosen for the opposite access pattern. That single flip is what "OLAP" is built on.

Words we'll earn before we lean on them

  • OLTP (Online Transaction Processing) — the serving workload of every prior lesson: many tiny reads and writes, each touching one row (place an order, read a balance), answered in milliseconds. Row-oriented, indexed by key, normalized.
  • OLAP (Online Analytical Processing) — the analytics workload: a few huge queries, each scanning and aggregating many rows over few columns ("sum revenue by region"). Throughput and scan speed matter; per-query latency of seconds is fine.
  • Row store vs column store — whether the bytes of one row sit together on disk (row store, good for "all columns of one row") or the bytes of one column sit together (column store / columnar, good for "one column of all rows").
  • Projection — reading only the columns a query needs. Trivial to do cheaply in a column store; impossible in a row store, which must read whole rows.
  • Partition — a table physically split into independent chunks by some key (here, by day). Partition pruning — the engine skipping entire partitions it can prove the query doesn't need (last quarter → read 90 day-partitions, ignore the other 1,005), reading nothing from them at all.
  • Data warehouse — a separate database built for OLAP: columnar, partitioned, denormalized, fed a copy of the operational data. Data lake — the rawer cousin: files (often columnar, like Parquet) in cheap object storage (Lesson 20), queried in place.
  • ETL / ELT — the pipeline that copies data from the serving systems into the warehouse: Extract, then Transform then Load (ETL) or Load then Transform (ELT). This is what makes the warehouse's data a copy, and therefore behind.
  • Batch layer vs speed (streaming) layer — recomputing aggregates from all history on a schedule (batch: complete and correct, but hours stale) vs updating them event-by-event as data arrives (streaming: fresh to seconds, but only recent and harder to get exactly right).
  • Lambda vs kappa architecture — run both a batch and a streaming layer and merge them (lambda), or run one streaming pipeline and reprocess history by replaying the log (kappa). The two standard answers to "I want fresh and correct."

Move 1 — Estimate: the same query, two storage layouts

Before choosing a design, price the query on the machine we already have (the row-oriented serving DB of Lesson 12) and on the one we're proposing. The disk reads only so fast — Lesson 12's 500 MB/s — so the design that reads fewer bytes wins, and the gap is enormous.

Worked (way 1): the row store drags whole rows. Even after limiting to the quarter, that's 10M/day × 90 = 900M rows. In a row store, the 2 columns we want (region, amount) are physically interleaved with the other 48, so to read them the engine must pull each entire 1 KB row off disk:

• bytes read = 900M × 1 KB = 900 GB (98% of it columns we never use).

• time = 900,000 MB ÷ 500 MB/s = 1,800 s = 30 minutes — for one dashboard tile. (And scanning the whole 11 TB table, if you couldn't prune, would be 22,000 s ≈ 6.1 hours.) A row store makes you pay for every column of every row you touch, even the ones your query never mentions.

Worked (way 2): the column store reads only what's asked, then compresses it. Store each column separately. The query reads exactly two: amount (8 bytes) and region (dictionary-encoded to ~2 bytes — there are only a handful of regions). Day is the partition key, so filtering to 90 days is free (Move 2).

• projected bytes = 900M × (8 + 2) = 9 GB — already 100× less than 900 GB, purely from not reading 48 columns.

• compression: a column is a billion similar values, so it squeezes hard — region has ~5 distinct values → dictionary + run-length encoding; amount → delta/bit-packing. Call it a conservative 3.6× → 9 GB ÷ 3.6 ≈ 2.5 GB.

• time = 2,500 MB ÷ 500 MB/s = 5 s on one disk, and because the 90 partitions are independent you can scan them on 10 nodes at once → ~0.5 s. 30 minutes to 5 seconds — a 360× cut — and not one byte of it came from a faster disk. It came from reading fewer bytes: prune the days, project the columns, compress what's left.

Name the trade-off — layout follows access pattern. A row store is optimal for OLTP (fetch or update one whole row by key) and terrible for OLAP; a column store is optimal for OLAP (aggregate a few columns over many rows) and terrible at OLTP (assembling one full row means gathering 50 separate column reads, and single-row writes touch 50 files). There is no layout that is best at both — so you keep two copies of the data in two shapes. The cost of fast analytics is a second, differently-shaped copy of your data — and a pipeline to keep it fed.

Move 2 — Model: a separate columnar warehouse, fed by a pipeline

The estimate says: store it by column and partition it by day. Two more decisions turn that into a system — where it lives, and how the data gets there.

Why the query must not run on the serving database

You might be tempted to just run the 30-minute scan on the production OLTP database at night. Don't. That scan reads 900 GB, evicts the hot working set from the buffer cache (Lesson 02), holds resources, and competes with live customer traffic — one analyst's report can push the serving tier past its utilization knee (Lesson 27) and into timeouts and retry storms (Lesson 07/28). Analytics and serving have opposite shapes and opposite failure modes; sharing one database means each can take the other down. So the warehouse is a separate system, and the operational data is copied into it.

The pipeline (ETL/ELT) and the star schema

  HOW DATA REACHES THE WAREHOUSE  —  and why it's always a little behind

   OLTP (serving, row store)          PIPELINE                 WAREHOUSE (columnar)
   +---------------------+     extract changes (CDC / dump)    +------------------+
   | orders  (L12/L25)   | ------------------------------->    |  fact_orders     |
   | customers, items... |     transform (clean, join, denorm) |  (columnar,      |
   +---------------------+     load into columnar partitions    |   partitioned    |
        live, fresh              runs on a SCHEDULE (nightly)   |   by day)        |
        millisecond writes       or continuously (streaming)   +------------------+
                                                                    STALE by the
                                                                    batch interval
  STAR SCHEMA (denormalized for scans, the opposite of OLTP's normalized):
     one big FACT table (one row per order: measures + foreign keys)
     small DIMENSION tables (region, item, customer) joined in
     -> the analyst scans the fact table; joins are few and small

Two design notes hide here. First, the pipeline runs on a schedule (say nightly) or continuously, and either way the warehouse holds a copy that is as fresh as the last run — the seed of this lesson's central problem. Second, the warehouse is denormalized into a star schema: instead of the OLTP world's many small normalized tables (which minimize duplication for cheap single-row writes), you pre-join into one wide fact table (one row per order, with the measures) surrounded by small dimension tables. Analytics almost never updates a row, so the storage duplication is harmless, and the pre-joining means a scan doesn't pay for a 10-way join every time. This is the same "precompute the read" move as the feed fan-out of Lesson 15 — spend storage and write-time work to make the read cheap.

Name the trade-off — normalize for writes, denormalize for reads. OLTP normalizes (each fact stored once) because it writes constantly and duplication would mean updating the same fact in many places. OLAP denormalizes (facts pre-joined and duplicated) because it barely writes and wants every read to avoid the join. Same data, opposite normal form, because one system optimizes the write and the other optimizes the read. Normal form is not a virtue; it's a bet on your write-to-read ratio.

Move 3 — Trace the paths

Follow one order from the live system to the dashboard, then follow the analyst's query back down.

Path A — an order becomes a warehouse row (the load)

  t=0        customer places order #12345 -> OLTP commits the row (L25 ledger), ms
  ...        order lives in the serving DB, powering "your order" pages all day
  t=nightly  pipeline EXTRACTS the day's new/changed orders (change-data-capture)
             TRANSFORMS: clean, join region/item dimensions, denormalize
             LOADS into fact_orders, appended to TODAY'S day-partition (columnar)
             columns get dictionary/RLE/delta encoded + compressed on write
  t=morning  analyst's dashboard can now see order #12345 -- up to ~24 h after it happened

The order was queryable for operations in milliseconds, but it enters the analytics world only at the next batch. That lag — the batch interval — is not a bug; it's the price of keeping analytics off the serving box and letting it run on a big, cheap, columnar copy. Whether 24 hours of staleness is fine depends entirely on the question (last quarter's trend: fine; live revenue-per-minute: useless).

Path B — the analyst's query (the scan)

  SELECT region, day, SUM(amount) FROM fact_orders
  WHERE day BETWEEN '2026-04-01' AND '2026-06-30' GROUP BY region, day

  1. PRUNE   day is the partition key -> engine reads 90 of 1,095 partitions,
             touches ZERO bytes of the other 1,005            (11 TB -> 900 GB)
  2. PROJECT reads only the `region` and `amount` column files (48 skipped) (-> 9 GB)
  3. SCAN    decompress + sum, split across 10 nodes, each owns ~9 partitions (~2.5 GB)
  4. AGGREGATE  each node sums its slice by (region,day); a final MERGE adds the
             per-node partials  (same local-compute-then-merge shape as L21/L06)
  -> answer in ~0.5 s, the serving DB never touched

Notice the funnel does its work before any arithmetic: pruning throws away 91.8% of the data by reading a partition-boundary catalog, projection throws away 96% of each surviving row by ignoring column files, and only the ~2.5 GB that survives both is ever decompressed and summed. The aggregation itself is the Lesson 21 pattern — each node computes a partial over its slice, and a small final step merges the partials — which is exactly why it parallelizes so cleanly. Fast OLAP is mostly the art of not reading data: prune, then project, then (only then) compute.

Move 4 — Find the first bottleneck: the warehouse is stale, and three traps around fixing it

Path A named the wall: the warehouse is a copy, fresh only as of the last batch. For "last quarter's trend" that's invisible. But the moment someone wants "revenue in the last 5 minutes" — a live ops dashboard, fraud alerting, an inventory feed — a nightly (or even hourly) batch simply cannot answer. Closing the freshness gap is the whole frontier of this domain, and it has a standard shape with three traps.

Worked: why you can't just "run the batch more often." The batch recomputes aggregates over history. Recomputing the quarter every 5 minutes means re-scanning ~2.5 GB × (however many queries) every 5 minutes — and recomputing all 3 years to keep every rollup current is the 6.1-hour full scan, which cannot fit in a 5-minute window. Batch cost scales with history size × frequency; pushing frequency up runs headlong into history size. Freshness by "batch more often" is quadratic-flavored and loses — you need a layer whose cost scales with the new data, not all data.

The two standard answers — lambda and kappa

  LAMBDA — run BOTH layers, merge at read                KAPPA — one streaming pipeline
  events (L09 log) --+--> BATCH layer                     events (L09 retained log)
                     |    (recompute all history nightly;         |
                     |     complete, correct, HOURS stale)        v
                     |                                       STREAM job maintains
                     +--> SPEED/STREAM layer                 running aggregates
                          (event-by-event, last few hrs;     (fresh to seconds)
                           fresh to seconds, approximate)          |
        serving view = BATCH(old) + SPEED(recent)           reprocess history by
        merged at query time                                REPLAYING the log through
        cost: SAME logic maintained TWICE, reconciled       the SAME job. one codebase.

Lambda accepts that batch is correct-but-slow and streaming is fresh-but-approximate, and runs both: the batch layer owns everything up to last night, the speed layer covers "since last batch," and the serving layer stitches them (batch answer for old days, streaming answer for today, until tonight's batch absorbs today and the speed layer resets). It works, but you now maintain the same aggregation logic in two places — a batch job and a stream job — and any discrepancy between them is a bug that shows up as a number that jumps when the batch catches up. Kappa collapses that to one streaming pipeline and treats "reprocess history" as "replay the retained log (Lesson 09) from offset 0 through the same stream code" — one codebase, no reconciliation, at the cost of a very expensive replay to backfill or fix a bug over trillions of events. Lambda pays in duplicated logic; kappa pays in replay cost. Both are buying freshness, and the bill is complexity.

The three traps

  • Trap 1 — using OLAP for an OLTP job (the selectivity trap, mirrored). The warehouse is superb at "sum 900M rows" and awful at "fetch the one row for order #12345" — a point lookup becomes a columnar scan of a whole partition because there's no OLTP-style primary-key index to seek with. This is Lesson 12's selectivity trap in reverse: a full scan wins for low-selectivity aggregates and loses badly for high-selectivity point queries. Send point lookups to the OLTP store; send aggregates to the warehouse. Using either for the other's job is the classic mistake.
  • Trap 2 — over-partitioning into a small-file swamp. Partitioning by day is what makes pruning work — but partition by minute ("for freshness!") and 3 years becomes 1,095 × 1,440 ≈ 1.58M partitions, each a tiny file. Now the engine spends all its time opening millions of files and reading their metadata instead of scanning data — Lesson 20's small-file problem, back again on the analytics side. Partition coarsely enough that each partition is a healthy chunk (hundreds of MB), not per-event. Freshness comes from the streaming layer, not from shredding the batch table.
  • Trap 3 — late and out-of-order events break streaming aggregates. The speed layer sums "the last 5 minutes" as events arrive — but a phone that was offline delivers its 2:59 order at 3:04 (Lesson 26). If you already closed the 2:55–3:00 bucket, that revenue is silently lost. Streaming engines handle this with a watermark — a moving "I've probably seen everything up to time T" marker that holds windows open a little longer for stragglers, trading a bit more latency for correctness. This is the Lesson 17 tail/late-arrival problem wearing an analytics hat, and it's why streaming aggregates are "approximate" until the batch layer (which sees the complete, reordered history) reconciles them.

Name the core trade-off — query freshness vs cost & complexity. Every rung up the freshness ladder costs more moving parts. A nightly batch on a columnar warehouse is the cheapest, simplest, most correct thing you can build — and it's a day stale. Hourly batch, then micro-batch, then a full streaming speed layer each buy fresher answers with more infrastructure, more failure modes (late events, watermarks, dual-codebase drift), and more spend. Lambda buys freshness with duplicated logic; kappa buys it with replay cost; both are strictly more complex than "just run it at night." The whole skill is matching freshness to the question: don't build a streaming pipeline to answer "last quarter's trend," and don't run a nightly batch to drive a live fraud alert. Buy exactly as much freshness as the decision behind the query actually needs — no more, because you pay for every second of it.

Where we landed

We took one analytics question — revenue by region by day for last quarter, over 11 billion orders (~11 TB) — and traced why it needs a machine the previous 28 lessons never built. Estimate priced it twice: a row store drags whole 1 KB rows to read 2 of 50 columns → 900 GB → 30 minutes for one tile, while a column store reads only the 2 columns, prunes to 90 of 1,095 day-partitions, and compresses → ~2.5 GB → 5 s → ~0.5 s across 10 nodes — a 360× cut bought entirely by reading fewer bytes, not a faster disk. Model gave the system: a separate columnar warehouse (analytics and serving have opposite shapes and opposite failure modes, so never share one DB), partitioned for pruning and denormalized into a star schema (precompute the join, the Lesson 15 move — normalize for writes, denormalize for reads), fed by an ETL/ELT pipeline that makes the warehouse a copy, and therefore behind. Tracing the load (an order is queryable for ops in ms but enters analytics only at the next batch) and the scan (prune → project → scan → aggregate-and-merge, the Lesson 21 partial-then-merge shape) showed that fast OLAP is mostly the art of not reading data. The first bottleneck was the warehouse's staleness: "batch more often" loses because batch cost scales with history × frequency, so freshness forces the lambda (two layers merged, paid for in duplicated logic) vs kappa (one streaming pipeline, paid for in replay cost) choice — guarded by three traps: point lookups on a scan engine (Lesson 12's selectivity trap in reverse), over-partitioning into a small-file swamp (Lesson 20), and late/out-of-order events needing a watermark (Lesson 17). The real lesson: a serving system and an analytics system are two differently-shaped copies of the same data, and the entire discipline is buying exactly as much freshness as the question needs — because every second of freshness is paid for in cost and complexity. The trade is query freshness vs cost & complexity.
