Bulk Data Pipelines & Backfills: Reprocessing Two Billion Rows Without Taking Down the Live System

Lesson 56 reprocessed an immutable log to erase one user. This lesson turns that one-off into a general capability: re-running a transform over all of history — billions of rows — because a schema changed (Lesson 24), a warehouse model changed (Lesson 29), a search index needs rebuilding (Lesson 16), or a bug wrote wrong data you must repair. The catch is that history doesn't sit in a quiet corner: it lives in the same database serving live traffic right now. So a backfill is a race between two goals that pull opposite ways — finish fast and don't hurt production. This lesson takes one concrete job — backfill a new currency column across 2 billion historical orders, the table from Lessons 12/24/25, on a live DB serving 50k reads/s — and traces it end to end. We'll estimate why the one obvious command (a single big UPDATE) detonates three ways. We'll model the backfill as a chunked, checkpointed, idempotent, throttled job — four parts, each fixing one of those detonations. We'll trace the happy batch, a crash at hour 50, and the moment production starts to feel it. Then the wall: the backfill and production fight over the same disk, CPU, and replication stream — so its speed is capped by production's spare headroom, not by how many workers you throw at it. The trade underneath: throughput vs impact on the live system.

The one job we'll design

Back in Lesson 24 we split the orders table's ambiguous total into amount_cents + currency using expand/contract, and new writes have been dual-writing both shapes ever since. The schema is fine. New rows are fine. But there are 2 billion old order rows written before the change, and every one of them has currency = NULL. Until those are filled in, no reader can trust the column, and we can't finish the migration (Lesson 24's cutover is blocked on a zero-NULL check). We need to backfill: compute currency for every historical row (derive it from the merchant's country) and write it back — 2 billion times.

The naive engineer opens a SQL console and types one line:

  UPDATE orders
     SET currency = derive_currency(merchant_id)
   WHERE currency IS NULL;          -- 2,000,000,000 rows

This is a career-defining mistake, and it fails three separate ways at once. Naming them is the whole lesson, because each one maps to a part of the fix:

  • It's one giant transaction. The database must hold locks and undo/redo information for all 2 billion changed rows until it commits. If it fails at row 1.9 billion — a deadlock, a disk-full, a failover — the whole thing rolls back to zero (all-or-nothing), and the rollback itself can take as long as the run. Fifty hours of work, gone, twice.
  • It runs flat-out. A 2-billion-row scan-and-write saturates the disk and CPU of the same database serving 50,000 reads/s. Production sails past its utilization knee (Lesson 27) into the retry storm (Lesson 07). Your backfill just caused an outage.
  • It can't survive a crash. At any realistic rate this runs for hours, and anything that runs for hours will be interrupted. With one transaction there's no "resume" — only "start over."
  The backfill and production share ONE database. That's the whole problem.

     live traffic (50k reads/s, 5k writes/s)  ->  [ PRIMARY DB ]  ->  replicas
                                                     ^   |              (readers,
                                                     |   | replication   L06)
        BACKFILL JOB  ----batched writes------------+   v
        (2B rows to fill)                          [ same disk, same CPU,
             |                                       same replication stream ]
             |  reads a chunk (cursor, L18)
             |  transforms it (derive currency)
             |  writes it back (one small txn)
             |  checkpoints the cursor  <--- crash resumes HERE, not at zero
             |  watches replica lag  <------- the throttle's feedback signal
             +--> repeat 400,000 times, throttled to production's SPARE capacity

  Throughput and "don't hurt production" pull in opposite directions on the
  SAME resource. The backfill is a background citizen; production is the priority.

A backfill is reprocessing history at scale, on infrastructure that is busy serving the present. The skill is not the transform — it's running it in bounded, resumable, idempotent chunks, throttled to whatever capacity production isn't using. "One big UPDATE" is the tip; the lesson is the four things that make it safe.

Words we'll earn before we lean on them

  • Backfill — filling in or recomputing data for existing historical rows, as opposed to a live pipeline that handles new rows going forward. A one-off (or occasional) reprocessing of the past.
  • Batch / chunk — a small, fixed slice of the work (e.g. 5,000 rows) processed and committed as its own unit, so no single transaction is enormous.
  • Cursor (keyset) pagination — walking the table by remembering the last key seen (WHERE id > :last_id ... LIMIT n) instead of counting positions with OFFSET (Lesson 18). Each page is a cheap B-tree seek (Lesson 12).
  • Checkpoint — a durable record of how far the job has gotten (the last committed cursor), written after each batch so a crash resumes from there instead of restarting.
  • Idempotent — safe to apply more than once: re-running a batch produces the same result as running it once, with no double-counting (Lesson 13).
  • Throttle / rate limit — deliberately capping the backfill's speed (Lesson 08/28) so it leaves headroom for live traffic.
  • Replica lag — how far behind the read replicas are in applying the primary's writes (Lesson 06). When a backfill writes faster than replicas can catch up, lag grows — the signal to slow down.
  • Headroom — the fraction of the database's capacity that live traffic isn't using. The backfill may spend this and no more (Lesson 27).

Move 1 — Estimate: why the one big UPDATE detonates, in numbers

Three quantities decide the shape of this job: how long it takes, how big the transaction gets, and how likely a crash is over that window. Get any of them wrong and you either take down production or lose fifty hours of work.

1. How long — and why "just run it" means days. The orders table is 200 B/row (Lesson 12: 100M rows = 20 GB), so 2 billion rows is 2,000,000,000 × 200 B = 400 GB to read and rewrite. A backfill that respects production can safely sustain only a fraction of the DB's throughput — say 10,000 rows/s (Lesson 24's figure). Then the run takes 2,000,000,000 ÷ 10,000 = 200,000 s ≈ 55.6 hours. This is not a "run it over lunch" job; it's a job that spans days, and everything about the design follows from that one fact.

2. How big the transaction gets — the all-or-nothing trap. One UPDATE over 2B rows is a single transaction holding undo/redo for 400 GB of changes and locks on 2B rows. If it fails at 95% done, the engine rolls the entire thing back — you've done 52 hours of work and pay for the rollback, ending exactly where you started. Batching into 5,000-row chunks makes each transaction tiny (~1 MB) and independently committed, so a failure costs one batch, not the run. That's 2,000,000,000 ÷ 5,000 = 400,000 batches.

3. How likely a crash is — why resumability isn't optional. Over a 55-hour window you will hit a deploy, a failover, a spot-instance reclaim, a network blip, or an OOM. Treat interruption as certain. Without a checkpoint, a crash at hour 50 restarts from zero — a second 55-hour run. With a checkpoint after every batch, a crash costs at most one batch = 5,000 rows ÷ 10,000 rows/s = 0.5 s of redone work. A checkpoint converts "lose 50 hours" into "lose half a second." That is the entire value of writing down where you are.

Name the trade-off — throughput vs impact on the live system. Every knob in this lesson is the same dial. Turn the backfill's rate up and it finishes sooner but steals IOPS, CPU, and replication bandwidth from the 50k reads/s that pay the bills — past the knee (Lesson 27), replica lag explodes and real users lose read-your-writes (Lesson 06). Turn it down and production is untouched but the run stretches from days toward weeks, and the longer it runs the longer you live in the fragile half-migrated state (Lesson 24). There is no setting that is fast and free — you are dividing one database between the present and the past.

Move 2 — Model: chunked, checkpointed, idempotent, throttled

A safe backfill is a small kit of four parts. Each one directly disarms one of the three detonations above (idempotency disarms a fourth hazard the naive version didn't even reach).

1. Chunk the work — bounded transactions, cursor not offset

Process the table in fixed slices (5,000 rows), each its own committed transaction. Two rules make the chunking cheap and correct:

Page by cursor, never offset (Lesson 18). The tempting way to slice is LIMIT 5000 OFFSET n — but OFFSET makes the database read and throw away all n rows to reach your slice. At batch 400,000 that's OFFSET 2,000,000,000: the final batch alone scans ~2 billion rows to return 5,000. The cost grows with depth, and the whole run becomes O(N²) — it gets slower and slower as you go. Instead remember the last key: WHERE id > :last_id ORDER BY id LIMIT 5000. Each batch is an O(limit) B-tree seek to last_id plus 5,000 rows (Lesson 12), constant cost at any depth — batch 400,000 is as cheap as batch 1.

Chunk on the indexed primary key, in order. Walking by id means the "which rows are left?" question is always a fast range scan, and the cursor is a single number you can checkpoint. It also gives you a clean, gap-free sweep even as new rows arrive at the end.

2. Checkpoint — write down how far you got

After each batch commits, record the cursor (last_id = the max id in that batch) to a durable place — a backfill_progress row, ideally in the same database. On startup, the job reads the checkpoint and resumes from id > last_id. The checkpoint is the single line between "a crash costs half a second" and "a crash costs the whole run." The subtlety: committing the batch and advancing the checkpoint is itself a two-write problem (Lesson 33's dual-write). If the batch commits but the process dies before the checkpoint is saved, restart will re-run that batch. Two clean fixes: put the checkpoint update in the same transaction as the batch (atomic — they commit or fail together), or make the batch idempotent so re-running it is harmless. Which brings us to part 3.

3. Idempotent re-runs — a replayed batch must be a no-op

Because crashes and retries mean some batches will run twice, the transform must be safe to apply more than once (Lesson 13). Whether that's free or hard depends on the operation:

Self-guarding (free). Our backfill is UPDATE ... SET currency = ... WHERE currency IS NULL. A row that was already filled no longer matches currency IS NULL, so a replay skips it automatically. Setting a derived value is naturally idempotent — writing the same answer twice is the same as writing it once.

Dangerous (needs a key). If the backfill incremented a counter, inserted a row, or appended to a ledger (Lesson 25), replaying a committed-but-uncheckpointed batch would double-count. The fix is Lesson 13's: a natural idempotency key (e.g. UNIQUE(order_id, backfill_version)) or a conditional write, so the second application is rejected as a no-op. The rule: design the transform so that "did this batch already run?" is a question the data itself answers — never rely on "I'm pretty sure it didn't."

4. Throttle against live load — replica lag is the feedback signal

The backfill is a guest; production is the host. So cap the rate (Lesson 08/28) to a target like 10,000 rows/s — but a fixed cap is a guess. The adaptive control is to watch a signal that tells you production is hurting and back off in real time. The best signal is replica lag (Lesson 06): the backfill's writes replicate to the read replicas, and if it writes faster than replicas can apply, lag climbs — which directly breaks read-your-writes for real users. So the loop is Lesson 27's controller pointed at lag: measure replica lag → if it exceeds a threshold (say 1 s), cut the rate or pause → when it recovers, ramp back up. This is Lesson 28's backpressure applied to a batch job: the live system pushes back, and the backfill yields. Secondary signals — primary CPU, DB queue depth, error rate — feed the same loop.

The four parts, each disarming one detonation: chunking kills the all-or-nothing giant transaction (a failure costs one batch); checkpointing kills the restart-from-zero (a crash costs half a second); idempotency kills the double-count when a batch inevitably re-runs; throttling kills the production outage (the backfill spends only spare capacity). Drop any one and the corresponding failure comes straight back.

Move 3 — Trace the paths

Follow the backfill through three moments: an ordinary batch, a crash at hour 50, and the moment production starts to feel the load.

Path A — the happy batch (repeated 400,000 times)

  last_id := read_checkpoint()          # resume point, e.g. 8,412,326,000

  loop:
    rows := SELECT id, merchant_id FROM orders
            WHERE id > :last_id AND currency IS NULL
            ORDER BY id LIMIT 5000      # cursor seek, O(limit), L18/L12

    if rows is empty: DONE

    BEGIN                               # one small transaction, ~1 MB undo
      for r in rows:
        UPDATE orders SET currency = derive_currency(r.merchant_id)
        WHERE id = r.id AND currency IS NULL     # idempotent guard, L13
      UPDATE backfill_progress SET last_id = max(rows.id)   # checkpoint...
    COMMIT                             # ...committed ATOMICALLY with the batch (L33)

    sleep_to_hit_rate_target()         # throttle to ~10k rows/s, L08/L28
    last_id := max(rows.id)

  Each iteration: a cheap seek, a tiny committed txn, a durable checkpoint,
  a pause. Nothing here can grow unbounded, roll back for hours, or lose
  more than one batch. 400,000 of these = the whole 2B-row history.

Path B — the crash at hour 50

  A deploy restarts the worker at batch 380,000 (id ~ 1.9B, 95% done).

  NAIVE (one big UPDATE):  the whole transaction rolls back -> 0% done.
                           50 hours of work gone. Start over.

  CHUNKED + CHECKPOINTED:  on restart, read_checkpoint() -> last_id = 1.9B.
                           Resume from id > 1.9B. The ~5,000 rows in the
                           in-flight batch fall into one of two cases:
                             (a) batch never committed -> re-selected, redone.
                             (b) batch committed but crash before checkpoint
                                 -> re-run finds those rows already non-NULL
                                    -> "WHERE currency IS NULL" skips them.
                           Either way: no gap, no double-write. Cost of the
                           crash: redo at most one 5,000-row batch (~0.5 s).

  The idempotent guard is what makes case (b) safe: a replayed batch is a
  no-op, so "did the checkpoint save?" stops being a question you must answer.

Path C — production starts to feel it

  Hour 12: an unrelated traffic spike pushes the primary toward its knee (L27).
  The backfill is now writing INTO a database that has no spare capacity.

  WITHOUT adaptive throttle (fixed 10k rows/s):
     backfill keeps writing -> replication can't keep up -> replica lag
     climbs 50 ms -> 4 s -> readers on replicas serve 4-second-stale data ->
     read-your-writes breaks (L06) -> the spike + backfill together tip the
     DB past the knee -> retry storm (L07). The backfill caused an incident.

  WITH adaptive throttle (watch replica lag):
     lag crosses 1 s -> controller cuts backfill rate to ~0 -> lag recovers
     as the spike drains -> backfill ramps back up. Production never noticed;
     the backfill just took a bit longer. The guest yielded to the host.

  This is L28's backpressure with the batch job as the thing being pushed back.

Path A is the steady state everyone eventually gets right. Path B is why you checkpoint and why idempotency is load-bearing, not decoration: a job that runs for days is a job that will crash, so "resume cheaply" and "safe to redo a batch" are the difference between a hiccup and a lost weekend. Path C is the whole trade in one picture: the backfill's only right to run is production's spare capacity, and the moment that spare vanishes, the backfill — not production — must give way.

Move 4 — Find the first bottleneck: the backfill and production fight over one machine

You've made each batch cheap, resumable, and idempotent. The wall that remains is structural and doesn't move: the history you're reprocessing lives in the same database that's serving live traffic, so the backfill competes with production for the exact same disk, CPU, and replication stream. Its speed is bounded by production's headroom, not by your cleverness — and that has sharp consequences.

1. More workers don't escape the wall — they spend the headroom faster. The obvious speedup is to parallelize by key range (Lesson 03): 8 workers, each owning a disjoint id range with its own cursor and checkpoint, gives 8 × 10,000 = 80,000 rows/s → the run drops to 2,000,000,000 ÷ 80,000 = 25,000 s ≈ 6.9 h. But 8 workers also put 8× the load on the shared DB. You haven't beaten the constraint — you've spent the spare capacity 8× faster. Parallelism is bounded by production's headroom ÷ per-worker load, not by how many workers you can launch. Add a ninth worker past the headroom and you're back to Path C's incident.

2. The real escape is to move the heavy read OFF the primary. The backfill's cost is mostly the giant read scan of history. So don't scan the primary at all: run the transform against a read replica or an offline snapshot / warehouse copy (Lesson 29) — the analytics-shaped store built exactly for full-history scans — compute the answers there, and write only the results back to the primary in small throttled batches. Now the 400 GB scan never touches the machine serving 50k reads/s; only the far smaller write stream does. You can't stop the backfill from competing with production, but you can move its biggest half to a copy that production doesn't depend on.

Two more walls sit behind those. The backfill's duration IS the mixed-state window. While it runs, currency is partially populated — some rows filled, some still NULL — so every reader must tolerate the gap (coalesce to a fallback, or read the old total) until the sweep completes and you flip the read path (Lesson 24's cutover, Lesson 31's staged rollout). A faster backfill shortens this fragile window; a throttled one lengthens it — the same throughput-vs-impact dial, now measured in how long you live half-migrated. And a backfill of a live table chases a moving target: new rows arrive at the end while you sweep. Chunking on the ascending primary key handles this cleanly (new ids are always ahead of your cursor, so you sweep right into them), but a backfill keyed on a mutable column, or one that must also fix rows written during the run, needs a second pass or a dual-write bridge (Lesson 24) so nothing slips through the gap between "backfilled" and "now."

The deepest point: a backfill is not a data problem, it's a scheduling problem. The transform is usually trivial; the hard part is dividing one busy database between the present and the past so that the past gets reprocessed without the present noticing. You do it by making the work interruptible (chunks), resumable (checkpoints), safe to redo (idempotency), and polite (adaptive throttle) — and then by accepting that its speed is a loan against production's spare capacity that you must repay the instant production needs it back. The standing tension: throughput vs impact on the live system.

The four traps

  • Trap 1 — one giant statement. A single UPDATE/DELETE over billions of rows is one transaction that holds locks and undo for the whole run and rolls back all-or-nothing on any failure. Always chunk into small, independently committed batches so a failure costs one batch, not the run.
  • Trap 2 — OFFSET pagination. LIMIT n OFFSET k reads and discards k rows every batch, so the job gets quadratically slower as it goes deep and the last batches crawl. Page by cursor (WHERE id > :last_id) so every batch is a constant-cost B-tree seek (L18/L12).
  • Trap 3 — no checkpoint, or a non-idempotent transform. Without a durable checkpoint, a crash at hour 50 starts over. And because a committed-but-uncheckpointed batch will re-run, a transform that increments/inserts without an idempotency key double-counts. Checkpoint every batch, and make the write safe to redo (L13).
  • Trap 4 — no throttle, or ignoring the live signals. A backfill running flat-out steals the capacity production needs, blows up replica lag (breaking read-your-writes, L06) and tips the DB past its knee into a retry storm (L07/L27). Throttle to spare capacity and back off adaptively when replica lag or CPU rises (L28) — the backfill is the guest.

Where we landed

Backfilling is reprocessing the past on machinery busy with the present. Estimate sized the job — 2 billion 200-byte rows = 400 GB, ~55 hours at a production-safe 10k rows/s, 400,000 batches — and showed the one big UPDATE detonating three ways: all-or-nothing rollback, a live-DB outage, and no way to survive the crash a 55-hour run guarantees. Model gave the four-part kit, each disarming one detonation: chunk (bounded committed batches, cursor not offset, L18/L12), checkpoint (resume in 0.5 s instead of restarting 55 h), idempotency (a replayed batch is a no-op, L13/L33), and throttle (adaptive on replica lag, the guest yields to the host, L06/L28). Tracing walked the happy batch, a crash at hour 50 that costs one batch, and the spike where the adaptive throttle backs off so production never notices. The first bottleneck is structural: the backfill fights production for one machine's disk, CPU, and replication — so its speed is capped by production's headroom, not by worker count (parallelism just spends the headroom faster), and the real escape is to move the heavy history scan onto a replica or offline copy (L29) and write only results back. Bounded further by the mixed-state window the backfill's duration defines (L24/L31) and by the moving target of rows arriving mid-sweep. A backfill is a scheduling problem wearing a data problem's clothes: make the work interruptible, resumable, redo-safe, and polite — then treat its speed as a loan against spare capacity you repay the instant production needs it back. The standing tension: throughput vs impact on the live system.
