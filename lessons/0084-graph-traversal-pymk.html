Graph & Recommendation Traversal at Scale: One "People You May Know" Request Across a Billion-Edge Graph

Lesson 41 built the social graph — users are vertices, friendships are edges, and "who is connected to whom" is a query you answer by walking the edges. This lesson runs the query that made graphs famous: People You May Know (PYMK). It looks simple — "show me friends of my friends" — but at a billion users it is the sharpest fan-out problem in this whole course, and the place where a single celebrity in your friend list can take down a shard. We take one PYMK request for one user and trace it end to end: the friends we read, the friends-of-friends we expand into, how we score them by mutual friends, and what happens when one of those friends has ten million edges. We'll estimate the graph's size and watch the fan-out explode hop by hop (200 → 40,000 → 8,000,000). We'll model the graph as sharded adjacency lists and see why a two-hop query scatters across the whole cluster. We'll trace three paths — traverse-at-read, precompute-offline, and the supernode that blows both up. And we'll find the first bottleneck: the fan-out itself — the cost of a graph query is set by the degree of the nodes it touches, not the number of hops. The trade under all of it: traversal freshness vs precompute cost.

The one system we'll build

A social network: 1 billion users, each with about 200 friends. Friendship is mutual, so an edge runs both ways. We serve People You May Know: given a user, suggest people they aren't friends with yet but probably know — ranked so the best suggestion is on top. One very specific request travels through the system in this lesson, and we'll follow it step by step:

  • One request: compute PYMK for a user we'll call Alice — find her friends-of-friends, rank them by how many friends they share with her, return the top 50.
  • One toy graph to make it concrete: Alice's friends are Bob, Carol, Dave (real Alice has 200; we use 3 so the arithmetic fits on the page), and we'll watch the two-hop expansion turn into a ranked list.

Here is the whole query on one page. The signal we want — "how well do I probably know this person" — is just how many of my friends are also their friends. Watch the two-hop walk produce exactly that count:

  ALICE's friends (hop 1)          their friends (hop 2 = friends-of-friends)
  -----------------------          -------------------------------------------
        Bob   ───────────────────▶  Carol*, Eve,   Frank
        Carol ───────────────────▶  Bob*,   Eve,   Grace
        Dave  ───────────────────▶  Eve,    Frank, Heidi
                                        (* = already Alice's friend, drop it)

  Collect every hop-2 person, DROP Alice + Alice's existing friends,
  then COUNT how many times each name appears:

        Eve   appears 3×  (via Bob, Carol, Dave)  -> 3 mutual friends
        Frank appears 2×  (via Bob, Dave)         -> 2 mutual friends
        Grace appears 1×  (via Carol)             -> 1 mutual friend
        Heidi appears 1×  (via Dave)              -> 1 mutual friend

  RANK by that count  ->  PYMK: [ Eve(3), Frank(2), Grace(1), Heidi(1) ]
                          Eve is friends with ALL of Alice's friends -> top pick

The one idea the whole lesson turns on: a PYMK query is a two-hop breadth-first walk (out to my friends, then out to their friends), and the number of times a person shows up in that second hop is the number of mutual friends we share — the dedup and the scoring are the same operation. That walk is cheap for one user drawn on paper. The entire engineering problem is that each hop multiplies the work by the average number of friends (the degree), so the walk that touches 200 people at hop 1 touches 40,000 at hop 2 and 8,000,000 at hop 3 — and one abnormally connected person (a celebrity) makes a single hop touch millions on its own. Everything hard about graph systems is bounding and pre-shaping that fan-out.

Words we'll earn before we lean on them

  • Vertex (node) — one thing in the graph. Here, a user.
  • Edge — a connection between two vertices. Here, a friendship. Mutual friendship = an edge in both directions.
  • Degree — how many edges a vertex has: a user's friend count. Average degree here is 200; a celebrity's degree can be millions.
  • Adjacency list — how the graph is stored: for each vertex, the list of its neighbors' ids. Alice → [Bob, Carol, Dave]. Reading one vertex's neighbors is one list read.
  • Hop — one step along an edge. Friends are 1 hop away; friends-of-friends are 2 hops away.
  • BFS (breadth-first search) — explore the graph level by level: everything 1 hop out, then everything 2 hops out. PYMK is a 2-hop BFS.
  • Fan-out — how the work multiplies at each hop: at average degree d, hop 1 touches d vertices, hop 2 touches ~d², hop 3 ~d³.
  • Friends-of-friends (FoF) — the vertices exactly 2 hops away. The candidate pool for PYMK.
  • Mutual friends — people who are friends with both me and a candidate; the count is the main PYMK ranking signal.
  • Supernode — a vertex with a huge degree (a celebrity, a brand). Expanding it reads millions of edges at once — the graph's version of L79's hot key.
  • Edge sharding / graph partitioning — splitting the graph across machines by vertex (L79). The trouble: neighbors land on different shards, so a multi-hop walk crosses machines.
  • Traverse-at-read — compute the answer by walking the graph live, on each request. Fresh, but expensive per request.
  • Precompute — walk the graph offline in a batch, store each user's answer, and read it back with one lookup (L15). Cheap read, but stale between runs.
  • Candidate generation — the cheap step that produces a pool of possibilities (here, the FoF) before an expensive ranker scores them (L16/L53's two-phase shape).

Move 1 — Estimate: the graph's size, and the fan-out explosion

Three numbers set up every decision: how big the graph is, how fast the walk explodes hop by hop, and how lopsided traversing-live is versus precomputing.

How big is the graph? One edge is just a neighbor id in an adjacency list. Friendship is mutual, so every friendship is stored as two directed edges (Alice→Bob and Bob→Alice), which is what makes a walk in either direction a single list read:

  users          N = 1,000,000,000            (1 billion vertices)
  avg friends    d = 200                       (average degree)
  directed edges E = N × d = 200,000,000,000   (200 billion edges)

  one stored edge = one 8-byte neighbor id
     200,000,000,000 × 8 B = 1.6 TB of edges
     + vertex records / list overhead        ≈ ~2 TB total

  won't fit one machine -> shard across ~100 nodes
     ~2 TB / 100 = ~20 GB of graph per node    (fits in RAM)

The graph is ~2 TB — small enough to hold in memory across a hundred nodes, but far too big for one. So it is sharded (L79): each user's adjacency list lives on one shard, chosen by hashing the user id. Remember that fact — it is the reason a two-hop walk is about to become a scatter across the whole cluster.

The fan-out explosion: each hop multiplies by the degree. This is the single fact that shapes everything. Start at Alice, walk outward, and count how many vertices each hop reaches at average degree 200:

  hop 0:  Alice                                  1 vertex
  hop 1:  her friends           200^1  =            200
  hop 2:  friends-of-friends    200^2  =         40,000   <- PYMK candidates
  hop 3:  FoF-of-friends        200^3  =      8,000,000
  hop 4:  one more hop          200^4  =  1,600,000,000   <- 1.6× the ENTIRE
                                                             user base

  By hop 4 the walk has "eaten the graph": it touches more vertices
  than exist. The small-world effect (everyone ~6 hops apart) is the
  same fact from the cost side -- a few hops reach everyone.

This is why PYMK stops at exactly 2 hops. Hop 2 is the sweet spot on both axes: the signal is strong (a friend-of-a-friend you share several friends with is very likely someone you know), and the fan-out is still tractable (~40,000 raw candidates). Hop 3 is worse on both — the signal is thin ("a friend of a friend of a friend" barely means anything) and the count jumps to 8 million. The design rule the arithmetic forces: never take a hop you don't need, because every hop costs a factor of the degree.

Dedup and scoring are the same pass. Those 40,000 hop-2 hits are a multiset — the same person is reached along many paths. Collapsing the duplicates is where the ranking signal falls out for free:

  raw hop-2 hits (with multiplicity)  ~40,000
     -> build a histogram: id -> how many of my friends point at it
     -> that count = number of 2-hop paths from me to id
                   = number of MUTUAL FRIENDS we share
  after dedup (friend circles overlap heavily) unique FoF ≈ ~30,000
     minus the people already my friends, minus me
  keep the top 50 by mutual-friend count  ->  the PYMK list

The count that dedups the list is the score. A candidate reached along 8 different paths is a candidate you share 8 mutual friends with — no separate scoring pass, no second graph walk. (In production the mutual-friend count is one signal among several — recency, shared workplace, whether they're in your contacts — layered on top exactly like L16's re-rank, but the mutual-friend count from this one walk is the backbone.) Trade already visible: a wider candidate pool (more hops, higher degree kept) catches more true suggestions but costs more to expand and score — recall vs cost, the same funnel as search (L16).

The asymmetry: traversing live is heavy per request; precomputing is heavy once. This is L15's precompute-the-read, now on a graph.

  TRAVERSE-AT-READ (per PYMK request):
     read the adjacency list of each of Alice's 200 friends
       = 200 random list reads, scattered across ~200 shards
       -> ~40,000 candidate ids -> histogram -> rank -> top 50
     at 100M PYMK requests/day (~1,200/s avg, ~5,000/s peak):
       5,000 req/s × 200 reads = 1,000,000 adjacency reads/second
     ==> heavy, paid on EVERY request.

  PRECOMPUTE (offline batch, run nightly):
     for all 1B users: expand hop-2, score, keep top 50, store in a KV
       work = 1e9 users × 40,000 touches = 4e13 edge-touches / run
       at ~1e11 traversals/s across the cluster -> ~400 s ≈ 7 min
     read path becomes: ONE key lookup "pymk:alice" -> [top 50]
     ==> heavy ONCE per night; the read is a single cache hit.

Same choice as every read-heavy system in this course. Walk the graph live and every request pays the fan-out (fresh, but a million reads a second); or walk it once offline, store the answer, and serve reads from a lookup (cheap, but stale until the next run). The trade named — traversal freshness vs precompute cost — is the axis the rest of the lesson lives on.

Why the whole design is a fight against fan-out: a graph query looks like a tiny thing ("friends of my friends") but its cost is multiplicative — every hop multiplies by the average degree, and any single high-degree vertex multiplies by its degree. So the engineering is never about the number of hops in the abstract; it's about how many edges those hops actually touch. Precomputing, capping the degree you'll expand, and choosing 2 hops not 3 are all the same move: keep the number of edges the query touches bounded and small.

Move 2 — Model: sharded adjacency lists, and why a 2-hop walk scatters

Where the graph lives: shard by vertex

Each user's adjacency list lives on one shard, chosen by hashing the user id (L79 / consistent hashing L04). That keeps writes and single-user reads simple — but it scatters neighbors:

  shard = hash(user_id) mod 100

  Alice  -> shard 12 : adj(Alice) = [Bob, Carol, Dave]
  Bob    -> shard 87 : adj(Bob)   = [Carol, Eve, Frank]
  Carol  -> shard 40 : adj(Carol) = [Bob, Eve, Grace]
  Dave   -> shard 03 : adj(Dave)  = [Eve, Frank, Heidi]

  Alice's 200 real friends hash to ~200 different shards at random.
  A 2-hop walk must therefore READ FROM up to 200 shards -> a scatter.

Sharding by vertex makes hop 1 a scatter-gather (L19/L21). To expand Alice's friends I must contact the shard that owns each friend's adjacency list. Two friends almost never share a shard, so the query fans out to as many shards as Alice has friends. This is the deep tension in graph storage, and Move 4's second wall: a graph has no clean shard boundary — cut it anywhere and you cut edges that a traversal wants to follow.

Why you can't just "keep friends together"

The obvious fix — put connected users on the same shard so a walk stays local — runs straight into a hard problem:

  Goal: partition 1B vertices into 100 balanced shards while cutting
        as FEW edges as possible (so walks stay on one shard).
  This is BALANCED GRAPH PARTITIONING (min-cut) -> NP-hard at this size.

  And even a perfect cut can't win: a social graph is densely tangled
  (small-world). Alice's 200 friends belong to overlapping communities
  that each pull toward different shards -> most edges get cut no matter
  how you slice. Heuristics (METIS, Louvain communities) reduce the cut
  but never eliminate the cross-shard hop.

The trade named: locality vs balance. Co-locate connected users and a walk touches fewer shards (fast) — but communities are lopsided in size, so shards become unbalanced (some overloaded, L79 hotspot). Hash by id and shards are perfectly balanced — but every hop scatters. You cannot maximize both; production systems pick a point on this line (usually hash-balanced with replication to soften the scatter) and live with the cross-shard tax.

The two-phase shape: cheap candidates, then expensive ranking

PYMK is a recommendation pipeline, and it has the same two phases as search (L16) and the feature store (L53):

  CANDIDATE GENERATION (cheap, broad): the 2-hop walk
     -> ~30,000 friends-of-friends, each with a mutual-friend count
  RANKING (expensive, on candidates only): score the survivors
     -> mutual friends + recency + shared org/school + contacts
     -> an ML model on the top few thousand (L53 features, L16 re-rank)
  FILTER (business rules): drop blocked users, people already dismissed,
     already-friends, self  ->  keep the top 50 shown

You never rank the whole graph — you rank the ~30k the walk already found. The graph traversal is the cheap wide gate that produces candidates; the costly per-candidate scoring runs only on what survives. Trade: a broader gate (3 hops, keep supernodes) catches more true connections (recall) but floods the ranker and slows the query (cost) — the L16 funnel, wearing a graph hat.

Move 3 — Trace the paths: traverse live, precompute offline, and the supernode

Path A — traverse-at-read for a normal user (heavy, per request)

   PYMK(Alice) arrives at a coordinator
     │
     ▼
   read adj(Alice) from shard 12 -> [Bob, Carol, Dave]   (hop 1: 1 read)
     │  (real Alice: 200 friends)
     ▼
   SCATTER: ask each friend's shard for its adjacency list  (hop 2)
     shard 87: adj(Bob)   = [Carol, Eve, Frank]
     shard 40: adj(Carol) = [Bob, Eve, Grace]
     shard 03: adj(Dave)  = [Eve, Frank, Heidi]
     │  (real Alice: ~200 reads across ~200 shards)
     ▼
   GATHER + histogram, drop Alice & her friends (Bob,Carol,Dave):
     Eve:3  Frank:2  Grace:1  Heidi:1
     ▼
   rank by count, apply filters, keep top 50  ->  return

Fresh to the millisecond — a friendship made 5 seconds ago is already in the walk. The price is the scatter: ~200 cross-shard reads per request, ~1,000,000 reads/second across the cluster at peak. Correct and current, but expensive.

Path B — precompute offline (cheap read, stale by construction)

   NIGHTLY BATCH (graph engine over the whole 2 TB graph):
     for every user u:  expand hop-2, histogram, keep top 50
     -> write  pymk:{u} -> [top 50 candidates]  into a KV store (L15)
     total work ~4e13 touches -> ~7 min on the cluster

   READ PATH at request time:
     PYMK(Alice) -> GET pymk:alice -> [Eve, Frank, Grace, Heidi, ...]
     -> one lookup, ~1 ms, no graph walk at all

Path B turns a million-read scatter into a single key lookup — but the answer is as old as the last batch. A friendship Alice made this morning won't influence her suggestions until tonight's run. That staleness is the whole cost of precomputing, and it's the first wall in Move 4.

Path C — the supernode blows both paths up

   Suppose one of Alice's friends is a CELEBRITY with 10,000,000 friends.
   Expanding that ONE friend at hop 2:
     read adj(celebrity) -> 10,000,000 neighbor ids in a single step
       -> the FoF set explodes from ~30k to 10,000,000+
       -> one shard (the celebrity's) gets hammered by every PYMK query
          that has the celebrity as a friend  ==> HOT SHARD (L79)
   And the payoff is near zero:
     10M people are all "friends-of-a-friend" through the celebrity,
     so being reached via the celebrity says almost NOTHING about
     whether Alice knows them. High cost, no signal.

One supernode is both the cost bomb and the noise source — and that alignment is the fix. Because expanding a very-high-degree vertex is expensive and carries almost no PYMK signal, the standard move is to skip or sample high-degree nodes during expansion: don't traverse through anyone with degree above some threshold (say 5,000). It bounds the fan-out and removes noise in the same stroke. Trade: you might miss a genuine connection that only exists through a hub — accepted, because keeping it would cost millions of reads to surface a near-worthless candidate.

Move 4 — Find the first bottleneck: the fan-out, and the walls around it

The first bottleneck — the fan-out itself, concentrated in supernodes. A normal 2-hop walk touches ~40,000 vertices; a single celebrity friend makes it touch 10,000,000. The cost of a graph query is set by the degree of the vertices it touches, not the abstract hop count. Two moves tame it:

  1) PRECOMPUTE the common answer (L15): pay the 40,000-touch walk once
     per user, nightly, and serve reads from a KV lookup. Turns 1M
     reads/s into 1M cache hits/s.
  2) CAP THE DEGREE you expand: skip vertices with degree > threshold
     during traversal. Bounds any single hop to ~threshold edges and
     strips the low-signal celebrity FoF at the same time.

Precompute bounds how often you pay the walk; degree-capping bounds how big any one walk gets. Together they turn an unbounded, spiky fan-out into a bounded, predictable one. The trade each buys: precompute trades freshness for read cost; degree-capping trades a little recall (a missed hub connection) for a hard ceiling on cost.

The second wall — freshness: precompute is stale, so patch it online. A nightly batch means new friendships don't show for up to a day. The fix is the hybrid from L15 (push+pull) and L53 (offline+online features): a large precomputed base, patched at read time with the handful of edges that changed since the batch.

   OFFLINE (nightly): the heavy 2-hop walk -> pymk:{u} base list  (bulk)
   ONLINE (per request): read the base list, THEN cheaply fold in the
     user's edges created since the batch:
       new friend X made today -> read adj(X) (one list) -> merge its
       FoF into the base list, re-rank the top 50
   result: yesterday's expensive answer + today's cheap delta = fresh,
     without re-walking the whole 2-hop neighborhood on every request.

This is a lambda-shaped split (L29/L53): a cheap-to-serve batch base plus a small real-time patch. You pay the giant walk once, but you stay current by only ever expanding the few edges that are new. Trade named: how much you push to the batch (cheaper reads, more staleness between patches) vs how much you patch online (fresher, more per-request work) — the freshness-vs-cost dial set to a middle notch.

The third wall — the cross-shard traversal tax. Because the graph is sharded by vertex (Move 2), a 2-hop walk contacts up to ~200 shards, and its latency is the slowest of those shards (the tail-latency / straggler problem from L18/L21's scatter-gather). Two mitigations, both trading storage for speed:

   REPLICATE hot / high-degree vertices onto every shard (or a cache
     tier) so expanding a popular friend is a local read, not a network
     hop (L02/L48 caching, L20 read replicas).
   PARTITION for locality where you can (community detection, L79) so
     dense friend-clusters share a shard and most hops stay local --
     accepting some shard imbalance in return.

There is no cut that keeps a tangled graph local and balanced (Move 2), so you buy your way around it with copies. Replicating supernodes and caching hot adjacency lists turns cross-shard hops into local reads — spending memory to dodge the network, the same storage-for-latency trade as a covering index (L12) or a CDN (L14). Trade: more replication = faster local walks but more memory and more write fan-out to keep the copies in sync.

The fourth wall — offline vs online candidate generation is a spectrum, not a switch. The very same freshness-vs-cost choice appears one level up, in where candidates are generated (L53's feature store shape). Fully offline: nightly batch, cheapest reads, stalest. Fully online: walk live on every request, freshest, most expensive. Most systems sit in between — precompute the bulk, generate a few candidates online from recent activity, and let an online ranker combine them. The graph traversal is just one candidate source feeding a ranker that also pulls from embeddings, contacts, and shared-attribute matches. Trade named for the last time: traversal freshness vs precompute cost — and the honest answer is almost always "both, split by how fast each signal changes."

So the honest picture: PYMK is a two-hop breadth-first walk whose cost is governed by fan-out. At average degree 200 the walk reaches 200 → 40,000 → 8,000,000 vertices per hop, which is why it stops at 2 (strong signal, tractable size). Estimate sized the graph (~2 TB, sharded across ~100 nodes) and showed the mutual-friend score falling out of the hop-2 multiplicity for free, and the asymmetry between traversing-live (~1M reads/s) and precomputing (heavy once, ~7 min nightly, then a lookup). Model put the graph in sharded adjacency lists, showed why a 2-hop walk scatters across ~200 shards, why you can't keep friends co-located (balanced min-cut is NP-hard and a tangled graph resists any clean cut), and framed PYMK as candidate-generation → ranking (L16/L53). Tracing a live walk, a precomputed lookup, and a supernode showed the celebrity as both the cost bomb and the noise source. The first bottleneck — the fan-out concentrated in supernodes — is tamed by precomputing the common answer (L15) and capping the degree you expand. Around it: freshness is bought back with an offline base + online patch (L29/L53 lambda), the cross-shard tax is softened by replicating hot vertices (storage-for-latency, L02/L48), and candidate generation lives on an offline↔online spectrum (L53). The standing tension: traversal freshness vs precompute cost.

The deepest point: a graph query's cost is set by the degree of the vertices it touches, not the number of hops on the whiteboard. "Friends of friends" is one line of English and a multiplicative bomb — one hop is ×200, a celebrity friend is ×10,000,000. Once you see that, every graph technique in this course is the same instinct: bound the fan-out and pre-shape it. Take the fewest hops that carry signal (2, not 3). Precompute the walk so you pay it once, not per request (L15). Cap the degree you expand so no single vertex can blow up a hop. Replicate the hubs so the walk stays local (L79/L48). And patch the stale precompute with a cheap online delta so you get freshness without re-walking the world (L29/L53). The genuinely new idea is small: a traversal is a controlled explosion, and the whole job is keeping it controlled. The trade to carry: traversal freshness vs precompute cost.

The four traps

  • Trap 1 — taking one hop too many. Every extra hop multiplies the work by the average degree (×200) and thins the signal. Three hops isn't "a bit more" than two — it's 200× the candidates for connections that barely mean anything. Take the fewest hops that carry signal.
  • Trap 2 — treating all vertices as equal. A degree-200 user and a degree-10,000,000 celebrity cost wildly different amounts to expand. Ignore the degree distribution and one supernode in a friend list takes down a shard. Cap or sample high-degree expansion; the graph's cost lives in its hubs.
  • Trap 3 — sharding a graph by hashing the vertex and forgetting the edges. Hashing balances storage perfectly but scatters every multi-hop walk across the cluster, and its latency becomes the slowest shard's. Plan for the cross-shard tax: replicate hot vertices, cache hot lists, or partition for locality — you cannot have balance and locality both.
  • Trap 4 — choosing precompute or live traversal. Pure offline is stale; pure online is a fan-out bomb per request. The real answer is the hybrid: a precomputed base for the bulk of the signal plus a cheap online delta for what changed since the batch — freshness bought without re-walking the world.

Where we landed

We ran the query that made graph systems famous — People You May Know — for one user, end to end. Estimate sized the graph at ~2 TB (1B users × 200 friends × 8 B, sharded across ~100 nodes) and watched the fan-out explode 200 → 40,000 → 8,000,000 → 1.6 billion, which is exactly why PYMK stops at 2 hops; the mutual-friend score fell out of the hop-2 multiplicity for free, and traversing live (~1M adjacency reads/s at peak) proved far heavier than precomputing (one ~7-minute nightly walk, then a KV lookup — L15). Model stored the graph as sharded adjacency lists, showed that hashing users to shards scatters a 2-hop walk across ~200 machines, explained why keeping friends co-located fails (balanced min-cut is NP-hard and a small-world graph resists any clean cut — locality vs balance), and framed PYMK as candidate-generation → ranking (L16/L53). Tracing a live walk, a precomputed lookup, and a supernode revealed the celebrity as both the cost bomb (10M edges in one hop → hot shard, L79) and the noise source (FoF-via-celebrity means nothing) — the alignment that makes degree-capping the fix. The first bottleneck — fan-out concentrated in supernodes — is tamed by precompute (bounds how often) plus degree-capping (bounds how big); around it, freshness is bought back with an offline base + online patch (L29/L53), the cross-shard tax is softened by replicating hot vertices (storage-for-latency, L02/L48), and candidate generation lives on an offline↔online spectrum. The deepest point: a graph query's cost is set by the degree of the vertices it touches, and the whole discipline is bounding and pre-shaping that fan-out. The standing tension: traversal freshness vs precompute cost.
