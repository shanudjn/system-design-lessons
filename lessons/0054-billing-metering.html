Billing & Metering: Turning a Usage Firehose into a Bill Correct to the Cent

Lesson 53 fed a fraud model the right numbers to guard the money. This lesson follows the money the other way — into the bill. A pay-as-you-go platform charges for what customers actually use: every API call, every GB stored for an hour, every GB shipped out. Each of those is a tiny usage event, and at month's end billions of them must become an invoice that is correct to the cent — no event counted twice, none dropped, each priced by the plan in force when it happened, and every figure reconcilable against the ledger. This is where "approximate is fine" — the friendly shortcut of Lesson 21 — becomes a liability, because a rounding error here is not noise on a dashboard, it is money moved wrongly, and it hurts in both directions: undercount and you leak revenue silently; overcount and you overbill a customer, who disputes, refunds, and leaves. We'll take one concrete pipeline — a cloud platform metering 50 billion billable events a day — and estimate the firehose and the true cost of a small error. We'll model the four stages that turn usage into an invoice (ingest → aggregate → rate → reconcile) and the four properties that make it trustworthy. We'll trace one event to a line item, a duplicate that must not double-charge, and a month-end close that catches drift before the customer sees it. Then we'll hit the wall the whole thing leans on: to promise "no event counted twice" you must remember every event you've ever seen — and you cannot make that memory approximate. The trade underneath: billing accuracy vs metering cost & latency.

The one system we'll design

A cloud platform sells three metered products. Customers don't pay a flat fee; they pay for exactly what they consume:

  • API calls — every request to the platform's API is one billable unit.
  • Storage — every GB kept, charged per GB-hour it sits there.
  • Egress — every GB shipped out to the internet.

The whole book is about $50 million a month across roughly 1 million paying accounts. Every one of those charges traces back to a usage event — a small record emitted by the service that did the work: "account A made 1 API call at 12:00:03," "account B held 40 GB for this hour." The billing system's only job is unglamorous and unforgiving: take the firehose of usage events and turn it into an invoice that is exactly right — no double-counts, no drops, correctly priced, and provable against the money that actually changed hands.

Why is "exactly right" the whole game? Because a metering error is not symmetric noise you can wave away. It breaks two ways, and both are bad:

  • Undercount (you drop events) → you bill less than the customer used → revenue leakage, silent, forever.
  • Overcount (you double-count events) → you bill more than they used → the customer disputes, you refund, trust erodes, and in regulated markets you have an overbilling problem, not a bug.
  A billing pipeline: usage firehose -> invoice, with a reconciliation loop.

   services emit                                              customer

  The raw log is the source of truth. Aggregates, invoices, and dashboards are

A billing/metering system ingests a firehose of usage events exactly once each, aggregates them into per-account usage counters, rates those counters against the pricing plan in effect when the usage happened, sums the result into an invoice, and independently reconciles every derived number against the durable raw event log and the ledger — so the bill is provably correct to the cent. It is not a counter with a price sign taped on. The hard parts are all about exactness under an unreliable firehose: making at-least-once delivery count once, keeping the aggregate exact rather than approximate, pricing by time, and being able to prove the total.

Words we'll earn before we lean on them

  • Usage event — a small durable record that some unit of billable work happened: {account, meter, quantity, event_time, event_id}.
  • Meter — a named thing you count and charge for: api_calls, storage_gb_hours, egress_gb.
  • Event id / idempotency key — a unique id the emitter stamps on each event (Lesson 13), so a retried event can be recognized as the same one and counted once.
  • Dedup window — how far back the system remembers event ids to reject duplicates. Bounded by how late a duplicate can possibly arrive (Lesson 13's TTL).
  • Usage counter / aggregate — the running exact sum of a meter for one account over a time bucket (e.g. account A's api_calls for this hour).
  • Rating — turning a usage quantity into money by applying the price plan. Also called pricing.
  • Tiered pricing — a plan where the per-unit price changes as usage grows (first 1M calls free, next block cheaper, and so on). Rating is a function over the aggregate, not one multiply.
  • Point-in-time pricing — rating each unit of usage at the price that was in effect when it was consumed, not the price today (the Lesson 53 point-in-time idea, applied to money).
  • Invoice — the customer-facing bill at cycle close: the summed, rated charges.
  • Reconciliation — independently recomputing totals from the raw log and comparing them to the aggregates, the invoice, and the ledger to catch drift.
  • Drift — any disagreement between two numbers that should be equal (raw log vs aggregate, invoiced vs collected). Drift = a bug you catch before the customer does.
  • Event-time vs processing-time — when the usage happened vs when the billing system saw it. Bills must use event-time (Lesson 35).
  • Watermark — a moving "we've now seen all events up to time T" marker that lets you close a billing period without cutting off stragglers (Lesson 29).

Move 1 — Estimate: the firehose is huge, and a tiny error is expensive money

Two numbers set the whole design: how big the stream is (so you know what "keep every event" costs), and what a fraction-of-a-percent error is worth (so you know why you can't approximate it away).

1. The firehose. Peak billable volume is 50 billion events/day. That's 50,000,000,000 ÷ 86,400 s ≈ 579,000 events/s on average (call it ~1.2M/s at peak). Each event is small — {account, meter, quantity, event_time, event_id} at about 200 bytes — so the raw stream is 50e9 × 200 B = 10 TB/day. You cannot bill from a stream you've thrown away (a dispute six weeks later needs the evidence), so the raw log is retained: a 30-day cycle alone is 300 TB, and a 12-month audit window is 365 × 10 TB ≈ 3.65 PB/yr. That number forces tiered storage later (Lesson 39) — but note what you can not do: you cannot down-sample or sketch the raw log to shrink it, because it is the evidence you reconcile against.

2. Why "exactly right" is not fussiness — it's money. The book is $50M/month. Suppose the pipeline has a quiet 0.5% metering error (half a percent — the kind a naive design leaks through duplicate retries and dropped events). That is 0.5% × $50,000,000 = $250,000/month mischarged, = $3,000,000/yr. If it leans undercount, that's $3M/yr of revenue gone with no alarm. If it leans overcount, that's $3M/yr of overbilling — disputes, refunds, churn, and regulatory exposure. A 0.5% error that would be invisible on an analytics dashboard is a seven-figure problem on an invoice. That single fact is why billing is the one place the cheap approximate counters of Lesson 21 are disqualified for the number you actually charge.

Name the trade-off — billing accuracy vs metering cost & latency. You could meter cheaply and fast: sample events, sum with a Count–Min sketch, skip the dedup store. That's perfect for a live "usage so far this month" dashboard, where being a hair off and a minute stale costs nothing. It is fatal for the invoice, where the number is money and must be exact and provable. Exactness is bought with real resources: storing every event, remembering every event id to dedup, and accepting that the authoritative total lags (you close the period after the stragglers are in). The whole lesson is spending accuracy where it's money and approximation where it's only a dashboard.

Move 2 — Model: four stages, and the four properties that make the bill trustworthy

The pipeline is four stages — ingest, aggregate, rate, reconcile — and each exists to protect one of four properties. Miss any property and the bill is wrong in a specific, nameable way.

1. Ingest — count each event exactly once (the double-count / drop guard)

Events arrive from thousands of services over a queue (Lesson 9), which delivers at-least-once: a producer that times out and retries, or a broker that redelivers after a blip, will hand you the same event twice. Count both and you overbill. So every emitter stamps each event with a unique event id (Lesson 13's idempotency key), and ingestion does an atomic insert-if-absent keyed on that id — the exact same move as Lesson 25's UNIQUE(idem_key) and Lesson 6's compare-and-set. If the insert wins, the event is new: append it to the raw log and let it count. If it collides, it's a duplicate: drop it. The flip side is durability: an accepted event must be on durable storage before it's acknowledged, or a crash drops it (undercount). Ingest is where "at-least-once delivery" is converted into "counted exactly once" — not by making the wire exactly-once (you can't, Lesson 9), but by deduplicating on a durable key.

2. Aggregate — sum exactly, not approximately

You don't rate 50 billion individual events; you roll them into usage counters: for each (account, meter, hour), the exact running sum. Here is the discipline that separates billing from analytics: this sum must be exact. A HyperLogLog or Count–Min sketch (Lesson 21) is gorgeous for "roughly how many distinct users this month" but it over- or under-estimates by design, and that estimate is now dollars. So the counter is a plain exact accumulator (Lesson 6's atomic increment). You may keep an approximate sketch alongside it to power the customer's live "usage so far" dashboard cheaply — fast and slightly stale is fine there — but the billed number always comes from the exact aggregate. Two views of one meter, shaped for two jobs: the same split as Lesson 29 (batch-exact vs stream-fast) and Lesson 53 (offline-honest vs online-fast).

3. Rate — price each unit by the plan in effect when it happened

Rating turns a usage counter into money. It is rarely one multiply, because plans are tiered. Take this API-call plan and a customer who made 12,500,000 calls this month:

  Tier 1  first 1,000,000 calls .............. free
  Tier 2  next  9,000,000 (up to 10M) ....... $0.50 per million
  Tier 3  everything above 10,000,000 ....... $0.40 per million

Walk it, block by block:

• Tier 1: first 1,000,000 → $0.00.
• Tier 2: next 9,000,000 → 9 × $0.50 = $4.50.
• Tier 3: remaining 12,500,000 − 10,000,000 = 2,500,000 → 2.5 × $0.40 = $1.00.
• Invoice line = $0.00 + $4.50 + $1.00 = $5.50.

Now the point-in-time twist. Suppose the Tier-3 price dropped from $0.40 to $0.30 per million on the 15th. You cannot rate all 12.5M calls at today's price — the calls before the 15th were consumed under the old plan. So usage stays bucketed by event-time, and each bucket is rated at the price in effect then: pre-15th tiers at the old prices, post-15th at the new. Rating reads the price as of the usage instant, never "now" — the exact point-in-time rule from Lesson 53, now protecting money instead of a model. Get this wrong and every mid-cycle price change silently misprices half the month.

4. Reconcile — prove the total, don't trust it

Every number the customer sees is derived from the raw log: the aggregate is a sum of it, the invoice is a sum of aggregates. So you can independently recompute each level and check it. Reconciliation re-sums the raw event log for the cycle and asserts it equals the sum of the usage counters, which equals the sum of the invoice lines. Then it checks the money side against the ledger (Lesson 25): invoiced amounts should become receivables, payments should land as ledger entries, and billed-but-never-collected or collected-but-never-billed are drift classes reconciliation surfaces. Any mismatch is a bug caught before the invoice is sent — you hold the bill and alert, rather than shipping a wrong number and refunding later. This is only possible because the raw log was kept (Move 1's durability) and never approximated (Move 2's exactness). Reconciliation is the property that turns "we think it's right" into "we can prove it."

The four properties, named: counted-once (ingest dedup), summed-exactly (exact aggregate), priced-as-of-then (point-in-time rating), and provable (reconciliation vs the raw log and ledger). Each maps to one failure: double-count/drop, approximation error, misprice, and undetected drift. A billing system is exactly these four guards bolted to a firehose.

Move 3 — Trace the paths

Follow the same meter through the three journeys that decide correctness: one event to a line item, a duplicate that must not double-charge, and the month-end close where reconciliation earns its keep.

Path A — one usage event becomes a line item

  Account A makes an API call at 12:00:03. The API service emits an event.

   t=0      service stamps: {account:A, meter:api_calls, qty:1,
            event_time:12:00:03, event_id:"e-9f3a..."} and enqueues it (L09).
   ingest   atomic insert-if-absent on event_id:
            -> id is NEW -> append to raw log (durable) -> ACK. Counts once.
   aggregate the exact counter (A, api_calls, hour=12:00) += 1.
   rate     at cycle close, the hour buckets sum to A's monthly total, rated
            tier-by-tier at the price in effect in each bucket -> $5.50.
   invoice  the $5.50 lands as one line on A's invoice; the total reconciles
            back to the exact sum of A's raw events. Nothing was estimated.

  The hot path (emit -> ingest) is cheap and fast; the expensive exactness work
  (aggregate, rate, reconcile) runs off the request, on event-time buckets.

Path B — a duplicate that must not double-charge (at-least-once, L09/L13)

  The API service emits the event, then times out waiting for the queue ACK,
  and RETRIES -- so the SAME event arrives twice.

  FIRST copy   event_id "e-9f3a" -> insert-if-absent WINS -> logged, counted.
  SECOND copy  event_id "e-9f3a" -> insert-if-absent COLLIDES -> dropped.
               A's counter stays +1, not +2. No double-charge.

  Contrast the naive design (no event_id, count every arrival):
     the retry adds a second +1 -> A billed for 2 calls, made 1 -> overcount.
     Across the book at 0.5% duplication that is ~$3M/yr of overbilling.

  The dual failure -- a DROPPED event (crash before durable append) -> undercount
  -- is NOT caught here. It is caught later, by reconciliation (Path C): the raw
  log is missing an event the emitter's own sequence numbers say should exist.

Path C — month-end close: watermark, then reconcile

  It's 23:59:59 on the last day. Can you close the invoice? Not yet.

  STRAGGLERS: an event with event_time 23:59:58 might be INGESTED at 00:00:07
  next month (queue lag, a retried emitter). Bill by EVENT-TIME (L35), so it
  belongs to THIS invoice even though you saw it next month.
   -> hold the period open behind a WATERMARK (L29): "all events up to T are in."
      Close only once the watermark passes the cycle end + a grace window.
   -> a straggler later than the grace window -> a correcting adjustment on the
      NEXT invoice (a compensating line, L25/L32) -- never silently dropped.

  RECONCILE before sending:
     sum(raw log for cycle)  ==?  sum(usage counters)  ==?  sum(invoice lines)
     invoiced amounts        ==?  receivables posted to the LEDGER (L25)
   -> all equal        -> send the invoice.
   -> any mismatch     -> HOLD, alert, investigate. A wrong bill never ships.

  Trade named: close FAST (customer gets billed promptly) vs WAIT for stragglers
  (accuracy). The watermark + grace window is exactly that dial.

Path A is the happy path: an event stamped with an id, deduplicated once, summed exactly, rated by event-time, reconciled. Path B is why the id exists — at-least-once delivery guarantees duplicates, and the only thing standing between a retry and a double-charge is the atomic claim on the event id (Lesson 13's exactly-once effect on at-least-once delivery, no exactly-once on the wire). Path C is the part everyone underestimates: a billing cycle has a boundary, events straddle it, and closing on processing-time ("whatever we'd seen by midnight") bills the wrong month — so you close on event-time behind a watermark, and you never send until the derived totals reconcile against the raw log and the ledger.

Move 4 — Find the first bottleneck: to promise "counted once," you must remember every event — exactly

The dedup in Move 1 is what makes billing trustworthy, and it is also the wall. "No event is ever counted twice" is a promise about memory: to reject a duplicate you must remember that you already saw its event id. Remember for how long, and in how much space? Two forces press here, and the escape is precise about what you may and may not approximate.

1. The dedup store grows with the firehose, and you can't sketch it away. Event ids are ~16 bytes (a UUID). At 50 billion/day that's 50e9 × 16 B = 800 GB/day of ids just to remember them. If a duplicate can arrive up to 7 days late (an emitter that was partitioned and retried), the dedup window must cover 7 days: 7 × 800 GB = 5.6 TB of live keys, sharded across an in-memory tier (Lesson 4/48). This is the Lesson 13 TTL trap in dollars: forget too soon and a late duplicate sails through and double-charges; keep ids forever and the store grows without bound. You bound the window by the maximum retry horizon — how late a duplicate can physically be — and no longer. And you cannot shrink it with a sketch: a Count–Min or plain probabilistic counter would make the dedup itself approximate, and an approximate dedup is an approximate bill.

2. A Bloom filter can cut the lookups — but only because of which way its error points. Most arriving events are first-timers, so most exact-store lookups return "not seen" and are wasted. Put a Bloom filter (Lesson 21) in front, covering the full dedup window: if it says "definitely not seen," the event is genuinely new — accept it — because a Bloom has no false negatives. If it says "maybe seen," fall through to the exact insert-if-absent to be sure. The Bloom's only error is a false positive — "maybe seen" for something new — and that costs one extra exact lookup, never a wrong bill. Its impossible error, a false negative, is the only one that could drop a real duplicate. So the safe error points the safe way (Lesson 21's Bloom-as-guard, sized at ~9.6 bits/key). One caveat that is easy to get lethally wrong: the Bloom must cover the whole dedup window — age a key out early and "not seen" becomes a lie, and the duplicate double-charges. Bloom trims cost; the exact store still owns correctness.

Two more walls sit behind that one, both familiar. The hot account is a hot aggregate: one giant customer emitting a huge share of the events makes their usage counter a contended row (Lesson 6's lost-update race, Lesson 3's hot shard). The fix is the exact sharded counter — split the counter into N per-shard sub-counters, increment locally, and sum at read (Lesson 21/25's mergeable counter) — but every shard is an exact integer, so the merged total is still exact, unlike the approximate version. And late events never fully stop: even after the watermark and grace window, a straggler can arrive, so a billing cycle is never truly "sealed" — it's closed with a correction path (a compensating line on the next invoice, Lesson 25/32), the same way a ledger appends a reversal rather than editing the past. The deepest point: everything a customer is charged must trace back, exactly and provably, to a durable event that really happened — billing is the discipline of never losing, never doubling, and always being able to show your work.

The four traps

  • Trap 1 — counting every arrival (double-charge). Without an emitter-stamped event id, at-least-once redelivery adds a second +1 for the same usage, and 0.5% duplication becomes ~$3M/yr of overbilling and customer disputes. Stamp a unique event_id and dedup with an atomic insert-if-absent (Lesson 13/25); the retry then collides and is dropped, counted exactly once.
  • Trap 2 — approximating the billed number. A HyperLogLog or Count–Min sketch (Lesson 21) is cheap and perfect for a dashboard, but its inherent over/under-estimate is a billing error once it's money. Keep the billed aggregate exact; use a sketch only for the live "usage so far" view, never for the invoice.
  • Trap 3 — rating at today's price (mispricing time). Applying the current plan to the whole cycle silently misprices every unit consumed before a mid-cycle price change. Bucket usage by event-time and rate each bucket at the price in effect then — point-in-time pricing (Lesson 53's rule applied to money).
  • Trap 4 — closing on processing-time, and trusting the total. Cutting the invoice at "whatever we'd ingested by midnight" bills stragglers to the wrong month; sending it without an independent recompute ships wrong bills you refund later. Close on event-time behind a watermark with a grace window (Lesson 29/35), and reconcile every derived total against the raw log and the ledger (Lesson 25) before the invoice leaves.

Where we landed

A bill is a promise about money, and keeping it is a systems problem, not an accounting one. Estimate sized the firehose — 50 billion events/day ≈ 579,000/s, 10 TB/day of raw events that must be kept (3.65 PB/yr for audit) — and priced the danger: a quiet 0.5% error on a $50M/month book is $3M/yr mischarged, so approximation that's harmless on a dashboard is a seven-figure fault on an invoice. Model built the four-stage pipeline — ingest (dedup an at-least-once firehose to counted-once), aggregate (exact counters, never sketches), rate (tiered, point-in-time pricing: a 12.5M-call month rated block-by-block to $5.50), and reconcile (recompute every derived total against the durable raw log and the ledger) — mapped to the four properties: counted-once, summed-exactly, priced-as-of-then, and provable. Tracing walked one event to a line item, a retried duplicate that the atomic event_id claim drops before it double-charges, and a month-end close held behind a watermark so stragglers bill to the right cycle and no invoice ships until it reconciles. The first bottleneck is the dedup memory: to promise "never counted twice" you must remember every event id for the whole retry horizon — 5.6 TB of keys — and you may cut the lookups with a Bloom filter (whose only error, a false positive, costs an extra check, never a wrong bill) but you may not make the dedup itself approximate, with the hot-account counter sharded (still exact) and a permanent late-event correction path behind it. Billing is the discipline of never losing an event, never doubling one, pricing each by the moment it happened, and always being able to show your work. The standing tension: billing accuracy vs metering cost & latency.
