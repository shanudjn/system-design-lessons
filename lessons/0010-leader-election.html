Leader Election & Coordination: One Leader, No Split-Brain

Lesson 09 ended on a small panic: when a consumer flaps in and out, the group keeps re-deciding who owns which partition — a rebalance storm. Underneath that is one of the oldest problems in distributed systems: a group of equal machines has to agree on a single decision-maker, and keep agreeing even as machines die and networks split. This lesson takes ONE concrete cluster — five nodes that must run a billing cron job exactly once — and traces how they pick a leader, detect its death, and survive the nightmare where the network tears in two and both halves think they're in charge. The villain has a name: split-brain. The cure is a single counting trick: a majority. We name the trade-off behind every choice.

The one cluster we'll trace

We run a job that charges every customer their monthly bill. It must run on exactly one machine — if two machines run it, every customer is charged twice. But one machine is a single point of failure: if it dies on the 1st of the month, nobody gets billed. So we run five identical nodes and have them elect one leader to do the job; the other four stand by, ready to take over. Here is the whole shape on one screen.

  FIVE NODES, ONE LEADER, A HEARTBEAT EVERY SECOND

                       ┌─────────────┐
                       │  LEADER  A  │  term 1   ── runs the billing job
                       │  (epoch 1)  │
                       └──────┬──────┘
            heartbeat ♥/1s    │   "I'm alive, I'm term 1"
        ┌──────────┬──────────┼──────────┬──────────┐
        ▼          ▼          ▼          ▼          ▼
     ┌─────┐    ┌─────┐               ┌─────┐    ┌─────┐
     │  B  │    │  C  │               │  D  │    │  E  │   followers
     └─────┘    └─────┘               └─────┘    └─────┘   (watch the ♥)

  If 3 heartbeats are missed → a follower becomes a CANDIDATE, bumps the
  term to 2, and asks the others to VOTE. Win a MAJORITY (3 of 5) → leader.

Leader election is how a group of equal machines agrees on exactly one of them to make a class of decisions, and re-agrees when that one dies. The decision could be "who runs the cron job," "who is the primary that accepts writes," or "who assigns partitions to consumers" — the rebalance from Lesson 09. The shape is always the same, so we learn it once here.

Words we'll earn before we lean on them

  • Leader — the one node currently allowed to do the singleton work. Follower — a node that isn't, but is watching.
  • Heartbeat — a tiny "I'm alive" message the leader sends on a fixed interval. Silence is how death is detected.
  • Term (or epoch) — a number that goes up by one with every election. It stamps "which reign are we in," so stale messages from an old leader can be spotted and ignored.
  • Candidate — a follower that has stopped hearing heartbeats and is now asking others to vote for it.
  • Quorum / majority — more than half the nodes: ⌊N/2⌋ + 1. For N=5 that's 3. A decision needs a quorum to count.
  • Split-brain — the failure where two nodes both believe they are leader at the same time, and both act.
  • Fencing token — the term/epoch attached to every action, so a resource can reject an action stamped with an old reign.

Move 1 — Estimate: how fast can we notice the leader died?

Before any election, size the clock. Two numbers set everything: the heartbeat interval (how often the leader says "alive") and the timeout (how long of silence means "dead"). From those falls the failover time — the window where nobody is leader and no billing runs.

Worked: the failover budget.

Heartbeat interval H = 1 s. We declare death after 3 missed heartbeats, so timeout T = 3 s.

• A follower notices silence somewhere between 2 s and 3 s after the leader's last beat. Worst case = 3 s to suspect death.
• It becomes a candidate and runs an election: one round of "request vote" out and "yes" back ≈ one network round-trip ≈ 0.2 s.
• To avoid two candidates splitting the vote at once, each waits a small random extra delay (say 0–0.3 s) before standing — call it 0.3 s worst case.
• Failover ≈ 3 s + 0.3 s + 0.2 s ≈ 3.5 s with no leader. For a monthly billing job that runs for minutes, 3.5 s of dead air is invisible — totally fine.

Name the trade-off: the timeout is a dial between fast failover and false positives. Shorten T to 0.5 s and you fail over in under a second — but now a healthy leader that pauses for a 600 ms garbage-collection or hits a network blip looks dead, triggers a needless election, comes back, gets re-elected, pauses again… that's leadership flapping — the exact rebalance storm from Lesson 09, wearing a crown. So T must sit comfortably above the worst benign pause you expect (think p99.9 of GC + network jitter, the same p99-sizing reflex from Lesson 07). Detect too eagerly and you manufacture the very instability you were guarding against.

Move 2 — Model: term, vote, and the majority

Every node keeps three facts: who it thinks the leader is, the current term, and (per term) who it has voted for. Two rules turn those into a working election:

  • One vote per node per term. Once node C votes for someone in term 2, it will not vote for anyone else in term 2.
  • You must win a majority to lead. A candidate becomes leader only after collecting votes from more than half the nodes — 3 of 5.

Those two rules, together, are the whole defense against split-brain — and the reason is pure counting, not magic. Hold that thought; we cash it out in Path B.

  ONE ELECTION (leader A has gone silent)

   term 1: A leader            B,C,D,E hear nothing for 3 s
                                       │
   C times out first ──► becomes CANDIDATE, term = 2, votes for itself (1)
        │  "RequestVote(term 2)"
        ├────────► B: haven't voted in term 2 → YES   (2)
        ├────────► D: haven't voted in term 2 → YES   (3)  ◄── majority reached
        └────────► E: (vote arrives later, doesn't matter)
   C has 3 of 5 ──► LEADER for term 2, epoch 2, starts heartbeating.

Move 3 — Trace the paths

Path A — a clean failover

Worked: the leader's machine dies. Leader A (term 1) loses power mid-month. Its heartbeats stop. After 3 s of silence, follower C times out first (its random delay was shortest), bumps the term to 2, and votes for itself. It asks the others; B and D haven't voted in term 2, so they say yes. C now has 3 votes — a majority — and becomes leader for term 2 with epoch 2. It resumes the billing job from where A's progress was committed. Total dead air: ~3.5 s. When A's machine reboots, it hears a heartbeat stamped term 2, sees that 2 > its own 1, and quietly steps down to follower. The higher term always wins.

Path B — the network splits, and why a majority saves us

This is the dangerous one. The cluster doesn't lose a machine — it loses a cable. The five nodes are torn into two groups that can each talk internally but not across the gap: {A, B} on one side, {C, D, E} on the other. Each side sees the other as "dead." Without the majority rule, both sides would elect a leader, both would run the billing job, and every customer would be charged twice.

  NETWORK PARTITION — the cable between the racks is cut

        side L (minority)            ╳            side R (majority)
     ┌─────┐   ┌─────┐          cut here        ┌─────┐ ┌─────┐ ┌─────┐
     │  A  │   │  B  │     ◄── can't reach ──►   │  C  │ │  D  │ │  E  │
     └─────┘   └─────┘                          └─────┘ └─────┘ └─────┘
       2 nodes  → needs 3 to lead                3 nodes → has a majority

   A (old leader) can reach only B = 2 votes. 2 < 3 → A must STEP DOWN.
   C asks D,E → 3 votes ≥ 3 → C becomes leader (term 2). Exactly ONE leader.

Worked: why two leaders is mathematically impossible. A leader needs 3 of 5 votes. Suppose, for contradiction, two different nodes each won. That's two voting groups of size ≥ 3 inside a set of 5 nodes. But 3 + 3 = 6 > 5, so by the pigeonhole principle the two groups must share at least one node. That shared node would have had to vote for both leaders in the same term — and rule one says each node votes at most once per term. Contradiction. Any two majorities of the same set always overlap, so at most one can exist per term. The minority side {A,B} simply can't reach 3, so it can't lead — it goes read-only and waits.

The cost, in dollars avoided: with 200,000 customers billed $5/month, a split-brain double-run would issue 200,000 × $5 = $1,000,000 of duplicate charges. The majority rule turns that into one leader and zero duplicates — by making the minority refuse to act.

Name the trade-off: a majority quorum buys safety (never two leaders) and pays with availability for the minority. The {A,B} side is alive and healthy but deliberately does nothing, because the only way to guarantee "at most one leader" is to make the side that can't prove it has a majority give up. You are choosing consistency over availability under a partition — which is exactly the CAP choice we open next lesson.

Path C — the zombie leader, and the fencing token

One nasty gap remains. Suppose A wasn't partitioned — it just froze (a long GC pause) for 4 s. While frozen, the others timed out and elected C (term 2, epoch 2). Now A unfreezes, still believing it is leader of term 1, and tries to write "billing run complete" to the database. A doesn't yet know it's been replaced. How do we stop this zombie leader from corrupting state?

The answer is a fencing token: every action the leader sends to a shared resource carries its epoch, and the resource remembers the highest epoch it has ever seen and rejects anything lower. C has already written with epoch 2, so the database's high-water mark is 2. A's stale write arrives stamped epoch 1; 1 < 2, so the database refuses it. The zombie is fenced out without the database needing to know anything about elections.

  FENCING — the resource rejects any action from an old reign

   C (epoch 2) writes ─────► DB:  highest epoch seen = 2   ✓ accept
   A (epoch 1, zombie) writes ─►  DB:  1 < 2  ✗ REJECT
                                  (A is fenced; no need to "kill" A)

Name the trade-off: heartbeats alone can detect a crashed leader, but they can't protect against a slow one that wakes up confused. Leases and timeouts reduce the window; the fencing token closes it for good — at the cost of threading an epoch number through every write and asking the resource to enforce it. Detection (heartbeats) tells you who should lead; fencing makes sure only that one's writes land.

Move 4 — Find the next bottleneck: why 5, why odd, and what you give up

The majority rule decides how many nodes you should run — and the answer is "an odd number," for a reason that's pure arithmetic. Fault tolerance = how many nodes can die while a majority is still reachable = N − quorum.

Nodes NQuorum ⌊N/2⌋+1Failures tolerated (N − quorum)
321
431 — same as 3, but costs a node
532
642 — same as 5, but costs a node
743

Read the bold rows: going from 5 to 6 nodes does not let you survive more failures — both tolerate 2 — but it raises the quorum to 4, so you now need more machines healthy to make any decision. An even N gives you the fault tolerance of the odd number below it, at higher cost and higher quorum. That's why coordination clusters run 3, 5, or 7 nodes — almost never 4 or 6. Five is the common sweet spot: tolerate two simultaneous failures, keep the quorum at a cheap 3.

And notice the wall the table runs into: under a partition, the minority side cannot make progress at all. It's alive, it's serving reads, but it refuses every write to preserve the one-leader guarantee. We deliberately traded that side's availability for safety. Whether that's the right trade — and what the other choice (stay available, give up single-truth) actually looks like — is the formal subject of the next lesson.

In practice you rarely hand-roll this. You lean on a coordination service — ZooKeeper, etcd, or Consul — which is itself a small odd-sized cluster running a consensus algorithm (Raft, Paxos) that bundles exactly what we traced: heartbeats, terms, majority votes, and a monotonic epoch you use as a fencing token. You ask it for a leader lease; it does the counting. Knowing what's inside is what lets you reason about it when it misbehaves.

Where we landed

We took one five-node cluster that must run a billing job exactly once and traced how it stays single-headed. Estimate sized the clock — 1 s heartbeat, 3 s timeout, ~3.5 s failover — and named the detection-speed-vs-false-positive dial that turns a too-short timeout into leadership flapping. Model gave us terms, one-vote-per-term, and the majority rule. Tracing the paths walked a clean failover, then the partition where a majority makes two leaders mathematically impossible (two groups of 3 in a set of 5 must overlap, and the shared node can't vote twice), then the zombie leader that a fencing token shuts out by epoch. The next bottleneck was sizing the cluster itself — odd N, because an even one buys no extra fault tolerance — and the wall behind it: the minority side gives up availability to keep safety. The thread through all of it: agreement among equals is just careful counting of votes, stamped with a term so the past can't outvote the present.
