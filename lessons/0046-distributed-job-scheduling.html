Distributed Job Scheduling: A Million Cron Jobs, Each Firing Once, On Time

Lesson 45 delivered events the instant they happened. This lesson delivers them on a clock — not "when the order ships" but "at 3:00 AM," "every hour on the hour," "on the 1st of the month." A scheduled job is a unit of work bound to a time: a subscription renewal (Lesson 25's billing), a nightly report, a five-minute health sweep, a "your trial ends tomorrow" reminder. Running one on a single machine is the humble cron line every developer knows. Running a million of them across a fleet — so that each one fires exactly once, at the right time, even when a machine dies mid-tick, even when the whole cluster was down at midnight — is a different animal, and it breaks on two words that sound like the same word but aren't: once and on time. We'll estimate why one scheduler box is both a bottleneck and a single point of failure, and why the innocent convention "run it at midnight" detonates into an ~8,600× load spike. We'll model the split every real scheduler builds — a dispatcher that decides what is due and a worker fleet that runs it (Lessons 5/9) — plus the lease and atomic claim that stop two nodes double-firing the same job (Lessons 10/22), and the idempotent job that survives the double-fire no lease can fully prevent (Lesson 13). We'll trace a clean fire, the double-fire race, and the crash-then-recover that resurrects missed jobs. And we'll hit the wall that defines the design: the midnight thundering herd, plus clock skew (Lesson 35) that makes "the right time" a thing no two machines agree on. The trade underneath: timeliness vs exactly-once execution.

The one system we'll design

Our platform is the same e-commerce/SaaS backend from Lessons 12/24/25/33/45. Customers and internal teams register jobs that must run on a schedule: renew a subscription, email a trial-ending notice, roll up yesterday's metrics (Lesson 44), sweep stale carts, retry a failed payout. Concretely: 1,000,000 registered jobs. Each has a schedule — a cron expression (0 3 * * * = 3 AM daily) or a one-shot "fire at time T." Across all of them they fire, on average, ~2,000,000 times/day → 2,000,000 ÷ 86,400 s ≈ 23 fires/second on average. Two properties define everything, and they pull in opposite directions. Once: a job that renews a subscription must fire exactly once — fire it twice and you double-charge a customer (Lesson 25), fire it zero times and their service lapses. On time: "3 AM" means 3 AM, not 3:04 when a slow box finally gets to it. A single cron daemon on one server gives you neither at scale. We'll build the system that gives you both — and see exactly where they fight.

  ONE SCHEDULER:  1,000,000 jobs  ->  ~23 fires/s average, but SPIKY.

  [ jobs table ]  id | schedule | next_fire_time | status | owner | lease_until
       |          (indexed on next_fire_time, L12 -- so "what's due?" is a
       |           tiny range read, not a 1,000,000-row scan every second)
       v
  +----------------------------------------------------------------------+
  |  DISPATCHER (sharded by hash(job_id), each shard has ONE live owner)  |
  |    every TICK (~1 s):  find jobs with next_fire_time <= now           |
  |      -> ATOMIC CLAIM each (lease it, L22) so no peer also fires it     |
  |      -> ENQUEUE a run job {job_id, scheduled_time} (durable, L05/09)   |
  |      -> compute + store the NEXT next_fire_time (reschedule)          |
  +----------------------------------------------------------------------+
       |  (dispatcher NEVER runs the job inline -- a slow job must not
       v   block the tick; it only decides + hands off)
  [ run queue ] --> [ WORKERS ] --run the job (idempotent, keyed on
                                   (job_id, scheduled_time), L13)--> effect
                                   ack -> mark that fire DONE

  Two words fight:  ONCE  (lease + atomic claim + idempotent job)
                    ON TIME (tick fast, shard for throughput, but the
                             midnight herd + clock skew cap precision)

A scheduled job is a unit of work bound to a time; a scheduler's job is to fire each one once, at (or near) that time, across a fleet where any single machine may die. Two words we'll lean on: the tick is the dispatcher's heartbeat — it wakes every second or so and asks "what's due now?"; next_fire_time is the stored timestamp of a job's next scheduled run, which the dispatcher advances every time it fires (a daily job's next_fire_time jumps 24 h forward). Firing a job means enqueuing it, not running it.

Words we'll earn before we lean on them

  • Scheduled job / cron — a unit of work bound to a schedule (a cron expression or a one-shot time), to be run when that time arrives.
  • Tick — the dispatcher's periodic wake-up (here ~1 s) where it looks for due jobs.
  • Dispatcher (scheduler) — the component that decides what is due and enqueues it; it does not execute the work.
  • Worker — a process that pulls a run job off the queue and actually executes it (Lessons 5/9).
  • Lease — a time-boxed claim on a job (or a shard of jobs), auto-renewed by heartbeat, so a dead owner's claim expires and another node can take over (Lesson 22).
  • Atomic claim — a compare-and-set that lets exactly one node mark a due job as "mine" (Lesson 6), so two dispatchers racing the same job don't both fire it.
  • Fencing token — a monotonically increasing number attached to a claim, so a resource can reject a write from a stale (paused) owner whose lease already expired (Lessons 10/22).
  • Idempotent job — a job whose second execution has no additional effect, keyed on (job_id, scheduled_time), so an unavoidable double-fire is harmless (Lesson 13).
  • Missed-fire catch-up — the policy for what to do with a job whose scheduled time passed while the cluster was down: coalesce (fire once) or backfill (fire each missed occurrence).
  • Schedule jitter — spreading jobs that would all fire at the same instant across a small window, to break the thundering herd (Lessons 7/26/45).

Move 1 — Estimate: why one cron box gives you neither "once" nor "on time"

The naive design is the one on every laptop: a single machine runs a cron daemon that wakes each second, checks which jobs are due, and runs them. Let's price why that box fails at a million jobs — on availability first, then on throughput, then on the spike that dwarfs both.

One box is a single point of failure (Lesson 7). If the scheduler machine is down from 23:59 to 00:11 — a deploy, a kernel panic, a bad disk — then every job scheduled for midnight simply never fires. There is no retry, because the thing that would issue the retry is the thing that's down. A missed webhook (Lesson 45) is a late delivery; a missed schedule is a fire that never happened — a subscription not renewed, a report not generated, and nobody notices until a customer does.

One box is a throughput ceiling. Suppose 200,000 of our million jobs are "daily," and — by the oldest convention in cron — their authors all wrote 0 0 * * *, i.e. midnight. So 200,000 jobs come due in the same one-second tick. Even if claiming and enqueuing one job costs a lean 0.5 ms of dispatcher work, firing them serially on one core takes 200,000 × 0.5 ms = 100 seconds. The jobs meant for 00:00:00 are still being dispatched at 00:01:40 — "on time" is already broken by two minutes, on the happy path, with no failure at all.

And the spike dwarfs the average by ~8,600×. The fleet averages ~23 fires/s, but that one midnight tick wants 200,000 ÷ 23 ≈ 8,600× the average rate in a single second — and then hands all 200,000 runs to the workers at once, who slam the billing DB, the email service, and every downstream in lockstep. This is Lesson 2's thundering herd and Lesson 7's load spike, born not from real traffic but from humans rounding to a nice number.

The fix is the course's oldest reflex, twice. For availability and throughput: don't run one scheduler — shard the schedule across N dispatchers and make each shard failover-able (Lessons 3/4/10). For the herd: don't run the job inline — the dispatcher only decides and enqueues; a separate worker fleet absorbs the burst behind a durable queue (Lessons 5/9/28).

Name the trade-off — a simple single scheduler vs a distributed one. One cron box is trivially correct about "once" (there's only one thing firing, so nothing can double-fire) and trivially wrong about availability and throughput. The moment you add a second machine for availability and throughput, you have reintroduced the double-fire problem — now two machines can both think a job is due. Distributing the scheduler is what buys "on time" at scale, and it is exactly what puts "exactly once" at risk. The rest of this lesson is spent buying "once" back.

Move 2 — Model: a sharded dispatcher, a lease that stops the double-fire, and a job that survives it anyway

Split the work, then make firing safe across machines.

Dispatcher and worker are different jobs — keep them apart (Lessons 5/9)

The dispatcher's tick must be fast and predictable: wake, find due jobs, claim them, enqueue them, advance their next_fire_time, sleep. If the dispatcher ran the jobs, a single slow job (a 40-second report) would stall the tick, and every other job scheduled for that second would fire late — one slow job poisoning the clock for all (Lesson 9's head-of-line blocking, Lesson 45's inline-delivery trap in a new costume). So the dispatcher only ever enqueues a small run job {job_id, scheduled_time} onto a durable queue; the worker fleet pulls and executes, scaling independently and soaking up the midnight burst (Lessons 27/28). The dispatcher owns time; the workers own work.

Shard the schedule so N dispatchers fire in parallel (Lessons 3/4)

Partition the jobs by hash(job_id) into, say, 16 shards, each owned by one live dispatcher. Now the midnight batch splits: 200,000 ÷ 16 = 12,500 jobs per shard, 12,500 × 0.5 ms = 6.25 s to dispatch instead of 100 s — a 16× cut, and if one dispatcher dies only its shard stalls, not the whole schedule. Each shard is owned via a lease (Lesson 22): the owner heartbeats to renew it; if it dies, the lease expires and a standby claims the shard (Lesson 10's failover, applied to a slice of the schedule). Consistent hashing (Lesson 4) means adding or removing a dispatcher reshuffles only ~1/N of shards, not all of them.

The atomic claim — the one line that stops the double-fire (Lessons 6/22)

Even sharded, two dispatchers can briefly overlap on a shard (a lease handoff, a network hiccup, a slow-clock standby that thinks the owner is dead). If both tick and both see job job_42 due, both must not fire it. The fix is a compare-and-set (Lesson 6) that only one can win:

Worked claim. Instead of "read due jobs, then fire them" (a check-then-act race, Lesson 13), the dispatcher conditionally claims each due job in one atomic step:

UPDATE jobs SET owner='disp-7', lease_until = now()+30s, epoch = epoch+1
 WHERE id='job_42' AND next_fire_time <= now() AND (owner IS NULL OR lease_until < now())

The database applies this to the row atomically, so of two dispatchers racing it, exactly one gets rows_affected = 1 (it fires) and the other gets 0 (it moves on). This is Lesson 22's lease as a single conditional write: the lease_until makes a crashed owner's claim expire so the job isn't stuck forever, and the epoch that ticks up on each claim is a fencing token (Lessons 10/22) — carried through to the run job so a downstream resource can reject a write from a stale owner.

Why not "just elect one global leader to fire everything"? A single leader (Lesson 10) removes the race but re-creates the throughput ceiling and the failover gap of the one cron box. Sharded leases give you one owner per shard — the concurrency of many boxes with the single-owner safety of one, per slice.

The job must be idempotent, because no lease makes double-fire impossible (Lesson 13)

Here is the hard truth the lease can't escape, and it's Lesson 22 verbatim. Dispatcher disp-7 claims job_42 with a 30-second lease, enqueues the run, and then stops-the-world for a 40-second GC pause (Lesson 22). At t+30s its lease expires; a standby legitimately reclaims job_42 and enqueues it again. Two run jobs now exist for the same fire. A finite lease is a liveness knob (it bounds how long a dead owner blocks a job), never a safety guarantee — a paused-but-alive owner can always overrun it. So the fire is at-least-once, and the only real defense is to make the job itself idempotent (Lesson 13): key every execution on (job_id, scheduled_time), record that key when the effect commits, and make a repeat a no-op. "Charge subscription 88 for the 2026-08-02 cycle" run twice charges once. Exactly-once scheduling attempts is impossible across a fleet; exactly-once effect is achievable — and it lives in the job, not the scheduler. This is precisely Lesson 45's lesson (at-least-once on the wire, idempotency at the destination) pointed at the clock.

Name the trade-off — lease length: false double-fire vs slow failover. A short lease detects a dead dispatcher fast (jobs recover quickly) but a live-but-slow owner overruns it more easily → more double-fires the idempotency layer must absorb, and more leadership flap (Lesson 10). A long lease rarely false-expires but leaves a genuinely dead owner's jobs frozen longer. You can't tune the double-fire to zero (the GC pause always wins), so you tune the lease for liveness and put correctness in idempotent jobs — the same division of labor as Lesson 22's fencing.

Move 3 — Trace the paths

One clean fire, one double-fire race, and the crash-then-recover that resurrects missed jobs.

Path A — a clean fire

  Job:  job_42  "renew subscription 88"  schedule 0 3 * * *  next_fire=03:00:00

   1. TICK at 03:00:00 on the shard that owns job_42. Range-read the index:
        WHERE next_fire_time <= now()   -> job_42 is in the due slice (L12).
   2. ATOMIC CLAIM (the UPDATE above): rows_affected=1 -> this dispatcher owns
        the fire. epoch bumped to 57 (the fencing token).
   3. ENQUEUE run job { job_id: job_42, scheduled_time: 03:00:00, epoch: 57 }.
        The dispatcher is now DONE with job_42 -- microseconds.
   4. RESCHEDULE: next_fire_time = tomorrow 03:00:00, status=scheduled.
   5. A worker pulls the run job. Idempotency check: has (job_42, 03:00:00)
        committed? No -> charge subscription 88 (L25), record the key in the
        SAME transaction as the charge (L13/33), ack. Fire DONE, exactly once.

Path B — two dispatchers race the same job

  A lease handoff leaves disp-7 and disp-9 both briefly owning the shard.
  Both tick at 03:00:00 and both see job_42 due.

   disp-7:  UPDATE ... WHERE id=job_42 AND lease_until rows_affected=1  (WINS, fires)
   disp-9:  UPDATE ... WHERE id=job_42 AND lease_until rows_affected=0  (LOSES, skips)

   The row can only be claimed once; the compare-and-set (L06) serializes the
   two writers at the database, so only ONE run job is enqueued. No double-fire.

   Compare to the GC-pause case (Path B'):
   disp-7 claims (lease 30s), enqueues, then PAUSES 40s. At t+30 lease expires,
   disp-9 reclaims + enqueues a SECOND run. Now two runs exist -> the WORKER's
   idempotency key (job_42, 03:00:00) makes the second a no-op (L13/22). The
   lease bounds liveness; the idempotent job guarantees the effect. Both needed.

Path C — the cluster was down at midnight (missed-fire catch-up)

  Whole scheduler cluster down 00:00 -> 00:11. At 00:11 it comes back and ticks.
  200,000 jobs now have next_fire_time in the PAST (they were due at 00:00).

  The tick sees them ALL as due at once. What should firing "10 minutes late"
  even mean?  It depends on the JOB -- two policies, chosen per job:

   COALESCE (fire once):   "send the daily digest email"
       -> firing 1 digest is right; firing "the one we missed" = fire once now
          (or skip to the next future slot). Do NOT send yesterday's + today's.

   BACKFILL (fire each):   "aggregate usage for hourly billing" (L25/44)
       -> each missed hour is a distinct period that MUST run, or a billing
          window is silently skipped = lost revenue. Fire every missed occurrence.

  Trap: 200,000 coalesced/backfilled jobs all firing at 00:11 = the midnight
  herd, again, on recovery (L07 metastable). So catch-up is rate-limited + jittered
  (L08/07), and idempotent (L13) so a retry of the catch-up itself is harmless.

Path A is the 99.99% we design around; Path B is the concurrency safety (atomic claim + idempotent job) that earns the word "once"; Path C is the one people forget until an outage — a scheduler that can't answer "what about the fires I missed while I was down?" silently loses work, and whether the right answer is "fire once" or "fire each" is a property of the job, not a global switch. Every piece works as long as the fires that all pile onto the same instant don't crush the system. That "as long as" is the wall.

Move 4 — Find the first bottleneck: the midnight herd, and the clock nobody agrees on

We sharded for throughput and leased for safety. But the load isn't spread over time — it's spiked onto the round numbers humans choose, and the "time" every node compares against isn't the same time.

The herd, quantified. 200,000 jobs at 0 0 * * * land in one tick. Sharding to 16 dispatchers cuts dispatch time to ~6.25 s, but all 200,000 runs still hit the workers and their downstreams (billing DB, email, Lesson 25's ledger) in a few seconds — 8,600× the average rate, straight into Lesson 27's utilization knee and Lesson 7's retry storm. Adding dispatchers doesn't help: the spike is in the schedule, not the scheduler.

The fix is jitter — spread the fire times (Lessons 7/26/45). Treat "midnight" as "somewhere in the first minute after midnight": assign each of the 200,000 jobs a deterministic offset (e.g. hash(job_id) mod 60 s). Now they fire at 200,000 ÷ 60 ≈ 3,333/s — a smooth, sustainable rate instead of an 8,600× wall. For jobs that don't truly need second-precision (a nightly report doesn't care if it starts at 00:00:07), this is free. The queue (Lesson 28) then absorbs whatever burst remains, and the workers autoscale into it (Lesson 27).

Name the trade-off — timeliness precision vs smooth load. Firing exactly at 00:00:00.000 is what caused the herd; spreading the same jobs over 60 seconds smooths the load but means a given job fires up to a minute "late." Almost no scheduled job actually needs sub-minute precision — but the few that do (a market-open trade, a coordinated cutover) can't be jittered, so those you must provision for the spike, not spread it away. The default is jitter; precision is the exception you pay real capacity for.

The other wall: clock skew — whose "now" is now? (Lesson 35)

Every claim asks next_fire_time <= now() — but now is measured by a wall clock, and no two machines' wall clocks agree (Lesson 35). If disp-7's clock runs 500 ms ahead, it fires jobs 500 ms early; a standby whose clock lags may think the owner's lease hasn't expired when it has, delaying failover. Two disciplines keep skew from corrupting "once" and "on time":

• Compare against one clock, not each node's own (Lesson 35). Condition the claim on the datastore's now(), or a coordination service's time — so all dispatchers evaluate "is it due?" and "is the lease expired?" against a single authority, not their private wall clocks. This is Lesson 35's "don't sort by local wall-clock time" applied to firing.

• Don't promise precision finer than your skew. NTP pins clocks to roughly a few milliseconds to tens of ms (Lesson 35); a 1-second tick already can't distinguish sub-second times honestly. So set the tick granularity and your SLA at or above the skew bound — promising "fires within ~1 s of schedule" is truthful; promising "fires at exactly T" across a fleet is a lie the physics won't keep.

• Leases must expire on real elapsed time, not logical order. A fencing token (Lesson 22) orders claims without a clock, but the lease timeout itself is a physical duration — so it needs a real, if approximate, clock. Logical clocks order events; they don't tell you when 30 seconds have passed (Lesson 35's closing caution).

The four traps

  • Trap 1 — one scheduler, or running jobs inline in the tick. A single cron box is a SPOF (a midnight outage silently drops every midnight job) and a throughput ceiling (200k serial fires = 100 s late); running the job inside the tick lets one slow job stall the clock for all. Shard the dispatcher for availability + throughput (Lessons 3/4/10), and hand work to a separate queue + worker fleet (Lessons 5/9).
  • Trap 2 — assuming a lease gives exactly-once firing. A GC pause or a network partition always lets a paused owner overrun its lease, so two nodes can fire the same job; a lease is a liveness bound, not a safety guarantee. Make the job idempotent, keyed on (job_id, scheduled_time), so the unavoidable double-fire is a no-op (Lessons 13/22), and carry a fencing token for resources that need it.
  • Trap 3 — no missed-fire policy. After an outage, jobs whose time passed are either silently dropped (work lost) or all replayed at once (herd on recovery). Decide per job whether to coalesce (one digest, not ten) or backfill (every missed billing hour), and rate-limit + jitter the catch-up so recovery isn't a second stampede (Lessons 7/8).
  • Trap 4 — the midnight herd and trusting local clocks. Humans round schedules to 0 0, concentrating a day's fires into one instant (~8,600× the average); and each node's wall clock disagrees on when that instant is. Jitter fire times over a window (Lessons 7/26), and evaluate due-ness and lease expiry against one authoritative clock, never each node's own (Lesson 35).

Where we landed

A distributed scheduler firing 1,000,000 jobs (~23 fires/s average, wildly spiky) so each runs once, on time, across a fleet where any node may die. Estimate killed the single cron box three ways: it's a SPOF (a midnight outage drops every midnight job with no retry, because the retrier is what's down), a throughput ceiling (200,000 × 0.5 ms = 100 s to dispatch the midnight batch serially → two minutes late on the happy path), and the herd — 200,000 jobs written 0 0 * * * come due in one tick, 8,600× the average rate — so shard the dispatcher for availability and throughput (Lessons 3/4/10) and hand work to a queue + worker fleet so the tick never runs a job inline (Lessons 5/9/28). Model gave firing safety across machines: a dispatcher that only decides-and-enqueues, sharded by hash(job_id) with a per-shard lease for failover (Lesson 22), an atomic claim (compare-and-set, Lesson 6) so two racing dispatchers enqueue one run not two, and — because a GC pause always lets a paused owner overrun its 30 s lease — an idempotent job keyed on (job_id, scheduled_time) that makes the unavoidable double-fire a no-op (Lessons 13/22): the lease bounds liveness, the idempotent job guarantees the effect. Tracing showed the clean one-fire path, the double-fire race (the DB serializes the claim → one run) versus the GC-pause double-fire (two runs → idempotency saves it), and the crash-then-recover where 200,000 overdue jobs force a per-job choice between coalesce (fire once) and backfill (fire each missed occurrence). The first bottleneck is the herd the schedule itself creates — fixed by jitter (spread 200,000 midnight fires over 60 s → ~3,333/s instead of an 8,600× wall, Lessons 7/26) — and the clock skew that makes "now" disagree across nodes, fixed by comparing due-ness and lease expiry against one authoritative clock and refusing to promise precision finer than the skew (Lesson 35). The real lesson: binding work to a clock across a fleet splits into two words that fight — "on time" forces you to distribute (which reintroduces the double-fire), and "once" forces you to lease and to make the job idempotent (because no lease survives a pause). You can't have exactly-once attempts; you engineer exactly-once effect, spread the herd the calendar creates, and tell the truth about a precision the clocks can't keep. The standing tension: timeliness vs exactly-once execution.
