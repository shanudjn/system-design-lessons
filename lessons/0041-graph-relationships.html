Graph & Relationship Systems: Traversing a Billion-Edge Graph

Every table we've built holds rows — an order, a tenant, a photo — and the questions were about one row at a time: fetch it, count it, cache it. This lesson is about the relationships between rows as the thing you're actually querying. Take a social network: 1 billion people, each with about 200 friends. The question isn't "fetch user X" — it's "who are my friends' friends?" — the people two hops away, the pool every "People You May Know" suggestion is drawn from. That one innocent question is where the row-at-a-time database we've leaned on for 40 lessons falls off a cliff. We'll estimate why a relational JOIN explodes as the question reaches deeper — each hop multiplying the work by 200, so three hops is 8 million lookups and five hops is the entire planet (Lesson 12's N+1, now compounding). We'll model the fix — an adjacency list and a native graph store whose index-free adjacency makes "get my neighbors" a pointer-follow whose cost doesn't grow with the graph. We'll trace a two-hop traversal both ways and watch the difference. And we'll hit the wall that makes graphs genuinely hard: you cannot cut a well-connected graph into shards without cutting almost every edge, so the moment the graph outgrows one machine, each cheap local pointer-follow becomes a network round trip — and the supernode (a celebrity with 100M followers) recurs as the hot shard we first met in Lesson 15. The trade running underneath: traversal speed vs the partitionability of connected data.

The one system we'll design

A social graph. 1 billion users (we'll call them nodes), each with an average of 200 friends. Friendship is mutual, so we store it as two directed edges (A→B and B→A) to make "who are A's friends?" and "who are B's friends?" both a single lookup. The one query we must answer fast, millions of times a second, is the friend-of-friend (FoF) query: starting from you, walk out two hops and return the distinct people you're not yet friends with. That set powers friend suggestions, "3 mutual friends" badges, and reach estimates. Simple to ask. Brutal to serve at a billion nodes.

  A SOCIAL GRAPH  (nodes = people, edges = friendships)  --  "friends of friends?"

        me
       / | \                 hop 0:  1 node      (me)
      A  B  C   ...           hop 1:  200 nodes   (my friends)          <- easy
     /|  |  |\                hop 2:  ~40,000     (friends of friends)  <- the query
    ...........               hop 3:  ~8,000,000  (friends^3)
   .............              hop 4:  ~1.6 billion (> whole graph)
  ...............            hop 5:  everyone     ("six degrees")

  Each hop multiplies the frontier by the average degree d = 200.
  The graph has 1e9 nodes but only ~5 hops SEPARATE ANY TWO PEOPLE.
  That tiny diameter is exactly why deep traversal explodes: the answer
  itself grows 200x per hop until it swallows the whole graph.

A graph is data whose value is in the connections: nodes joined by edges. A traversal walks edges from a starting node to reach others; a hop is one edge crossed; degree is how many edges a node has. The whole difficulty of this lesson is that a graph's answers grow with the number of paths, and paths multiply at every hop — while the storage engines we've used are built to fetch rows, one indexed lookup at a time.

Words we'll earn before we lean on them

  • Node (vertex) — one entity in the graph: here, one person. Identified by a user_id.
  • Edge — a relationship between two nodes: here, a friendship. Directed means it has a from/to (A→B); an undirected friendship is stored as two directed edges.
  • Degree — how many edges a node has. Average degree d = 200 here; a supernode is a node with a huge degree (a celebrity with millions of followers).
  • Hop — one edge crossed during a walk. "Friends" are 1 hop away; "friends of friends" are 2 hops.
  • Traversal — walking edges from a start node to visit others. BFS (breadth-first search) visits all 1-hop nodes, then all 2-hop, and so on — the natural shape of a FoF query.
  • Adjacency list — for each node, the list of its neighbors' ids stored together. The opposite of scattering edges across a big shared table you must index into.
  • Index-free adjacency — a node's record points directly at its neighbors' records, so "get my neighbors" follows pointers instead of searching a global index. The defining trick of a native graph store.
  • Cross-shard edge — an edge whose two endpoints live on different machines, so following it costs a network round trip instead of a memory read.

Move 1 — Estimate: why the relational JOIN explodes at depth

Model friendships the obvious way — one table, two columns, indexed on the first: friendships(user_id, friend_id). Friendship is mutual and stored both ways, so the row count is 1e9 users × 200 friends = 2 × 10¹¹ — 200 billion rows. That alone is a warning (at ~16 bytes per edge that's ~3.2 TB just for the edges — it will not fit one machine, which is Move 3's problem). But the real trouble is the shape of the query.

Hop 1 — my friends. SELECT friend_id FROM friendships WHERE user_id = ME. One index seek into the 200-billion-row table, returns 200 rows. Cheap — this is the row-at-a-time lookup every earlier lesson optimized. If it misses cache and hits SSD, ~100 µs. No problem.

Hop 2 — friends of friends. Now self-join the table to itself:

SELECT f2.friend_id
FROM friendships f1
JOIN friendships f2 ON f1.friend_id = f2.user_id
WHERE f1.user_id = ME

To resolve it, the database takes each of my 200 friends and does one index seek per friend to fetch their 200 friends: 200 index seeks → 200 × 200 = 40,000 rows. This is Lesson 12's N+1 — one query fanning into N per-row lookups — except the N is now a whole hop of the graph. At ~100 µs per seek that's 200 × 100 µs = 20 ms. Still survivable.

Hop 3 — and here it detonates. Each of those 40,000 rows needs its own index seek to reach its friends: 40,000 index seeks → 8,000,000 rows. At ~100 µs each that's 40,000 × 100 µs = 4 seconds — for one query. Hop 4 would be 8,000,000 seeks → 1.6 billion rows → ~13 minutes. Every hop multiplies both the row count and the number of index seeks by the average degree, d = 200. The JOIN's cost is d^k — geometric in depth.

Name the trade-off — the relational model optimizes the wrong axis for connected data. A B-tree index makes finding one row among 200 billion cheap: log₂(2×10¹¹) ≈ 38 comparisons. That is exactly the right tool for "fetch order #12345." But a traversal isn't one lookup — it's d^k lookups, and it re-pays that log N index descent on every single one, at every hop. The relational engine is built to jump to a row by key; a graph question is built out of following edges, and the JOIN turns each edge-follow into a fresh global index search. The cost you want a traversal to pay is "proportional to the answer"; the JOIN makes you pay "answer × the size of the whole table, per hop." That mismatch is the entire reason graph stores exist.

Move 2 — Model: adjacency lists and index-free adjacency

Two ideas fix the mismatch. The first — the adjacency list — changes how edges are stored. The second — index-free adjacency — changes how you reach a neighbor.

Adjacency list: keep a node's neighbors together

Instead of scattering 200 billion edges across one giant table you must index into, store with each node the list of its neighbor ids: me → [A, B, C, …] (200 ids). "Get my friends" becomes "read my node's list" — a single sequential read of one record, not an index seek. In a key-value store (Lesson 3) this is one GET by user_id. The 40,000-row hop-2 query becomes: read my list (200 ids), then read 200 lists (one per friend). Still 201 reads — but each is a direct key lookup, not a self-join, and the ids for a hop arrive already grouped.

Index-free adjacency: point straight at the neighbor

A native graph store (Neo4j, and the spirit of Facebook's TAO) goes one step further. Each node's record holds direct pointers to its neighbors' records — physical references, not ids you must look up. Following an edge is dereferencing a pointer: O(1), a memory-or-disk read at a known address. Crucially, the cost of one hop no longer depends on how big the graph is.

  TWO WAYS TO CROSS ONE EDGE  (from me to a friend)

  RELATIONAL JOIN                          INDEX-FREE ADJACENCY
  ----------------------------------       ----------------------------------
  friend_id = ?                            my node record
      |  search a B-tree over                  |  holds a direct pointer
      v  200,000,000,000 rows                  v  to the friend's record
  ~38 comparisons + maybe random I/O       follow pointer -> arrive. O(1).
  COST PER HOP GROWS with graph size       COST PER HOP is CONSTANT
  (log N), paid d^k times                  (independent of N), paid d^k times

  Double the graph to 2 billion users:
    JOIN  -> every one of the d^k seeks gets slower (log N grew)
    graph -> a 2-hop query runs at EXACTLY the same speed (N doesn't appear)

Both still visit d^k nodes at depth k — that fan-out is the answer's own size and no store can dodge it. What index-free adjacency removes is the per-graph-size tax: the log N you paid on every seek, every hop. For a shallow query (1–2 hops) on a graph that fits in one machine's memory, that turns a 20 ms self-join into a sub-millisecond pointer-chase.

Name the trade-off — a graph store buys traversal speed by giving up the general index. A relational table lets you ask any question — filter by any column, aggregate, join arbitrary tables — because everything is indexed and set-based. A native graph store is specialized: it is superb at "follow edges from here" and mediocre at "sum every order in Europe last Tuesday" (Lesson 29's job). You trade the generality of the relational model for one thing done exceptionally well — walking relationships — and you pay for it whenever the question isn't a traversal. That's why real systems keep both: the graph store for connections, a relational or columnar store for everything else, kept in sync by Lesson 33's change stream.

Move 3 — Trace the paths

Three runs: the two-hop FoF on one machine (the good case the graph store was built for), the same query once the graph is sharded across many machines (where it falls apart), and the query when it passes through a celebrity supernode (the fan-out bomb).

Path A — friends-of-friends on ONE machine (graph fits in RAM)

  FoF(me), 2 hops, index-free adjacency, whole graph in one machine's memory

   1. Read my node -> follow 200 neighbor pointers        -> 200 friends (hop 1)
   2. For each friend, follow ITS neighbor pointers       -> 40,000 ids (hop 2)
   3. Dedup, drop people I'm already friends with, rank by #mutuals
   4. Return top suggestions.  ~40,200 pointer-follows in memory -> single-digit ms.

  No index seeks, no JOINs, no network. Cost tracks the ANSWER size (40k),
  not the GRAPH size (200 billion edges). This is the whole point of a graph store.

Path B — the SAME query, graph sharded across 100 machines

200 billion edges (~3.2 TB) don't fit one machine, so we shard nodes by hash(user_id) across, say, 100 machines — each holds ~1% of the nodes. Now the trouble: an edge connects two nodes that were hashed independently, so they usually land on different shards.

How many edges get cut? With random assignment across P = 100 shards, the chance both endpoints of an edge land on the same shard is 1/P = 1/100. So 99% of edges are cross-shard — following them means a network round trip, not a memory read. A random partition of a well-connected graph cuts nearly every edge.

What that does to the FoF query. Hop 1 fans to 200 friends spread across ~100 shards. Hop 2 must expand each of those 40,000 frontier nodes where it lives — so the traversal bounces machine-to-machine, ~40,000 cross-shard steps. Even batched per shard, that's a scatter-gather across the whole cluster with a network hop (~0.5 ms each, even in one datacenter) on the tail latency (Lesson 27), and the query's speed is now set by the slowest shard it must wait for (Lesson 7's fan-out fragility). The elegant in-memory pointer-chase of Path A became a distributed storm.

  WHY YOU CAN'T JUST SHARD A GRAPH  (hash(user_id) -> 100 machines)

     shard 1        shard 2        shard 3   ...   shard 100
     [me, X]        [A, C]         [B, ...]        [...]
        \___edge___/  \___edge____/                       every edge that
         cross-shard   cross-shard                        crosses = 1 network hop

   Random partition: P(both ends same shard) = 1/100  ->  ~99% of edges CUT.
   A 2-hop walk of 40,000 nodes crosses shard boundaries ~40,000 times.
   Local pointer-follow (nanoseconds) -> network round trip (~0.5 ms). 10^4x slower.

Path C — the query passes through a celebrity (supernode)

  FoF where one of my friends is a celebrity with 100,000,000 followers

   1. Hop 1: 200 friends -- one of them is @celebrity.
   2. Hop 2: expanding @celebrity alone fans out to 100,000,000 nodes.
      -> the frontier is no longer ~40,000; it's ~100 MILLION, from ONE edge.
   3. @celebrity's node is a HOT SHARD (Lesson 3): every FoF query touching a fan
      hits it, and its adjacency list is 100M entries -- it can't sit "with the node"
      cheaply, and it can't be traversed through without exploding the walk.

  This is Lesson 15's fan-out problem, reincarnated as graph structure. The average
  degree is 200; the MAX degree is 100 million. The average is a lie (Lesson 2's skew).

Path A is why a graph store is magical; Path B is the wall that makes graphs the hardest thing to scale in this course; Path C is the skewed-degree monster that makes Path B worse. Look again at Path B: the whole cost came from cut edges. So the design question becomes — can we partition the graph so that most edges stay inside a shard? That's the first bottleneck.

Move 4 — Find the first bottleneck: you can't partition connected data cleanly

Every scaling tool in this course worked by cutting data into independent pieces: shard the URL table (Lesson 3), the tenants (Lesson 40), the object store (Lesson 20). It worked because those rows were independent — splitting them lost nothing. A graph is the opposite: its value is the edges, and an edge is a dependency between two rows. Cut the graph and you cut the very thing you're trying to query. There is no partition of a well-connected graph that keeps pieces both balanced (equal size) and rarely cut (few cross-shard edges) — finding the best one is a known hard problem (balanced graph partitioning is NP-hard). So you don't get a clean answer; you buy the best imperfect one. Three moves, each naming what it trades:

  FOUR WAYS TO FIGHT THE PARTITION WALL

  1. REPLICATE instead of partition.  Keep the whole graph (or just the edge lists) in
        RAM on every server, so every hop is local -- no cut edges at all (Path A, cloned).
        200B edges ~ 3.2 TB: too big for one box's RAM, but the HOT working set (recent,
        active users' adjacency) often fits. Facebook's TAO does this: a graph-shaped cache
        over the edges, read-mostly, in memory. Trade: fast local traversal vs RAM cost &
        write-propagation to every replica (Lesson 6's replication lag returns).

  2. PARTITION SMARTLY, not randomly.  Social graphs have COMMUNITY structure -- people
        cluster by geography, school, workplace (triadic closure, Lesson 15). Co-locate a
        community on one shard and most of its edges stay LOCAL. Cross-shard edges drop from
        ~99% toward, say, ~30%. Trade: far fewer cut edges vs an expensive, NP-hard partition
        that must be recomputed as the graph changes (a moving target).

  3. VERTEX-CUT the supernodes (Path C).  Don't force a celebrity onto one shard (a hot spot
        no bulkhead fixes). SPLIT the high-degree node: replicate it across shards and cut its
        EDGES among them instead of cutting the node's neighbors off from it. Handles the 100M-
        degree monster the way Lesson 15 handled celebrities -- special-case the few, not the many.

  4. DON'T TRAVERSE DEEP AT READ TIME.  d^k is the answer's own size; no store escapes it past
        ~3 hops. PRECOMPUTE what you can (materialize "mutual friends counts" like Lesson 15's
        fanned-out feed), and CAP depth (2 hops for suggestions, never 5). Trade: fresh answers
        vs precomputed staleness -- the same read-vs-write choice from Lesson 15, on a graph.

Name the trade-off — traversal speed vs partitionability. The two things you want are in direct tension. Fast traversal wants every edge to be a local pointer-follow — which wants the whole graph on one machine (Path A), which doesn't scale past one machine's RAM. Horizontal scale wants the graph cut across many machines — which cuts edges, which turns local follows into network hops (Path B), which destroys traversal speed. You cannot have both cheaply: co-location gives speed but caps size; partitioning gives size but cuts the edges that made traversal fast. Every graph system is a chosen point on that line — replicate the hot subgraph, partition the cold bulk by community, special-case the supernodes, and precompute the deep answers. That's why graphs are the last hard problem: the tool that scaled everything else — cut into independent shards — is the one tool a graph refuses.

The four traps

  • Trap 1 — serving deep traversals with relational JOINs. A self-join costs d^k index seeks and re-pays the log N B-tree descent on each — 4 seconds at hop 3, minutes at hop 4. Fine for 1 hop ("my friends"); a cliff beyond it. Use an adjacency list / graph store whose per-hop cost is O(1), not O(log N).
  • Trap 2 — sharding a graph like a table. hash(user_id) across P shards cuts ~(P-1)/P of the edges, so every traversal becomes a cluster-wide network storm. Cut by community to keep edges local, or replicate the hot subgraph so hops stay in-process.
  • Trap 3 — trusting the average degree. "Average 200 friends" hides a celebrity with 100M — a supernode that is a hot shard, a fan-out bomb, and an adjacency list that won't fit the model. Degree is Zipf (Lesson 2); special-case the whales with vertex-cuts, exactly as Lesson 15 special-cased celebrity fan-out.
  • Trap 4 — traversing arbitrarily deep at read time. Because the frontier grows 200× per hop, an uncapped traversal reaches the whole graph in ~5 hops and never returns. Cap the depth (2 hops for suggestions) and precompute what you can — a graph store makes hops cheap, not free, and it can never beat the answer's own d^k size.

Where we landed

A graph is data whose value lives in its edges, and the row-at-a-time database we spent 40 lessons tuning is built for the opposite. We took one social graph — 1 billion nodes, ~200 friends each, 200 billion edges — and answered one question, "who are my friends' friends?" Estimate showed why the relational JOIN detonates: a self-join does d^k index seeks and re-pays the log N descent on every one, so 1 hop is trivial, 2 hops is Lesson 12's N+1 (~20 ms), 3 hops is 8M seeks (~4 s), and 5 hops is the entire planet — each hop multiplies the work by d = 200. Model gave the two fixes: the adjacency list (a node's neighbors stored together, so "get my friends" is one read, not a join) and index-free adjacency (a node points straight at its neighbors, so one hop is O(1) and — the key win — its cost is independent of graph size, trading the relational model's generality for one thing done superbly). Tracing showed the magic and the wall: on one machine the 40,000-node FoF is a sub-millisecond in-memory pointer-chase, but 3.2 TB of edges won't fit one machine, and sharding by hash(user_id) across 100 shards cuts ~99% of edges, turning every hop into a network round trip — and a celebrity supernode (100M degree) fans one edge out to 100 million nodes and becomes a hot shard, Lesson 15 and Lesson 3 reincarnated as graph structure. The first bottleneck is the deepest one in the course: you cannot cut connected data into independent shards — an edge is a dependency — so you buy the best imperfect partition: replicate the hot subgraph (TAO), partition by community not by hash (fewer cut edges, at the cost of an NP-hard, ever-changing layout), vertex-cut the supernodes, and precompute + cap depth because d^k is the answer's own size and no store escapes it. The real lesson: the one tool that scaled everything else — split into independent pieces — is the tool a graph refuses, so graph systems live entirely on the line between traversal speed and partitionability of connected data.
