Distributed Transactions & Sagas: Making Many Services Commit All-or-Nothing Without a Shared Transaction

Lesson 31 taught us to ship one service safely. But a single thing a user asks for often spans several services. "Book my trip" means reserve a flight, reserve a hotel, reserve a car, and charge the card — and the rule is all or none: a customer must never end up with a hotel but no flight, or a charge but no booking. Inside one database that's a solved problem — BEGIN … COMMIT gives you atomicity for free. But the flight, the hotel, the car, and the payment each live in their own database, owned by their own team, and a database transaction stops at the edge of its own database. There is no BEGIN that wraps four separate databases. So the atomicity we got for free inside one box, we now have to build across four. This lesson takes one trip-booking and traces two ways to build it: the two-phase commit that tries to hold all four in lockstep (and can freeze them all), and the saga that commits each step for real and undoes the earlier ones when a later one fails. We'll estimate what holding a cross-service lock costs, model 2PC vs saga, trace a happy booking and a failed one through compensating transactions, and hit the wall that defines the domain: a saga has no isolation — its half-finished state is really visible to everyone else. The trade shifts from Lesson 31's release-velocity to atomicity vs availability across service boundaries.

The one system we'll design

A travel platform books 2,000 trips/second at peak. One "trip" is four steps across four independent services, each with its own database:

  • Flight service — reserve a seat ($400).
  • Hotel service — reserve a room ($600).
  • Car service — reserve a car ($150).
  • Payment service — charge the card ($1,150 total).

The requirement is atomicity: the four steps commit all-or-nothing. If the car is sold out, the flight and hotel reservations must not stick, and the card must not be charged. Each service owns its data (the database-per-service rule — teams deploy and scale independently, Lesson 31), so no single transaction can span them. First, the words.

A transaction is a single-database feature. BEGIN … COMMIT gives you ACID: Atomic (all-or-nothing), Consistent, Isolated (concurrent transactions don't see each other's half-done state), Durable. All four guarantees are enforced by one database's lock manager and log. The moment your data lives in four databases, there is no shared lock manager and no shared log — so atomicity across them is not something you have, it's something you construct. This whole lesson is two ways to construct it, and what each one gives up.

  ONE USER ACTION, FOUR SERVICES, NO SHARED TRANSACTION

     "Book my trip"  =  reserve FLIGHT + reserve HOTEL + reserve CAR + CHARGE card
                         (own DB)       (own DB)        (own DB)      (own DB)

  TWO WAYS TO MAKE IT ALL-OR-NONE
  ------------------------------------------------------------------------------
  TWO-PHASE COMMIT (2PC)                    SAGA
   one COORDINATOR, lockstep                 do each step for real, undo on failure

   Phase 1 PREPARE: everyone locks & votes    reserve flight  -> COMMIT (lock freed)
     flight  YES   hotel  YES                 reserve hotel   -> COMMIT (lock freed)
     car     YES   pay    YES                 reserve car     -> FAILS (sold out)

The split is the same trade you've seen since Lesson 11. 2PC keeps everyone consistent by making them all wait together — and if the coordinator dies at the wrong instant, "wait together" becomes "freeze together." A saga keeps everyone available by letting each step finish on its own — and pays for it by exposing a window where the trip is half-booked. Atomicity vs availability, now across service boundaries.

Words we'll earn before we lean on them

  • Distributed transaction — one logical all-or-nothing unit of work spanning more than one database/service.
  • Two-phase commit (2PC) — a coordinator asks every participant to prepare (lock + vote), then, only if all vote yes, tells them all to commit. One "no" (or timeout) aborts everyone.
  • In-doubt / blocking — a participant that voted yes but hasn't heard the final decision must hold its locks and wait; if the coordinator is gone, it waits indefinitely.
  • Saga — a sequence of local transactions, each committing on its own; if step N fails, run a compensating transaction for every earlier step, in reverse.
  • Compensating transaction — a new action that semantically undoes a committed step (cancel the reservation, refund the charge). It is a business undo, not a rollback — the original effect really happened.
  • Orchestration vs choreography — a central saga orchestrator drives the steps, or each service reacts to events the previous one emitted (Lesson 9's pub/sub) with no central driver.
  • Pivot transaction — the step after which the saga can only go forward. Steps before it are compensatable (undoable); steps after it must be retriable (they can't be undone, so you keep retrying until they succeed).
  • Semantic lock — marking half-done state as "pending / in a saga" (seat = HELD, not SOLD) so other readers know it isn't a settled fact yet — the saga's substitute for the isolation it gave up.

Move 1 — Estimate: what a cross-service lock actually costs

The instinct is: "just take a lock across all four services until everything succeeds, then release." That is two-phase commit. Price it on two axes — throughput on a contended row, and availability of the whole commit — because both are worse than they look.

Worked: how long each service holds its lock. In 2PC a participant locks its row at prepare and can't release until the coordinator's final commit arrives — that's a full extra network round trip plus the coordinator's durable logging away. Call the 2PC window ~50 ms (prepare RTT + coordinator decision & fsync + commit RTT). A plain local transaction on that same row commits in ~5 ms and releases immediately. So under 2PC every contended row is held 50 / 5 = 10× longer.

On a hot resource — say one popular flight everybody wants during a sale — that row is a serial gate (Lesson 22): 2PC lets it commit 1 / 0.050 s = 20 bookings/sec; committing locally instead frees the lock after 5 ms → 1 / 0.005 s = 200/sec. Reaching across the network while holding a lock costs 10× the throughput of the hottest row in the system.

Worked: the availability of "all four at once." A synchronous 2PC can only commit if every participant is up at the same instant. Suppose each of the four services is a healthy 99.9% ("three nines"). The compound commit's availability is the product: 0.999⁴ ≈ 0.9960 = 99.6% — worse than any single service, and (1 − 0.996) × 8,760 h ≈ 35 h/yr where a trip cannot be booked even though each service is individually fine. (Three participants alone already give 0.999³ ≈ 99.7% ≈ 26 h/yr — the more services you rope into one synchronous commit, the lower the ceiling.)

And the darker failure: if the coordinator crashes after everyone voted yes but before it sends "commit," every participant is in-doubt — holding its locks, unable to decide alone — until the coordinator comes back. If recovery takes 5 minutes (Lesson 31's TTR), those hot inventory rows are frozen for 5 minutes from a single crash. That's not slowness; it's Lesson 10's coordinator failure with a worse blast radius — the coordinator here holds everyone's locks.

Name the trade-off — atomicity vs availability across service boundaries. 2PC buys you real atomicity and isolation across services — genuinely all-or-nothing, and nobody sees the half-done state. You pay in exactly the CAP currency of Lesson 11: to stay consistent it must block, so its throughput ceiling on hot rows is 10× lower and its availability is the product of every participant's — and a coordinator crash freezes the lot. 2PC is the CP choice wearing a transaction's clothes. The saga is the AP answer: never hold a cross-service lock, stay available, and repair the inconsistency afterward with a compensating action.

Move 2 — Model: 2PC vs the saga

The estimate says holding locks across the network is the whole cost. The saga's core idea is to never do it: commit each step locally and immediately, and if a later step fails, issue a new transaction that undoes the earlier ones.

Two-phase commit — real atomicity, at the price of blocking

A coordinator runs two rounds. Prepare: ask all four participants to lock their rows and promise they can commit; each votes yes/no and — crucially — stays locked and ready. Commit: if all voted yes, tell everyone to commit; a single no (or a timeout) makes everyone abort. It delivers true ACID across services: the customer never sees a half-booked trip. But every participant holds locks for the whole two-round window (the 10× tax), the commit needs everyone up at once (the availability product), and a coordinator failure in the gap between the rounds leaves participants in-doubt and frozen. 2PC is the right tool inside a small, trusted, low-contention boundary — say two databases you own, updated rarely. It is the wrong tool across autonomous, high-throughput services, which is exactly our trip booking.

Saga — stay available, undo instead of hold

Model the trip as an ordered list of local transactions, each paired with a compensation:

Step (forward)Compensation (backward)Kind
reserve flight seatcancel flight seatcompensatable
reserve hotel roomcancel hotel roomcompensatable
reserve carcancel carcompensatable
charge card $1,150— (this is the pivot) —pivot
issue tickets / confirm— (must succeed) —retriable

Each step commits for real the instant it runs, so no lock is ever held across the network — the 10× tax and the availability-product both vanish. If any compensatable step fails, the saga walks backward, running the compensation for each already-committed step. The order is deliberate: everything before the pivot must be undoable (cancel the reservation — the hold is released), and everything after the pivot must be retriable (once the card is charged we don't refund-and-abort; we push forward, retrying "issue tickets" until it works). You place the hard-to-undo step (the charge) as the pivot so you never have to un-charge a customer.

Who drives the saga — orchestration vs choreography

Orchestration. A central saga orchestrator — a state machine — calls each service, waits for the reply, and on failure drives the compensations in reverse. The whole flow lives in one place you can read, log, and debug. It is a component you must build and run, but note what it is not: unlike a 2PC coordinator it holds no participant locks — it only holds saga state, so if it briefly dies, nobody freezes; it resumes from its log.

Choreography. No central driver: the flight service emits flight-reserved, the hotel service reacts and emits hotel-reserved, and so on (Lesson 9's events, delivered via a durable log). Loosely coupled and no orchestrator to run — but the flow is smeared across services, so "what does booking do?" has no single answer, cycles are easy to create by accident, and debugging a stuck saga means chasing events across teams.

Name the trade-off — central clarity vs distributed coupling. Orchestration concentrates the workflow logic (easy to see and change, one more service to operate); choreography spreads it out (no central piece, but no single place shows the whole story). For a saga with real branching and compensations — like this one — orchestration is usually worth its keep, because the thing you most need when a distributed workflow half-fails at 3 a.m. is one place that knows where it got to.

Move 3 — Trace the paths

Run one happy booking and one failed booking through an orchestrated saga, and watch where the locks live and where the compensations fire.

Path A — everything succeeds

  1. reserve FLIGHT  -> local COMMIT (seat = HELD for this saga); lock freed in ~5 ms
  2. reserve HOTEL   -> local COMMIT (room = HELD);                lock freed
  3. reserve CAR     -> local COMMIT (car  = HELD);                lock freed
  4. CHARGE card     -> local COMMIT  <-- PIVOT: past here, only go forward
  5. CONFIRM         -> flip HELD -> SOLD on flight/hotel/car; issue tickets (retriable)
  -> trip booked. No lock was ever held across a network hop; each step stood alone.

Contrast the lock story with 2PC: there, all four rows would sit locked from step 1's prepare until the final commit (~50 ms of held locks across the whole set). Here each lock is held only for its own ~5 ms local commit. That is the entire availability and throughput win, made concrete.

Path B — the car is sold out

  1. reserve FLIGHT  -> COMMIT (seat HELD)      \
  2. reserve HOTEL   -> COMMIT (room HELD)       |  these really happened
  3. reserve CAR     -> FAILS (no cars left)     /
        |
        v  orchestrator runs COMPENSATIONS backward (before the pivot = all undoable)
  3c. (nothing to undo for car — it never committed)
  2c. cancel HOTEL   -> COMMIT (room HELD -> free)
  1c. cancel FLIGHT  -> COMMIT (seat HELD -> free)
  -> saga ends ABORTED. Card was never charged (charge is step 4, after the failure).
     Customer sees "couldn't complete your trip" — and no money moved.

The ordering did the heavy lifting: because the charge sits after the reservations, a failure among the reservations means we only ever cancel holds, never refund a real charge. But look closely at steps 1–2: between committing the flight and discovering the car is gone, the flight seat was genuinely reserved in the flight database — committed, visible, real. For a window of tens of milliseconds the world could see a trip that is about to be un-booked. That window is the wall.

Path C — the payment fails (why the pivot is placed where it is)

  1-3. reserve flight, hotel, car -> all COMMIT (HELD)
  4.   CHARGE card -> DECLINED
        |
        v  still before the pivot completes -> compensate backward
  cancel car, cancel hotel, cancel flight  (all holds released)  -> saga ABORTED cleanly

  BUT if we had ordered CHARGE first (step 1) and the HOTEL failed at step 3:
  1. charge $1,150 -> COMMIT   <-- money really left the customer
  3. hotel FAILS
  -> compensation must now REFUND a real charge (a visible debit + a refund on the
     statement) -- a business event, not an "undo".  Placing charge LAST avoids it.

Same steps, different order, very different customer experience. Charging last (as the pivot) keeps every pre-pivot step a cheap, invisible hold to release. Charging first forces a real refund on any later failure — money that visibly left and came back. Ordering steps by how hard they are to undo is a core saga design decision, not a detail.

Move 4 — Find the first bottleneck: a saga has no isolation

2PC gave up availability. The saga gave up the I in ACID — isolation. Because each step commits immediately, the saga's half-finished state is not hidden: other transactions can read and act on it. This isn't a bug you can patch away; it's the price of not holding locks. Two concrete anomalies show what breaks.

Anomaly 1 — a dirty read across the saga. Saga X reserves the last hotel room (committed, HELD), then its car step fails, so it will cancel the room. But in the gap, saga Y reads the hotel inventory, sees the room is taken, and tells its customer "sold out" — a decision based on a fact that is about to be un-made. Y read dirty, saga-intermediate state as if it were settled.

Anomaly 2 — a lost update across the saga. Two sagas both read "1 seat left," both reserve it, both commit locally (no shared lock stopped them) → the seat is double-booked, and now both compensations and a human apology are in your future. This is Lesson 6's lost update, except no single database could have prevented it, because the two writes landed through two separate saga steps.

The fix is not to bolt real isolation back on (that would reintroduce the cross-service lock we fled from). It is to make the intermediate state honest about being intermediate:

  • Semantic lock. Mark held state PENDING/HELD, not SOLD. Readers that care about settled facts treat HELD as "claimed but not final" — so saga Y waits or picks another room instead of trusting a soon-to-be-cancelled hold. The reservation row carries a flag that says "a saga owns me right now." You give up automatic isolation and take on the job of representing it in your data model.
  • Commutative updates. Design steps whose order doesn't matter where you can (increment/decrement a counter rather than set an absolute value, Lesson 6/21) — then two concurrent sagas don't clobber each other, they add up.
  • Reread & revalidate at the pivot. Before the point of no return, re-check the holds still exist and still say what you assumed (an optimistic check — Lesson 6's compare-and-set), and abort cheaply if the world moved.

Name the core trade-off — you traded blocking for a visible window. 2PC hides the intermediate state by holding locks (and pays in availability). The saga refuses to hold locks (and stays available) — so the intermediate state shows, and you must design for a world that can see a half-done trip. The saga doesn't make the trip atomic; it makes it eventually atomic — all-or-nothing that is briefly "some" in the middle. That's Lesson 11's AP again: available now, consistent soon, and a compensation is exactly the reconciliation that "AP is only as good as your ability to reconcile" was pointing at.

The four traps

  • Trap 1 — 2PC across services that are hot or autonomous. The 10× lock-hold tax and the availability product only bite under contention and scale; a coordinator crash freezes everyone in-doubt. 2PC is fine inside one trusted, low-throughput boundary (two DBs you own, XA, rarely-contended rows) and wrong across independent high-traffic services. Match the tool to the boundary.
  • Trap 2 — treating a compensation as a rollback. A rollback makes it as if the step never happened; a compensation is a new business action layered on top — the charge occurred and the refund occurred (two statement lines), the confirmation email that already went out cannot be un-sent. Design compensations as forward-moving corrections, and put anything truly un-undoable (send email, hand off to a partner) after the pivot as a retriable step, never before it as a compensatable one.
  • Trap 3 — non-idempotent steps or compensations. The messages driving a saga are delivered at least once (Lesson 9), so "cancel flight" or "charge card" can arrive twice. A non-idempotent charge double-charges; a non-idempotent cancel errors on the second try and stalls the saga. Every step and every compensation needs an idempotency key (Lesson 13) so a duplicate is a safe no-op. And compensations must be retriable — a compensation that can itself fail must be retried until it succeeds, or the saga leaks a half-undone booking.
  • Trap 4 — ignoring the isolation window. Assuming "committed = settled" and letting other work read saga-intermediate state as fact is how you get double-bookings and dirty reads. Represent in-flight state explicitly (semantic lock / PENDING status), or accept the anomaly and reconcile it — but decide on purpose. Silence here is a customer-visible bug, not a theoretical one.

Where we landed

We took one user action — book a trip = reserve flight + hotel + car + charge card — and faced the fact that a database transaction stops at the edge of its own database, so atomicity across four services must be built. Estimate priced the obvious "just hold a lock across all four" (that's two-phase commit): every contended row is held 50 / 5 = 10× longer than a local commit (a hot flight drops from ~200 to ~20 bookings/sec), the whole commit is only as available as the product of its participants (0.999⁴ ≈ 99.6% ≈ 35 h/yr unbookable), and a coordinator crash between the two phases leaves participants in-doubt — frozen holding locks until it recovers. That's the CP price: consistency by blocking. Model laid out the saga as the AP answer: commit each step locally and immediately (no cross-service lock ever held), and on failure walk backward running compensating transactions — ordered so everything before the pivot (the charge) is undoable and everything after is retriable, driven by a central orchestrator (clear, debuggable, holds saga state but no participant locks) or by choreographed events (no central piece, but the flow is smeared across services). Tracing a clean booking (four standalone local commits, no held network locks), a sold-out car (compensate the holds backward, no charge ever made), and a declined card (why the charge is placed last, so failure cancels holds instead of refunding real money) exposed the first bottleneck: a saga has no isolation — its half-done state is really visible, so a second saga can dirty-read a soon-to-be-cancelled hold or double-book the last seat. The fix isn't to re-add the lock we fled; it's to make intermediate state honest — a semantic lock (HELD, not SOLD), commutative updates, and a revalidate-at-the-pivot check — plus idempotent, retriable steps and compensations because the driving messages are at-least-once. Four traps guard the build: 2PC across hot/autonomous services, treating a compensation as a rollback, non-idempotent steps, and ignoring the visible window. The real lesson: across services you can't have atomicity — you can only choose how to fake it. 2PC fakes it by making everyone wait (and freeze); the saga fakes it by letting everyone finish and then apologizing correctly. Atomicity vs availability across service boundaries — and a compensation is the shape that apology takes.

Next lesson closes a gap this one leaned on:

• Change data capture & the outbox pattern: our saga steps had to "commit locally and emit an event" (reserve the flight and tell the next step). Doing both is a dual write — two systems, and either can fail after the other succeeds, so an event goes out with no matching row, or a row exists with no event. We'll fix it by making the event a byproduct of the one atomic commit: write the event to a transactional outbox in the same local transaction (Lesson 13/25), then ship it by tailing the database's commit log (CDC) — the same trick that feeds search (L16), cache (L02), and the warehouse (L29) without inconsistency. The trade shifts from atomicity vs availability to freshness/consistency vs pipeline complexity.
