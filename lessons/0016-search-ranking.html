Search Ranking: Which 10 of 40,000 Matches Come First

Lesson 12 built the inverted index that answers "which documents contain these words" — it turned a 10-million-row scan into a small merge of postings lists. But it stopped one step short of a real search box. A shopper types "red running shoes" into a catalog of 10 million products and 40,000 of them match. You can show 10. Which 10? That question — ranking — is the whole difference between a search that feels magic and one that feels broken. This lesson takes that one query and traces it end to end: parse it, retrieve the candidates (Lesson 12's job), then score each one with real arithmetic — TF-IDF, then why it's naive, then BM25 — sort the top 10, and find the bottleneck that forces every large search system into two phases. We name the trade-off underneath it all: recall vs precision.

The one system we'll trace

A product catalog: 10,000,000 products, each with a short text title. We already have the inverted index from Lesson 12 — a map from each word to the sorted list of product ids that contain it. A shopper searches red running shoes. Lesson 12 tells us who matches; this lesson decides who wins. Here is the whole pipeline on one screen.

Retrieval finds the set that matches; ranking sorts that set so the best few float to the top. Lesson 12 was retrieval. This lesson is ranking. The core insight is that not all matches are equal and not all words are equal — a product that matches the rare, specific word "shoe" should beat one that only matches the common word "red," and a product whose title is about shoes should beat one that merely mentions the word once in passing. Turning "should" into a number is the job.

Words we'll earn before we lean on them

  • Query parsing — turning the raw text into search terms: tokenize into words, lowercase, stem to roots ("running"→"run"), drop stopwords (ultra-common words like "the").
  • Candidate set — the products retrieved as possible matches, before ranking. Bigger set = higher recall, more to score.
  • Term frequency (TF) — how many times a query word appears in one document. More = the doc is more "about" that word… up to a point.
  • Document frequency (DF) — how many documents in the whole corpus contain a word. High DF = common word = weak signal.
  • Inverse document frequency (IDF) — log(N / DF). Turns "rare word" into "high weight." The math that makes "shoe" matter more than "red."
  • TF-IDF — the classic score: sum over query words of TF × IDF. Rewards docs that use rare query words often.
  • BM25 — the modern default: TF-IDF with two fixes — saturation (the 5th occurrence of a word adds almost nothing) and length normalization (a long doc doesn't win just for being long).
  • Recall vs precision — recall = of all the relevant products, what fraction did we return; precision = of what we returned, what fraction is relevant. The central tension.

Move 1 — Estimate: why ranking is unavoidable

Maybe we don't need scoring. Maybe the matching set is small enough to just show. Let's price it.

Worked: how big is the candidate set?

• The word "shoe" appears in 40,000 of the 10M titles (it's a shoe store; lots of shoes).
• "red" appears in 500,000 (a common color word).
• "run" appears in 120,000.

If we require all three words (a strict AND, Lesson 12's intersection) we might get a few thousand. But most real search uses OR — return anything matching at least one word — so we don't miss "crimson jogging sneakers" that lacks the exact words. That OR-merge is roughly 600,000 candidates. The shopper will look at the first 10, maybe scroll to 20. So we must choose 10 out of 600,000 — and the order is the entire product. Results 11–600,000 might as well not exist; 99.998% of the matched set is never seen. Everything rides on which 10 we put first.

Name the first trade-off — recall vs precision. Requiring all words (AND) gives high precision (every result truly is a red running shoe) but poor recall (you miss "crimson sneakers for jogging"). Requiring any word (OR) gives high recall (you catch the near-matches) but poor precision (you also catch a red dress and a running watch). You can't maximize both at once — so the standard move is retrieve broadly (OR, high recall) and then let ranking restore precision by pushing the true matches to the top. Ranking is how you get recall's coverage without paying precision's price at the top of the page.

Move 2 — Model: score a document, from TF-IDF to BM25

We need a number for each candidate. Start with the two intuitions and turn each into math.

Intuition 1 — rare words matter more (IDF)

If a product matches "shoe," that's informative — few products do. If it matches "the," that's worthless — almost everything does. We want a weight that is high for rare words, near zero for common ones. That's inverse document frequency: idf(term) = log(N / df(term)), with N = total docs. Let's compute it (base-10 logs, N = 10,000,000):

Worked: IDF of each query word.

• idf("shoe") = log(10,000,000 / 40,000) = log(250) ≈ 2.40
• idf("run")  = log(10,000,000 / 120,000) = log(83.3) ≈ 1.92
• idf("red")  = log(10,000,000 / 500,000) = log(20) ≈ 1.30
• idf("the")  = log(10,000,000 / 9,000,000) = log(1.11) ≈ 0.05

Look at "the": it lands near zero, so matching it barely moves the score. That's IDF automatically discovering what a stopword list does by hand — a word in 90% of docs carries almost no information. And "shoe" (2.40) outweighs "red" (1.30) by nearly 2×, exactly the ranking we wanted: the specific word beats the generic one without anyone hand-tuning it. The logarithm is what compresses the huge DF range into a gentle weight.

Intuition 2 — a doc that uses the word more is more about it (TF)

Term frequency is just the count of the word in the doc. TF-IDF multiplies the two intuitions and sums over the query: score(d) = Σ tf(term,d) × idf(term). Let's rank two real candidates for "red running shoes":

Worked: TF-IDF of two candidates.

Doc A — title "Red Running Shoes": tf(red)=1, tf(run)=1, tf(shoe)=1.
score(A) = 1×1.30 + 1×1.92 + 1×2.40 = 5.62

Doc B — a spammy title "Running shoes, best running shoe, running running": tf(red)=0, tf(run)=4, tf(shoe)=2.
score(B) = 0×1.30 + 4×1.92 + 2×2.40 = 7.68 + 4.80 = 12.48

Doc B wins by a mile — even though Doc A is the perfect match ("red running shoes") and Doc B isn't even red. Raw TF-IDF let a seller keyword-stuff "running running running" straight to the top. This is TF-IDF's fatal flaw: term frequency grows without limit, so repeating a word keeps paying. We need to cap the reward.

The fix — BM25: saturate the TF, normalize by length

BM25 is TF-IDF with the two flaws patched. The key patch is saturation: instead of tf growing forever, it feeds through tf × (k+1) / (tf + k), which rises fast at first then flattens to a ceiling of k+1. With the common k = 1.2 (so the ceiling is 2.2):

Worked: the saturation curve.

• tf = 1 →  1×2.2 / (1+1.2) = 2.2/2.2 = 1.00
• tf = 2 →  2×2.2 / (2+1.2) = 4.4/3.2 = 1.38
• tf = 4 →  4×2.2 / (4+1.2) = 8.8/5.2 = 1.69
• tf = 10 → 10×2.2 / (10+1.2) = 22/11.2 = 1.96  (creeping toward the 2.2 ceiling)

Going from 1 to 4 occurrences raises the factor from 1.00 to only 1.69 — not 4×. The 4th "running" adds almost nothing; the 10th nearly nothing. So Doc B's spam of "running running" stops helping after the first couple, and Doc A's clean, complete match — which also covers the rare word "shoe" and matches "red" — climbs back above it. BM25's second patch, length normalization, further penalizes Doc B for padding its title: a long document is expected to contain more words, so each occurrence in a bloated title counts for less. BM25 = IDF weighting + saturating TF + length normalization — the reason a 40-year-old formula is still the default first-pass ranker everywhere.

Name the trade-off: saturation trades a little "more is better" linearity for robustness against manipulation and noise. A perfectly linear TF ranks by raw keyword count — easy to game and dominated by long, repetitive documents. Saturating it says "the first mention tells me a lot, the tenth tells me almost nothing," which matches how relevance actually works and closes the keyword-stuffing hole.

Move 3 — Trace the paths

Path A — parsing the query (garbage in, garbage out)

Before retrieval, the raw string "Red Running Shoes!" becomes clean terms — and it must be the same transformation used when the index was built, or nothing matches. Tokenize → lowercase → drop punctuation → stem ("running"→"run", "shoes"→"shoe") → drop stopwords. If the index stored the stem "run" but the query searches the literal "running," the postings list lookup misses and a relevant product vanishes. Query-time analysis must mirror index-time analysis exactly. This is the quiet source of half of all "why isn't my product showing up?" bugs.

Path B — retrieve, then score, then top-K

Parsed terms go to the inverted index (Lesson 12): fetch the postings list for each, OR-merge to ~600,000 candidates. Score each with BM25. Now we need the top 10 — but we do not sort all 600,000 (that's N log N work to throw away 599,990 of them). Instead keep a min-heap of size 10: scan the candidates once, and for each, if it beats the smallest score in the heap, swap it in. That's N log 10 — linear in candidates, tiny constant. The heap is the standard "top-K without a full sort" trick.

Path C — the same query, two intents (why text score isn't enough)

Two products tie on BM25 for "running shoes": one is a best-seller in stock, the other is a discontinued item nobody's bought in a year. Pure text relevance can't tell them apart — both match the words equally. Real rankers add non-text signals to the score: popularity (sales, clicks), recency, availability, personalization. The final score is a blend: score = w₁·BM25 + w₂·popularity + w₃·recency + …. Text relevance decides who is eligible; the other signals decide who wins among the relevant. This is why the same words can rank differently for different shoppers, and why a search team spends most of its time tuning these weights, not the BM25 formula.

Move 4 — Find the next bottleneck: scoring cost forces two phases

BM25 over 600,000 candidates is cheap. But two things break as the system grows.

Wall 1 — the expensive ranker can't touch every candidate

Worked: why you can't run the good ranker on everything. The best rankers today are machine-learned models that look at hundreds of signals per document — and they cost, say, 1 millisecond per document to score. Run that on all 600,000 candidates and you spend 600,000 × 1 ms = 600 seconds — 10 minutes for one search. Impossible against a ~200 ms latency budget. But run it on just the top 100 and it's 100 × 1 ms = 100 ms — fine. So search splits into two phases:

1. Retrieval / first-pass rank — cheap BM25 over all 600,000, keep the top ~1,000. Fast, approximate, high recall.
2. Re-rank — the expensive ML model over just those 1,000 (or 100), producing the final order. Slow, precise, applied only where it matters.

Trade-off named: you can have a cheap ranker over everything or an expensive ranker over a few, so you use both — cheap to narrow, expensive to perfect. This "retrieve cheap, re-rank rich" funnel is the backbone of every large search and recommendation system. It's the same shape as Lesson 12's covering index (do the cheap thing first, pay for precision only where needed) and Lesson 14's edge/origin split.

Wall 2 — the index is stale the moment you build it

A seller adds a new "red running shoe" at noon. When can a shopper find it? The inverted index (Lesson 12) is a separate structure that trails the source database — it must be updated, and updating a 10M-doc index in real time is expensive. So search runs near-real-time indexing: new and changed documents are batched and folded into the index on a short refresh interval (often ~1 second). Trade-off: index freshness vs indexing cost — refresh every second and you burn CPU constantly; refresh every hour and new products are invisible for an hour. This is Lesson 06's replication lag and Lesson 14's TTL, wearing a search hat: the index is an eventually-consistent copy, and you choose how far behind it may fall.

Where we landed

We took one query — "red running shoes" over 10M products — and ranked it. Estimate showed why ranking is unavoidable: OR-retrieval matches ~600,000, the shopper sees 10, so the order is the product, and we named the tension underneath — recall vs precision, resolved by retrieving broadly and ranking to restore precision at the top. Model built the score from two intuitions: IDF = log(N/DF) makes the rare word "shoe" (2.40) beat "red" (1.30) and drives "the" to ~0.05 for free; TF-IDF multiplies and sums — but its unbounded TF let a keyword-stuffed title (12.48) crush a perfect match (5.62), so BM25 patches it with a saturating TF (1→4 occurrences only lifts the factor 1.00→1.69) and length normalization. Tracing the paths covered query parsing that must mirror the index exactly, the retrieve→score→top-K heap pipeline, and why pure text score needs a blend of popularity and recency to break ties. The next bottleneck forced the universal shape: an ML ranker at 1 ms/doc would take 10 minutes over 600,000, so search splits into two phases — cheap BM25 to narrow to ~1,000, expensive re-rank on those — and the index runs near-real-time, an eventually-consistent copy trading freshness for cost. The thread: retrieval finds what matches; ranking decides what wins — and every scaling decision is some version of "narrow cheaply, then perfect expensively."
