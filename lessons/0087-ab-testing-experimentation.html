A/B Testing & Experimentation Platforms: Proving the New Re-Ranker Actually Helped

Lesson 86 built a recommender and shipped a new re-rank model. Then someone asked the only question that matters: did it actually help? Our instinct is to launch it and watch the click-through rate — but that number wobbles day to day for a hundred reasons (weekend, a holiday, a marketing email), so a rise after launch proves nothing. The honest way to know is to run the old and new rankers at the same time, on comparable users, and compare — an A/B test. Doing that once is easy. Doing it so the answer is trustworthy — and doing hundreds of them at once without them poisoning each other — is a real system. We take one concrete change (the new re-ranker from L86, "reranker_v2") and one concrete metric (click-through rate on the "Products you may like" strip), and trace the experiment end to end. We'll estimate how many users the test needs and how long it must run — a number most people guess wrong by 10×. We'll model the platform: deterministic bucketing by hash (L03/L04), an exposure-logging stream (L64), and layered assignment so many experiments run at once. We'll trace three paths — one user through assignment, a clean readout, and a broken one. And we'll find the first bottleneck: every number the test produces is worthless unless the split itself is valid — so the sample-ratio check is the tripwire you run before you believe anything. The trade under all of it: statistical rigor & safety vs shipping velocity.

The one system we'll build

The marketplace from L86: 5 million daily active users, one home-page strip ("Products you may like"). We have a candidate change and one question:

  • The change: reranker_v2 — the new re-rank model from Lesson 86. We believe it ranks better. We must prove it before it reaches all 5M users.
  • The metric: strip click-through rate (CTR) — the fraction of users who, when shown the strip, click at least one card. Today's baseline is 10%. We hope v2 lifts it.
  • The unit: we randomize by user — a given user always sees the same version, so their whole experience is consistent and their behavior isn't split across both arms.

Here is the whole experiment on one page. Users are split by a hash into a control group (sees the old ranker) and a treatment group (sees v2); we log who actually saw each version and what they did; then we compare — but only after checking the split itself is sound:

     │     buckets  500– 999  → TREATMENT (reranker_v2)  │  5%

The one idea the whole lesson turns on: an experiment is a machine for telling a real effect apart from random noise. Everything below — the sample-size math, deterministic bucketing, exposure logging, the SRM tripwire, guardrails, layered assignment — exists to protect that one distinction. Get the split clean and the counting honest, and the comparison is trustworthy; break either, and every decimal place downstream is a lie told with confidence.

Words we'll earn before we lean on them

  • A/B test (controlled experiment) — run two versions at the same time on randomly split, comparable users, and compare a metric. The random split is what lets you attribute a difference to the change and nothing else.
  • Control / treatment — control (A) is the current version; treatment (B) is the change. Control is the "what would have happened anyway" baseline the world doesn't otherwise give you.
  • Randomization unit — the thing you split on (here, the user). Same unit always gets the same variant.
  • Primary metric — the one number the experiment is designed to move (here, strip CTR). Decided before the test.
  • Guardrail metric — a metric that must not get worse, even if the primary metric wins (latency, revenue, crash rate).
  • Statistical significance / p-value — the chance you'd see a difference this big if the change actually did nothing. Small p = the noise-only explanation is unlikely.
  • Statistical power — the chance the test detects a real effect of a given size if one exists. Low power = a real win slips through as "no result."
  • Minimum detectable effect (MDE) — the smallest true lift the test can reliably catch. Smaller MDE needs more users.
  • Sample-ratio mismatch (SRM) — the observed split (e.g. 50.6% / 49.4%) differs from the intended split (50/50) by more than chance allows → the assignment or logging is broken.
  • Peeking — repeatedly checking significance and stopping the moment it crosses 0.05. It quietly inflates the false-positive rate far past 5%.
  • Exposure vs assignment — assigned = the hash put you in a group; exposed = you actually saw the thing. You analyze the exposed.
  • Layered / orthogonal assignment — a scheme that lets many experiments run at once without correlating: same layer = mutually exclusive, different layers = independent.
  • SUTVA / interference — the assumption that one user's treatment doesn't affect another user's outcome. Marketplaces and social networks break it.

Move 1 — Estimate: how many users, and for how long?

Before writing any code, one calculation decides whether the experiment is even possible: how many users you need to tell a real lift apart from noise. Guess too small and you'll "detect" nothing real; the answer shapes the ramp and the runtime.

The sample-size formula for comparing two rates. To catch a lift of size δ (absolute, in the same units as the rate) on a baseline rate p, with the usual 95% confidence and 80% power, the users needed per arm is:

  n per arm  ≈  16 · p·(1−p) / δ²

  where 16 ≈ (z₉₅ + z₈₀)² · 2 = (1.96 + 0.84)² · 2 = 15.68
        p   = baseline rate           (here 0.10 = 10% CTR)
        δ   = the lift you want to catch (absolute)

  detect a 2% RELATIVE lift  (10.0% → 10.2%,  δ = 0.002):
     n = 16 × 0.10×0.90 / 0.002²
       = 16 × 0.09 / 0.000004
       = 1.44 / 0.000004
       = 360,000  users per arm   → 720,000 total

  detect a 4% RELATIVE lift  (10.0% → 10.4%,  δ = 0.004):
     n = 1.44 / 0.000016 = 90,000 per arm → 180,000 total

The smaller the effect you want to catch, the far more users you need — and it grows with the square. Because n ∝ 1/δ², halving the effect you can detect costs 4× the users (and 4× the time). This is the sensitivity dial: a test sized for a 2% lift needs 720,000 users; wanting to catch a 1% lift would need ~2.9 million. You decide the MDE up front — chasing tinier effects is exponentially expensive, so pick the smallest lift that would actually change your decision, and no smaller.

How long must it run? Turn the user count into calendar time from the traffic you're willing to expose. We ramp carefully — 5% of users to control, 5% to treatment (the other 90% untouched, so a bad v2 can hurt at most 5%):

  exposed to treatment   = 5% × 5,000,000 DAU = 250,000 users/day
  need (2% MDE)          = 360,000 per arm
  time to accumulate     = 360,000 / 250,000  ≈ 1.5 days

  BUT run ≥ 1 full week anyway:
     · weekday and weekend users behave differently — a 2-day
       readout is a biased sample of "users"
     · novelty effect: a change looks great for 2 days because it's
       NEW, then fades — a week lets that wash out

The sample-size floor and the representativeness floor are two different limits — you wait for whichever is longer. Here the math is satisfied in ~1.5 days, but you still run a full week (often two) so the sample spans a whole weekly cycle and any short-lived novelty bump settles. Trade named for the first time: rigor vs velocity — every extra day of runtime is a day the winning change isn't shipped and the losing one keeps costing, but cutting the test short buys a fast answer that's wrong.

Why can't we just launch it and watch the CTR climb? Because CTR moves on its own every single day — weekends, holidays, a push notification, a viral product, a competitor's outage. A "before vs after" comparison confounds your change with everything else that changed at the same time, and you can never untangle them. The control group is the fix: it rides all those same external swings in parallel, so subtracting control from treatment cancels the noise and leaves only the effect of the change. No control group, no attribution — that's the whole reason the experiment exists.

Move 2 — Model: assignment, logging, and running many at once

Stage 1 — assignment: deterministic bucketing by hash

Every request must decide, fast and consistently, which variant a user sees — with no database lookup and no way for the same user to flip between arms. The tool is a hash (L03/L04), exactly as we routed keys to shards:

  variant(user, exp):
     h = hash(exp_name + ":" + user_id)   # e.g. "reranker_v2:U-8842"
     bucket = h % 10000                    # 10,000 stable buckets
     if   bucket <  500:  return CONTROL
     elif bucket < 1000:  return TREATMENT
     else:                return NOT_IN_EXPERIMENT

  · DETERMINISTIC: same user → same bucket → same variant, forever,
    on every server, with zero shared state (L03/L04, stateless).
  · SALTED with exp_name: the SAME user gets an INDEPENDENT bucket
    in a different experiment (why that matters: next stage).

Hashing the user id gives a random-but-repeatable split with no lookup table. Randomness makes the two groups comparable on everything (age, country, past spend) — known and unknown alike — so the only systematic difference between them is the change itself. Repeatability keeps a user's experience stable and their data on one side. Trade: hashing is free and stateless, but the split is fixed by the id — you can't hand-pick who's in which arm (and you shouldn't: that's how bias sneaks in).

Stage 2 — exposure logging: count who actually SAW it

Assignment is not experience. A user hashed into treatment who never scrolled to the strip saw nothing different — counting them just dilutes the signal with people the change never touched. So log an exposure event the moment the strip is actually shown, and analyze on those:

  when the strip renders for a user IN the experiment, emit:
     { user_id, exp:"reranker_v2", variant, shown_ts, clicked }
        → an append-only event stream (L64), 100k+ events/s

  ASSIGNED  = the hash chose a variant for this user
  EXPOSED   = the user actually saw the strip  (log THIS)
  analyze CTR over EXPOSED users only ("triggered" analysis)

  danger: the exposure log must fire IDENTICALLY for both arms.
     if treatment logs on render but control logs on scroll,
     the two counts aren't comparable → a built-in bias (and SRM).

You measure the effect on the people who experienced the change, not the people a hash merely labeled. This is a high-volume stream problem (L64): exposures pour in, get joined to outcomes (clicks, purchases), and roll up into per-variant metrics. The subtle rule: exposure must be logged the same way in both arms, or the very act of measuring introduces the difference you're trying to detect — a leading cause of the broken tests we'll see in Move 3.

Stage 3 — layered assignment: hundreds of experiments at once

A big product runs hundreds of experiments simultaneously. Two problems appear: they'd fight for the same users, and if two changes touch the same surface their effects confound. The fix is layers:

  LAYER = a full copy of the traffic with its own hash salt.

  same layer   → experiments are MUTUALLY EXCLUSIVE
                 (a user is in at most one; use when changes
                  interact — two ranking tweaks on the same strip)

  different layer → assignment is INDEPENDENT / orthogonal
                 (different salt → uncorrelated buckets; a user can
                  be in BOTH a ranking test and a checkout-button
                  test, and the two effects don't tangle)

  ranking-layer   hash("layer:ranking:"  + user) → picks a ranking exp
  checkout-layer  hash("layer:checkout:" + user) → picks a checkout exp
       independent salts ⇒ every combination of the two occurs,
       so each layer's effect averages cleanly over the other.

Put experiments that could interact in the same layer (mutually exclusive); put independent ones in different layers (concurrent and orthogonal). Orthogonality is exactly why salting the hash with the experiment/layer name mattered in Stage 1: without independent salts, the same users would always cluster together across experiments, and you could never tell which change caused an effect. Trade: same-layer experiments don't overlap so you can run fewer at once (less throughput), but their results are clean; cross-layer experiments run concurrently (high velocity) at the cost of assuming they don't interact.

Move 3 — Trace the paths: one user, a clean readout, a broken one

Path A — one user through assignment (no state, no lookup)

   user U-8842 loads the home page
     │
     ▼  the strip service computes, inline:
   h = hash("reranker_v2:U-8842") % 10000  → bucket 641
   641 is in [500,1000) → TREATMENT → serve reranker_v2
     │
     ▼  the strip renders
   emit exposure { U-8842, reranker_v2, TREATMENT, ts, clicked=? }
     │
     ▼  U-8842 returns 3 hours later, different server
   hash is deterministic → bucket 641 again → still TREATMENT
   (consistent experience, data stays on one side)

No database was consulted to decide the variant, and no coordination between servers was needed — the same property that let L03/L04 route a key to a shard from the key alone. The user's whole experience is stable, and their clicks never get split across both arms (which would contaminate both).

Path B — a clean readout after one week

   after 7 days, over EXPOSED users:
     control (old):   1,800,000 exposed,  180,000 clicks → CTR 10.00%
     treatment (v2):  1,800,000 exposed,  187,200 clicks → CTR 10.40%

   1. SRM check: 1,800,000 vs 1,800,000 → split is 50/50 ✓ (trust the rest)
   2. lift: 10.40% − 10.00% = +0.40pp  (a +4.0% relative lift)
      is it real, or noise? 95% confidence interval on the lift:
         ≈ [+0.34pp, +0.46pp]  — excludes 0 → significant (p < 0.001)
   3. guardrails: p99 latency +2 ms (ok), revenue/user flat, crashes flat ✓
     │
     ▼
   DECISION: v2 wins on CTR, breaks no guardrail → SHIP (ramp to 100%)

A result is a decision only when the split is valid, the effect clears noise, AND no guardrail regressed. All three gates passed here: the 50/50 split held (so the comparison is fair), the confidence interval on the lift excludes zero (so it's unlikely to be noise), and latency/revenue/crashes didn't get worse (so the CTR win isn't paid for elsewhere). Only then does "+0.4pp CTR" become "ship it."

Path C — the same readout, but the split is broken (SRM)

   after 7 days, over exposed users:
     control:    1,750,000 exposed
     treatment:  1,762,000 exposed        "only 2% off, close enough?"

   SRM check (χ², expected 1,756,000 each of 3,512,000 total):
     σ of a count under 50/50 = √(3,512,000 × 0.25) ≈ 937
     deviation = 6,000  →  6,000 / 937 ≈ 6.4 standard deviations
     χ² = 2 × 6,000² / 1,756,000 ≈ 41   (vs 3.84 threshold, 1 df)
     p  ≈ 0.00000000002   → this split is NOT chance.

   → SOMETHING IS BROKEN in assignment or exposure logging:
       · a bug drops some treatment users before they log exposure
       · treatment errors out for slow phones (survivorship bias)
       · the two arms log exposure on different triggers
   → the CTR comparison is now MEANINGLESS. Do not interpret it.
     Fix the pipeline, discard this run, rerun.

A "small" 2% imbalance at this scale is 6.4 standard deviations — impossible by chance, so it's a bug, and it invalidates every other number. SRM is the experiment's smoke alarm: it doesn't tell you what's on fire, only that the room is full of smoke and you must not trust anything inside it. The usual culprit — a crash or a filter that removes users unevenly across arms — also silently biases the CTR itself (the users who fell out weren't random), so a mismatched split and a corrupted metric are the same wound. You never "adjust for" SRM; you fix the cause and rerun.

Move 4 — Find the first bottleneck: trust, and the ways it leaks

The first bottleneck — the result is only as trustworthy as the split. The platform's entire output is one comparison, and that comparison assumes the two groups differ only by the change. The SRM check is the tripwire that guards that assumption, and you run it before reading any metric:

  the chain of trust:
     valid split  →  fair comparison  →  believable lift  →  decision
        ↑
     if this link breaks, EVERYTHING downstream is fiction —
     a beautiful p-value computed on two non-comparable groups.

  so the analysis ORDER is fixed:
     1. SRM?  (broken → stop, fix, rerun — do not interpret)
     2. only then: is the lift beyond noise?
     3. only then: did guardrails hold?

Statistics can't rescue a broken randomization — no confidence interval means anything if the two groups weren't comparable to begin with. This is why mature platforms compute SRM automatically on every experiment and refuse to show results when it fails. Trade: the SRM gate occasionally blocks a real (but slightly unbalanced) result and forces a rerun — a little velocity spent to keep every shipped decision honest.

The second wall — peeking inflates false positives. The dashboard updates live, and the temptation is to check every hour and ship the instant p < 0.05. Each look is another roll of the dice, and the false-positive rate climbs far past the 5% you think you're holding:

  a single fixed-horizon test:   false positive rate = 5%   (as designed)

  peek repeatedly, stop at the first p < 0.05:
     each new look is another chance to cross 0.05 by luck.
     a handful of daily peeks pushes the REAL false-positive rate
     from 5% to well over 20%; keep peeking on a null test long
     enough and you WILL eventually "find" significance (→ ~100%).

  fixes:
   · pre-register a fixed sample size + duration; read ONCE at the end.
   · or use SEQUENTIAL tests (always-valid p-values / group-sequential
     boundaries / Bayesian) that are DESIGNED to be watched continuously.

Looking many times and stopping at the first "win" is not the same test as looking once — it silently multiplies your chances of a false alarm. The honest options are to decide the horizon in advance and read the result once, or to switch to a method built for continuous monitoring (which pays for the right to peek by demanding a larger effect early on). Trade: fixed-horizon is simplest and most powerful but you must wait for the end; sequential methods let you stop early and safely at the cost of some sensitivity. Either way, never a naive peek-and-grab.

The third wall — a metric win that breaks the product (guardrails). Optimizing one number invites wrecking others. A re-ranker can lift CTR by surfacing loud, clickbait-y items that people click and regret — CTR up, satisfaction and revenue down:

  reranker_v3 result:
     primary:   CTR        +6%   (significant! looks like a big win)
     guardrail: revenue/user  −3%   (people click more, BUY less)
     guardrail: p99 latency  +45 ms (extra feature fetch, past L27's knee)
     guardrail: "hide this rec" rate +20%

  → a primary-metric win is GATED on guardrails: this ships NOTHING.
     the CTR lift was bought by degrading the things that matter more.

A metric is a proxy, and any proxy can be gamed — so you protect the things you actually care about with guardrails that must not regress. The metric hierarchy makes the decision honest: a primary metric to move, secondary metrics for context, and guardrails (latency, revenue, complaint rate, crash rate) that veto a launch no matter how good the primary looks. Trade: guardrails slow shipping and occasionally block a genuine local win — the price of not optimizing yourself off a cliff (the L27 latency knee, the L28 goodput cliff, all reachable by "winning" one metric).

The fourth wall — interference: one user's treatment leaks into another's (SUTVA breaks). A/B math assumes control users are unaffected by what treatment users do. In a two-sided marketplace, that's false — buyers and sellers share the same inventory:

  reranker_v2 makes treatment buyers snap up the best-value tents faster.
     → fewer good tents left in stock for CONTROL buyers
     → control's CTR/conversion DROPS — not because control got worse,
       but because treatment ATE the shared supply.
  the "lift" is partly treatment cannibalizing control → OVERSTATED.

  fixes (pick per interference type):
   · cluster/geo randomization: split by MARKET, not user, so a whole
     market is all-A or all-B and the shared pool isn't split.
   · budget/switchback tests for shared-resource or network effects.
   · measure the ecosystem metric, not just the per-user one.

When users compete for a shared resource or influence each other, splitting individuals mixes the two worlds and the comparison lies. This is the same shape as L23's shared invariant and L34's shared dependency: independence assumed where it doesn't hold. The fix is to randomize at a unit that contains the interference — a market, a region, a time slice — so each unit lives entirely in one world. Trade: cluster randomization removes interference bias but slashes your effective sample size (you have far fewer markets than users → bigger MDE, longer tests) — rigor bought with a lot of velocity.

So the honest picture: an experiment is a machine for separating a real effect from noise, and every part of it defends that separation. Estimate showed the size question decides feasibility — n ≈ 16·p(1−p)/δ² means 720,000 users to catch a 2% lift and 4× that to catch half as much, and you run a full week regardless so the sample spans the weekly cycle and novelty washes out. Model built the platform: deterministic hash bucketing (stateless, comparable, repeatable, salted per experiment — L03/L04), an exposure-logging stream that counts who actually saw the change and logs both arms identically (L64), and layered assignment that runs hundreds of experiments at once (mutually exclusive within a layer, orthogonal across layers). Tracing one user's stateless assignment, a clean three-gate readout, and a broken run showed the machine in motion. The first bottleneck — trust in the split — makes SRM the tripwire you check before any metric; beneath it, peeking is disarmed by pre-registration or sequential tests, metric-gaming by guardrails, and interference by cluster randomization. The standing tension: statistical rigor & safety vs shipping velocity.

The deepest point: an A/B test doesn't measure whether your change is good — it measures whether the difference you see is bigger than the noise, given a valid split. Every failure mode is a way that second clause quietly breaks. SRM is the split going invalid. Peeking is manufacturing a difference out of noise by looking until luck obliges. Guardrails are admitting your one metric isn't the whole good. Interference is the split failing to actually separate the two worlds. The genuinely new idea is small and sharp: experimentation is the discipline of earning the right to say "this change caused that outcome" — and that right rests entirely on a randomization you must constantly verify, not assume. The trade to carry: statistical rigor & safety vs shipping velocity.

The four traps

  • Trap 1 — before/after instead of A/B. Comparing CTR before and after launch confounds your change with every other thing that changed that week. Only a control group running in parallel cancels the external noise and lets you attribute the difference to the change.
  • Trap 2 — peeking until it's significant. Checking the dashboard repeatedly and shipping at the first p<0.05 inflates the false-positive rate from 5% to well over 20%. Pre-register the sample size and read once, or use a sequential test built for continuous monitoring.
  • Trap 3 — trusting a metric with no guardrails. Any single metric can be gamed (CTR up via clickbait, revenue down). A primary-metric win must clear guardrails — latency, revenue, complaint rate — or it ships nothing.
  • Trap 4 — reading results without checking the split. A gorgeous confidence interval computed on two non-comparable groups is fiction. SRM is the first thing you check; if the split is broken, you fix the pipeline and rerun — you never "adjust for it" and interpret anyway.
