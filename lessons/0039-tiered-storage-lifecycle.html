Tiered Storage & Data Lifecycle: When the Log Outgrows Fast Disk

Three lessons in a row handed us a pile of bytes that only ever grows. Lesson 20's object store held 100 PB of dashcam clips. Lesson 29's warehouse kept 11 TB of orders and re-scanned quarters of history. Lesson 38's event log grew ~130 GB/day ≈ 47 TB/year and, because events are immutable, never shrinks. Each of those lessons ended on the same quiet worry: we can't afford to keep all of this on fast disk forever. This lesson is the answer, and it rests on one fact you already met in Lesson 2: reads are wildly skewed toward new data. Yesterday's event is read constantly; a three-year-old one is read a thousand times less often — but you're still paying the same fast-SSD price to store it. So we split storage into tiers — RAM → SSD → HDD → object store → glacier — that get exponentially cheaper and exponentially slower as you go down, and we let each byte flow downhill as it cools, governed by a lifecycle policy. We'll take one concrete system — Lesson 38's event log, kept 7 years for compliance — and watch. We'll estimate the bill for keeping it all hot versus tiering it (a ~44× cut); model the tier ladder, temperature, and the policy that moves data; trace a write, a hot read, a cold read, and the background sweep that demotes and finally deletes; and hit the first wall — the retrieval-latency cliff, where reading an archived byte takes minutes, not milliseconds. The trade shifts from Lesson 38's auditability to storage cost vs retrieval latency & operational policy.

The one system we'll design

Lesson 38's event log: 5,000 events/second, each ~300 B, appended forever and never overwritten. A regulator says we must keep every event for 7 years, then we may delete it. That's the whole system — a firehose of small immutable records with a fixed retention window. The question is not how to store it (we know: append to a log). The question is where each record should live as it ages, because a record written today and a record written three years ago have the same size but are worth wildly different amounts of money to keep on fast disk.

  ACCESS FREQUENCY DECAYS WITH AGE  (Lesson 2's skew, drawn over time)

   reads/day

      +--------------------------------------------------------> age
       today   1wk        90d              1yr                7yr

  Same 300 B whether it's 1 day or 6 years old. But the 6-year-old byte is
  read ~1000x less often -- so paying the fast-SSD price to store it is waste.
  TIERED STORAGE = let each byte fall to a cheaper/slower tier as it COOLS.

Tiered storage keeps data in a hierarchy of storage classes that trade price against retrieval latency, and a lifecycle policy moves each record to a cheaper, slower tier as its access frequency falls. Everything in this lesson comes from two facts standing next to each other: storage tiers get roughly 5× cheaper and ~10× slower at each step down, and access frequency collapses with age. Put those together and the design writes itself: cheap-and-slow is exactly right for old-and-rarely-read.

Words we'll earn before we lean on them

  • Tier / storage class — a place to keep bytes with a fixed $/GB-month and a fixed retrieval latency. Cheaper tiers are slower. Examples: RAM, SSD, HDD, object store, glacier/tape.
  • Temperature (hot / warm / cold / archive) — how often data is read. Hot = read constantly; archive = read almost never. Age is usually a good proxy for temperature, but not always (Move 4).
  • Lifecycle policy — the rules that automatically move a record to another tier, or delete it, based on age or access. The thing that makes tiering happen without a human.
  • TTL (time to live) — "after N days, do X" (demote, or delete). The knob a lifecycle rule turns.
  • Retention — the minimum time you are required to keep data (compliance). A floor you may not delete below. Its opposite is a mandated maximum (e.g. privacy law says delete after N days).
  • Tiering vs expiration — two different actions a policy can take: tier = move to cheaper storage but keep it retrievable; expire = delete permanently. Never confuse them.
  • Retrieval / rehydration — pulling cold or archived data back up to a readable tier. On the archive tier this takes minutes to hours and often a per-GB fee — the cliff of Move 4.

Move 1 — Estimate: the bill for keeping it all hot vs tiering it

Two numbers drive everything: how much data we accumulate over the 7-year window, and how skewed the reads are. Let's get both, then price the naive design against the tiered one.

How much data, total. 5,000 events/s × 300 B = 1.5 MB/s = ~130 GB/day. Over the 7-year retention window: 130 GB × 365 × 7 ≈ 332 TB, and it only grows until the oldest data starts aging out at year 7. Call it ~330 TB in steady state.

How skewed the reads are (Lesson 2). Suppose ~90% of reads touch data from the last 7 days. Seven days is 130 GB × 7 = ~910 GB ≈ 1 TB — which is 1 / 330 = 0.3% of the pile serving 90% of the traffic. The other 99.7% is read rarely, and the oldest years almost never. That is the whole opportunity.

The naive bill — everything on hot SSD. Take hot SSD at $0.10/GB-month. 330,000 GB × $0.10 = $33,000/month ≈ $396k/year — to keep 99.7% of it fast for the 0.3% of reads that are, and the rest for almost nothing.

Now tier it. Pick four classes (real cloud pricing is close to these), each ~5× cheaper and ~10× slower than the one above, and slice the data by age:

  THE TIER LADDER  (price and latency per step; numbers approximate but realistic)

  tier      medium              $/GB-month   read latency        step down = ...
  --------  ------------------  -----------  ------------------  -------------------
  HOT       SSD / NVMe          $0.100       ~1 ms               (baseline)
  WARM      HDD / std object    $0.020       ~10 ms              ~5x cheaper, ~10x slower
  COLD      infrequent object   $0.004       ~100 ms - 1 s (+fee) ~5x cheaper, ~10x slower
  ARCHIVE   glacier / tape      $0.001       MINUTES to HOURS    ~4x cheaper, ~1000x slower  <-- the CLIFF

  SLICE THE 330 TB BY AGE, put each slice on the right tier:

  age range        data          tier      monthly cost
  0 - 7 days       ~1 TB         HOT       1,000 x $0.100 = $  100
  7 - 90 days      ~10.8 TB      WARM      10,800 x $0.020 = $  216
  90d - 1 year     ~35.8 TB      COLD      35,800 x $0.004 = $  143
  1 - 7 years      ~285 TB       ARCHIVE   285,000 x $0.001 = $ 285
                   --------                                 --------
                   ~332 TB                        TOTAL  ~ $  744 / month

  All-hot: $33,000/mo.   Tiered: ~$744/mo.   =>  ~44x cheaper, same data kept.

Double-check the arithmetic. Warm span = 90 − 7 = 83 days × 130 GB ≈ 10.8 TB → 10,800 × $0.02 = $216. Cold span = 365 − 90 = 275 days × 130 GB ≈ 35.8 TB → 35,800 × $0.004 = $143. Archive = 6 years × 47.5 TB/yr ≈ 285 TB → 285,000 × $0.001 = $285. Sum = 100 + 216 + 143 + 285 = $744/month vs $33,000 all-hot. The cut is ~44×, and every dollar of it came from the read skew — you moved the cold 99.7% onto storage that's cheap precisely because it's slow, and left the hot 0.3% fast. Note the shape: the biggest slice of data (archive, 86% of the bytes) is the smallest slice of the bill (38%), because its per-GB price is 100× lower.

Name the trade-off — price vs retrieval latency, tier by tier. There is no free lunch in the ladder: every step down multiplies your savings and multiplies the time to read the data back. Hot SSD is 100× the price of glacier and 100,000× faster to read. So tiering is not "make storage cheap" — it's match each byte's tier to how fast you'll need it back. A byte you might need in 1 ms must stay hot no matter how old it is; a byte you'll accept waiting hours for can go to archive. Age is the cheap proxy we use to guess that, but the real variable is retrieval urgency, and Move 4 is about what happens when the proxy is wrong.

Move 2 — Model: the ladder, the temperature, and the policy that moves data

Three parts. The ladder is the fixed menu of tiers. The temperature of a record decides which rung it belongs on. The lifecycle policy is the background machinery that actually moves records down the ladder as they cool — and deletes them at the end.

The ladder is a menu, not a decision. Each tier is just (price, latency). You don't design the tiers — the cloud/hardware gives them to you. You design the mapping from data to tier. The whole skill is deciding, per class of data, how far down the ladder it may fall and how fast it must come back.

Temperature is what you're really sorting by — age is only a proxy. The rung a record belongs on is set by how often it's read and how urgently. For an append-only log, age predicts temperature almost perfectly (new = hot, old = cold), so "demote by age" is a fine policy. But the two can diverge — a dormant account reactivated after two years is suddenly hot despite being old (Move 4's promotion path). Sort by temperature; use age as the estimator when it's a good one.

The lifecycle policy is a background sweep, not a per-read decision. A record carries metadata: created-at, last-accessed, current tier. A background job (Lesson 24's batched, throttled sweep) walks the data and applies rules: age > 7d → warm, age > 90d → cold, age > 1y → archive, age > 7y → DELETE. Two actions, kept strictly separate: tiering (move, still retrievable) and expiration (delete forever). Conflating them is how you either keep paying for data you were allowed to drop, or delete data you were required to keep.

Keep the index separate from the data — this is Lesson 20's metadata-plane / data-plane split, and it's the move that makes cold tiers usable:

  SPLIT THE INDEX (small, always HOT) FROM THE DATA (huge, tiered)

  METADATA PLANE  (tiny, stays HOT forever)          DATA PLANE (330 TB, tiered)
  ----------------------------------------           ------------------------------
  event_id | created_at  | tier    | locator  --->   HOT  : recent segments (SSD)
  ...9f3a  | 2026-07-26  | HOT     | seg-8891 --+     WARM : older segments (HDD)
  ...1c02  | 2024-02-11  | ARCHIVE | glc-0247 ----->  COLD : infrequent object store
  ...              (~a few GB total)                  ARCH : glacier / tape

  Because the INDEX is always hot, you can always FIND any event in ~1 ms and know
  exactly which tier holds its bytes. Only the BYTES are slow to fetch from cold tiers.
  "Where is event X?" is never the slow part; "give me X's payload from archive" is.

Name the trade-off — a lifecycle policy encodes two opposite legal limits at once. Retention is a floor (you must keep payment records 7 years — deleting early is a compliance violation) and privacy law is often a ceiling (you must delete personal data after N days — keeping it too long is a liability). The same policy engine enforces both: an expiration rule is either "don't you dare delete before T" or "you must delete by T." Tiering saves money; expiration manages risk. They run in the same sweep but answer to different masters — cost on one side, law on the other — so never let a cost-driven demotion quietly turn into a delete, and never let a legal-hold record get expired.

Move 3 — Trace the paths

Four runs: a write (always lands hot), a hot read (fast, the common case), a cold/archive read (the cliff, previewed here), and the background sweep that does the demoting and the deleting.

Path A — a write (a new event arrives)

  append event  {id: 9f3a, type: MoneyDeposited, ...}

   1. Write the payload to the current HOT segment (SSD) -- newest data is hottest,
        so it always starts at the top of the ladder. ~1 ms, done.
   2. Insert one row in the METADATA index: {id, created_at=now, tier=HOT, locator}.
   3. Return success. No tiering decision at write time -- cooling happens LATER,
        in the background sweep (Path D). Writes never touch cold tiers.

Path B — a hot read (the 90% case: recent data)

  GET event 9f3a  (written today)

   1. Look up 9f3a in the HOT metadata index -> {tier: HOT, locator: seg-8891}. ~1 ms.
   2. tier == HOT -> read the bytes straight from SSD. ~1 ms. Return.

  Total ~2 ms. This is 90% of traffic (Move 1's skew), served entirely from the
  cheap-to-serve 0.3% of the data that we chose to keep fast. This is WHY tiering pays.

Path C — a cold / archive read (the rare case: an old event)

  GET event 1c02  (written 2 years ago -> tier ARCHIVE)

   1. Look up 1c02 in the metadata index -> {tier: ARCHIVE, locator: glc-0247}. ~1 ms.
        (Finding it is still fast -- the index is always hot.)
   2. tier == ARCHIVE -> the bytes are on glacier/tape. You CANNOT read them inline.
        Issue a RESTORE (rehydration) job and respond 202 "retrieval in progress".
   3. Minutes to hours later, the bytes are copied up to a temporary readable tier.
        Now the read succeeds -- and you often pay a per-GB retrieval fee.

  This is the CLIFF. A read went from ~2 ms (Path B) to MINUTES. Fine for a rare
  audit; a disaster if a user-facing request lands here. Move 4 is how to not fall off it.

Path D — the lifecycle sweep (demote as data cools, delete at the end)

  Background job, runs continuously, throttled (L24: batch + don't starve live traffic):

   for each segment, by age:
     age crosses  7 days  -> COPY to WARM tier, update index tier=WARM, free HOT copy
     age crosses 90 days  -> COPY to COLD tier, update index tier=COLD, free WARM copy
     age crosses  1 year  -> COPY to ARCHIVE,   update index tier=ARCHIVE, free COLD copy
     age crosses  7 years -> EXPIRE: delete bytes + tombstone the index row (L20/L38)

  Two different actions, never conflated:
     TIER  = move to cheaper storage, STILL retrievable   (a cost decision)
     EXPIRE = delete permanently, gone forever            (a compliance decision)

  Order matters: write the new-tier copy and update the index BEFORE freeing the old
  copy -- or a crash mid-move loses the only copy (L20's "commit metadata last" instinct).

Path B is why you tier (90% of reads never leave hot); Path C is the price you agreed to pay for the cold 99.7% (rare reads are slow); Path D is the machinery that keeps the bill at $744 instead of $33,000 and honors the 7-year law. But look again at Path C: a read that should be milliseconds became minutes. If that ever lands on a path a user or a live system is waiting on, tiering just turned a cost win into an availability incident. That's the wall.

Move 4 — Find the first bottleneck: the retrieval-latency cliff

Every step down the ladder was ~10× slower — until the last one. Hot→warm→cold is milliseconds to a second: annoying but survivable. Cold→archive is a cliff: seconds become minutes to hours, because the bytes may be on spun-down disks or physical tape that has to be staged before it can be read. The naive reflex — "then keep everything warm so nothing is ever slow" — throws away the entire 44× saving (you're back to paying for 330 TB of fast disk). The real fix isn't to flatten the ladder; it's to make sure nothing latency-sensitive is ever parked below the tier its SLA allows.

Worked: tier to the access SLA, not to the age. Suppose 0.1% of old events are pulled by a support tool that must answer in < 1 second. If those live in archive, every such request falls off the cliff (minutes) and the tool is unusable. Keeping them in cold instead of archive costs $0.004 vs $0.001 per GB-month — 4× more for that slice — but stays under a second. You don't demote by age alone; you demote by the slowest retrieval the data's access pattern can tolerate. The support-critical slice has a tighter SLA, so it stops one rung higher. Age was only ever a proxy for temperature — when a known access pattern contradicts it, the access pattern wins.

Worked: keep the index hot even when the data is frozen (Lesson 20). The metadata plane is a few GB — keep all of it hot forever for pennies. Then "does event X exist / when / which tier?" is always ~1 ms, and only fetching X's payload from archive is slow. You never make a user wait minutes just to learn whether their record exists — only to read its cold bytes, and only when they actually ask for them.

Name the trade-off — a lower tier's storage saving vs its per-read retrieval tax. This is the trap that eats naive tiering. Cold and archive tiers are cheap to store but charge a per-GB fee every time you read (plus the latency). If you demote data by age while it's still read often, you save on storage and then lose it all — and more — to retrieval fees. The tier must follow the data's temperature (how often it's read), not just its age. Age is a good proxy for a write-once log; it's a terrible one for data that's old but still hot. The right mental model: cheap-to-store, expensive-to-read is a bet that you almost never read it. Only demote data that will honor that bet.

Past the cliff, three more walls decide whether your lifecycle design is sound:

  THE THREE WALLS AFTER THE CLIFF

  1. Retrieval FEES ambush a mis-tiered slice.  Demoting still-read data to cold/archive
        saves storage $ but pays a per-read fee each access -> can cost MORE than staying hot.
        -> Tier by TEMPERATURE (access frequency), not age alone. Watch read counts, not just
           the clock. A dashboard that scans "cold" every hour does NOT belong on a cold tier.

  2. The SMALL-OBJECT tax (Lesson 20 again).  Archiving billions of 300 B events one-by-one
        pays per-object request + minimum-size fees that dwarf the bytes.
        -> PACK cold data into large segments/containers before demoting (compact first),
           so you archive a few big blobs, not a billion tiny ones.

  3. Thrash / flapping thresholds (Lessons 2, 10).  A record parked right at a tier boundary,
        or a badly-chosen age cutoff, gets demoted then re-read then promoted, over and over --
        paying a move (and a retrieval fee) each way.
        -> Hysteresis: demote on age, but require a real cooling-off gap before promoting back;
           size thresholds against the real access histogram, not a guess.

The four traps

  • Trap 1 — tiering by age when age isn't the temperature. A write-once log cools predictably, so "demote by age" is safe. But data that's old yet still read (a popular legacy report, a reactivated account) will rack up retrieval fees and cliff-latency if you demote it on age alone. Sort by access frequency; use age only where it's a faithful proxy — and keep a promotion path for data that heats back up.
  • Trap 2 — confusing tiering with expiration. Tiering moves data to cheaper storage but keeps it retrievable; expiration deletes it forever. A policy bug that expires when it meant to tier destroys data you were legally required to keep; one that never expires keeps personal data past its lawful maximum. They run in the same sweep but answer to cost and law respectively — keep the two actions, and their thresholds, explicitly separate and audited.
  • Trap 3 — putting the index on a cold tier too. If finding a record is as slow as reading it, every existence check falls off the cliff. Keep the small, always-hot metadata plane (Lesson 20) so "where/whether/which tier" is always ~1 ms; let only the bulky payloads go cold. You should never wait minutes to learn a record exists.
  • Trap 4 — archiving a billion tiny objects individually, or freeing the old copy before the new one is committed. Cold tiers charge per request and per object, so pack small records into big segments before demoting (Lesson 20's small-file problem). And move data copy-new-then-free-old, updating the index before releasing the previous tier — a crash mid-move must never leave a record with zero copies (Lesson 20's "commit metadata last").

Where we landed

The immutable piles from Lessons 20, 29, and 38 all grow without bound, but their reads are skewed toward new data (Lesson 2), so paying one fast-disk price for every byte is waste. We took Lesson 38's event log — 5,000 events/s, 300 B each, kept 7 years — and tiered it. Estimate priced the two designs: ~330 TB all on hot SSD is ~$33,000/month, but 90% of reads touch the last 7 days (~1 TB, 0.3% of the data), so slicing by age across a HOT→WARM→COLD→ARCHIVE ladder (each ~5× cheaper, ~10× slower) drops the bill to ~$744/month — a ~44× cut, with the biggest slice of bytes (archive, 86%) being the smallest slice of the bill (38%). Model gave three parts: the fixed tier ladder, the temperature that decides a record's rung (age is only a proxy), and the background lifecycle policy that demotes as data cools and, at the 7-year line, expires it — with tiering (a cost move, still retrievable) kept strictly separate from expiration (a compliance move, gone forever), and the small index kept hot (Lesson 20) while only the payloads cool. Tracing showed writes always landing hot, hot reads served in ~2 ms (the 90% that make tiering pay), cold/archive reads issuing a restore job and waiting minutes, and the throttled sweep that moves copy-new-then-free-old and deletes at the end. The first bottleneck, the retrieval-latency cliff, is not fixed by flattening the ladder (that forfeits the 44×) but by tiering to the data's retrieval SLA and temperature rather than its age alone — keeping latency-sensitive or still-read data one rung higher, keeping the index hot, and packing small objects before archiving. The real lesson: storage is a hierarchy of price-vs-latency, and old data is cheap to keep only because it's rarely and un-urgently read — so let each byte fall to the cheapest tier its access pattern can tolerate, and never below it.
