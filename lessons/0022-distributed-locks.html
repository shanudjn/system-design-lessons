Distributed Locks & Leases: Two Workers, One Billing Job, and the Fencing Token

Lesson 21 ended with a hazard: two workers both believe they hold the lock on the nightly billing job, and both charge the customer. This lesson takes that exact scenario and traces it end to end. A lock is one worker's claim to be the only one touching a resource. But a process can freeze — a garbage-collection pause, a slow disk, a paused VM — for longer than anyone expects, and while it's frozen its lock quietly expires and someone else grabs it. When the frozen process wakes, it still thinks it holds the lock, and writes anyway. We'll estimate how to size a lock's expiry so it survives normal hiccups but frees a dead holder fast, model the lock as a lease plus a fencing token, trace the happy path, the crash, and the deadly GC-pause double-write, and hit the wall: the lock itself is a bottleneck and a single point of failure — which is why a distributed lock is a last resort. The trade underneath everything: safety vs liveness — never two writers, vs never stuck waiting on a dead one.

The one system we'll design

A fleet of five identical worker machines runs background jobs. One of those jobs is nightly billing: for each customer, charge their card once for last month's usage. It absolutely must run exactly once per customer — a second charge is real money taken twice and a very angry email.

We already know from Lesson 10 how to elect one leader to kick the job off. But leaders can hand off, workers can be duplicated by an over-eager scheduler, and a retry after a timeout (Lesson 07) can spin up a second attempt while the first is merely slow, not dead. So we want a hard guarantee at the resource itself: at most one worker is allowed to charge a given customer at a given moment. That guarantee is what a lock is supposed to buy. The whole lesson is about the ways a distributed lock quietly fails to buy it — and how to get the guarantee anyway.

  THE HAZARD WE'RE HERE TO KILL      (lock TTL = 15s)

  t=0s   Worker A: acquire lock "billing:cust-99"  -> granted, token 41
  t=0s   A starts charging customer 99 ...
  t=6s   A hits a STOP-THE-WORLD garbage-collection pause (frozen)
  t=15s  lock lease EXPIRES (A never renewed it — it's frozen)
  t=16s  Worker B: acquire lock "billing:cust-99"  -> granted, token 42
  t=16s  B charges customer 99  ($50)                       [1st charge]
  t=28s  A un-freezes, still BELIEVES it holds the lock,
         charges customer 99  ($50)                         [2nd charge!]

         => TWO writers held "the lock" at once. Money charged twice.

  The lock did its job at t=0. The bug is that A's belief outlived
  its lease, and nothing at the RESOURCE checked whether A's grant
  was still the current one.

A lock is a claim with an expiry date. The danger is never the lock working — it's a process acting on a lock it no longer holds, because it froze past the expiry and never noticed.

Words we'll earn before we lean on them

  • Mutual exclusion — the property that at most one worker is in the "critical section" (touching the shared resource) at any instant. The one thing a lock is supposed to guarantee.
  • Lock — a shared flag that one worker sets to say "mine"; others must wait until it's cleared. In one process this is easy; across machines it needs a shared store everyone agrees on.
  • Lease — a lock that expires on its own after a set time (its TTL), so a holder that dies without releasing can't hold it forever. The holder must keep renewing (heartbeating) to keep it.
  • TTL (time-to-live) — how long a lease is valid before it auto-expires. The single most important number to size.
  • Fencing token — a number the lock service hands out with every grant, strictly larger each time (41, 42, 43…). The protected resource remembers the highest it has seen and rejects any write carrying a smaller token. Same idea as Lesson 10's epoch, now applied to data.
  • Stop-the-world pause — a stretch where a process runs no code at all (a full garbage-collection cycle, a paused/migrating VM, a swapped-out page). It cannot renew its lease or check the clock while frozen — this is the whole problem.
  • Safety vs liveness — safety = "nothing bad ever happens" (never two writers). Liveness = "something good eventually happens" (a dead holder's lock is freed so work continues). A lock's design is a dial between them.
  • Clock skew — two machines' wall clocks disagree (drift, a leap second, a VM time-jump). Any safety argument that compares timestamps across machines is only as trustworthy as their clocks.

Move 1 — Estimate: how long should the lease live?

The TTL is the whole ballgame. Too long and a dead holder blocks everyone; too short and a live holder that hiccups gets evicted and the lock flaps (Lesson 10's leadership flap, one layer down). Let's size it with real numbers instead of a lazy default.

Worked: failover time from TTL and heartbeat. The holder renews the lease every heartbeat interval h; the lease is valid for TTL. Pick TTL = 3h so a holder survives two missed renewals (a dropped packet, a GC blip) before losing the lock.

• Choose h = 5s, so TTL = 3 × 5 = 15s.
• If the holder dies, its lease keeps the lock for at most the remaining TTL, so failover to another worker is ≤ 15s. That is the price of a crash: up to 15s of nobody making progress.
• Shrink it: h = 1s, TTL = 3s → failover ≤ 3s (Lesson 10's exact numbers: 1s heartbeat, 3-missed timeout). Faster recovery, but now any live pause longer than 3s falsely evicts a working holder.

TTL is failover speed. Smaller TTL = faster recovery from a real death, but a lower tolerance for a live holder's pauses.

Worked: the flap threshold. A live holder is falsely evicted whenever a pause between renewals exceeds the TTL. Suppose normal request hiccups and minor GC pauses are up to ~2s, but a full GC on a large heap can stall ~10–20s a few times a day.

• With TTL = 3s: every full-GC pause (>3s) evicts the holder mid-job → another worker grabs the lock, starts the same job, then the original wakes and collides. Constant flap and duplicate work.
• With TTL = 15s: the ~2s hiccups are absorbed (renewal resumes in time); only a rare >15s pause causes trouble. Fewer false evictions — but slower failover on a real crash, and the >15s pause is still possible. No finite TTL removes it.

Rule of thumb: TTL ≥ (worst pause you expect to tolerate) + (clock-skew margin). You cannot set it to "the worst pause that can ever happen," because that's unbounded — so a TTL alone can never be a safety guarantee, only a liveness knob.

Name the trade-off — safety vs liveness, dialed by the TTL. A long TTL protects a live-but-slow holder (fewer false evictions) but makes a truly dead holder block progress for longer. A short TTL recovers from death fast but evicts hiccuping-live holders, causing flap and duplicate work. Crucially, neither end makes the double-write impossible — a pause longer than the TTL always can happen. The TTL tunes liveness; safety has to come from somewhere else. That "somewhere else" is the fencing token.

Move 2 — Model: a lock is a lease, and safety is a fencing token

Build the lock in three layers, each fixing a hole in the one before.

Model A — the naive lock: set-if-absent in a shared store

Mutual exclusion across machines needs one place everyone agrees on — a lock service (Redis, etcd, ZooKeeper, or a database row). Acquire is a single atomic operation: "set this key to my id, but only if it doesn't already exist." In Redis that's SET billing:cust-99 A NX PX 15000 — NX = only if absent (so two workers can't both win), PX 15000 = auto-expire in 15,000 ms. Release deletes the key.

The atomic set-if-absent is doing the real work: even if A and B fire it at the same microsecond, the store serializes them and exactly one gets "OK." That much is fine. The hole is what happens after A wins and then stalls — covered next.

Model B — make it a lease: auto-expiry so a dead holder can't block forever

Without expiry, if A wins the lock and then its machine dies, the key stays set forever and no one ever bills customer 99 again — a permanent liveness failure. The PX 15000 above already made it a lease: the lock frees itself after the TTL even if the holder never releases it. A living holder keeps it by renewing every h seconds (re-setting the expiry), which is the heartbeat from Lesson 10 applied to a lock.

This buys liveness (a dead holder is cleaned up in ≤ TTL) but, as Move 1 showed, it creates the safety hole: a live-but-frozen holder's lease expires, someone else acquires, and now two workers believe they hold it. The lease fixed "held forever by the dead" and opened "held at once by two." We need one more layer.

One subtlety worth naming: release must check ownership. If A's lease expires and B acquires, and then A wakes and blindly runs DELETE billing:cust-99, A just deleted B's lock — and now a third worker C can acquire while B is still working. So release isn't a plain delete; it's "delete only if the value is still mine" (a compare-and-delete, done atomically with a small script). This is Lesson 06's compare-and-set race, reappearing at the lock layer.

Model C — the fencing token: let the resource reject a stale writer

Here is the key move, and the one people skip. Every time the lock service grants the lock, it also returns a fencing token: a counter that only ever goes up — 41, then 42, then 43. Whoever holds the lock stamps every write to the protected resource with their token. The resource (the billing ledger, the storage system) remembers the highest token it has ever accepted and rejects any write with a lower one.

  FENCING TOKEN   monotonic number handed out with each grant

  lock service ---- grant token 41 ---> Worker A
  lock service ---- grant token 42 ---> Worker B   (after A's lease expired)

  Billing ledger keeps: highest_token_seen

     B writes charge, token 42  ->  42 >= highest(41) -> ACCEPT, set highest=42
     A wakes, writes charge, token 41  ->  41 <  highest(42) -> REJECT

  A's stale write is FENCED OUT at the resource, even though A still
  *thinks* it holds the lock. Safety no longer depends on A noticing
  it froze, or on any clock being correct.

Why this is the real fix: the double-write bug happened because A never learned its grant was stale. You cannot force a frozen process to notice anything. So instead of trusting the writer to check, you make the resource the judge. The resource doesn't care about wall clocks, TTLs, or who thinks they hold the lock — it just enforces "tokens only go up." A grant that has been superseded carries a smaller number and is rejected, deterministically. This is exactly Lesson 10's epoch/fencing idea, now protecting data writes instead of leadership actions.

Name the trade-off — trust the writer vs trust the resource. A TTL-only lock trusts each holder to stop acting the moment its lease ends — impossible to guarantee, because a frozen process acts on stale beliefs the instant it wakes. A fencing token moves the check to the resource, which is always awake and needs no clock. The cost: the resource must support conditional writes keyed on a monotonic token (a compare-and-set on highest_token). If it can't — a dumb blob store, a third-party API with no such guard — then you cannot make the lock truly safe, and that limitation is itself a design signal.

Move 3 — Trace the paths

Three journeys through the same lock: the happy path, the clean crash, and the dangerous freeze that fencing saves.

Path 1 — happy path (renew, work, release)

  t=0.0s  A: SET billing:cust-99 A NX PX 15000  -> OK, token 41
  t=0.0s  A begins charging customer 99, stamping writes with 41
  t=5.0s  A: renew (re-set PX 15000)             -> OK (still A's)
  t=8.0s  A: write charge to ledger, token 41    -> 41>=highest -> ACCEPT
  t=8.2s  A: release = "DELETE if value==A"      -> OK, key gone
          Next worker can acquire; it gets token 42.

Nothing exotic: acquire, heartbeat to hold it, do the work stamped with the token, release with an ownership check. Total lock hold ~8s, comfortably inside the 15s TTL, renewed once along the way.

Path 2 — clean crash (the lease earns its keep)

  t=0s   A: acquire -> OK, token 41; starts work
  t=3s   A's machine hard-crashes (power loss). No release.
  t=3s   ... key still set, but its PX expiry is ticking ...
  t=15s  lease EXPIRES automatically (nobody renewed)
  t=16s  B: acquire -> OK, token 42; re-runs the job safely
         Failover took <= TTL = 15s. No human paged.

This is why the lease exists. A dead holder cannot release, so without auto-expiry the lock would be stuck forever. The TTL bounds the damage of a crash to a ≤15s stall, then the fleet self-heals. (For the job to be safely re-run, it must be idempotent — Lesson 13 — since A may have completed part of it before dying.)

Path 3 — the freeze (fencing turns a double-charge into a rejected write)

  t=0s   A: acquire -> OK, token 41; starts charging customer 99
  t=6s   A enters a 22s stop-the-world GC pause (frozen solid)
  t=15s  A's lease EXPIRES (A can't renew — it's frozen)
  t=16s  B: acquire -> OK, token 42; charges $50, ledger highest=42
  t=28s  A un-freezes. It has NO idea time passed. It believes it
         still holds the lock and issues its charge, token 41.
  t=28s  ledger: 41 < highest(42)  ->  REJECT A's write.
         Customer 99 charged exactly once. Crisis averted.

Walk the counterfactual: without the token, A's t=28s write lands and customer 99 is charged twice. The lease alone did not save us — it actively caused the overlap by expiring under A. What saved us is that the ledger refused a write whose grant had been superseded. The lease bounds how long a dead holder blocks; the fencing token bounds what a stale holder can do. You need both.

Name the trade-off — you can't prevent the overlap, only neutralize its effect. No lock design can stop A from waking up mid-job with stale beliefs; freezes are physics, not bugs. The fencing token doesn't prevent the two-holder window — it makes the window harmless by ensuring the loser's writes are refused. Prevention is impossible; neutralization is achievable. That reframing is the whole art of designing for partial failure (Lesson 07).

Move 4 — Find the first bottleneck: the lock is a serial gate, a SPOF, and clock-dependent

Suppose fencing works perfectly. The lock is still the most expensive tool in the box, for three reasons — which is why the honest advice is avoid the lock if you possibly can.

Worked: the serialization ceiling. A lock forces work through one holder at a time. If the critical section (acquire → do → release) takes t = 5 ms, then a single lock key can pass at most 1 / 0.005 s = 200 operations/second — no matter how many workers or CPUs you throw at it. To push 10M billing charges through one shared lock: 10,000,000 / 200 = 50,000 s ≈ 13.9 hours. The lock turned a parallel fleet back into a single-file line.

The fix is to not share one lock: lock per customer (billing:cust-99, not billing:all), so a million customers run on a million independent locks in parallel. Better still, shard the work by key (Lesson 03) so each worker owns a disjoint slice and needs no cross-worker lock at all. The coarser the lock, the lower the ceiling.

Two more walls beyond throughput:

  • The lock service is a single point of failure. Everyone must agree on who holds the lock, so the lock lives in one authoritative place. If that place is a single Redis node and it dies, either locking stops (liveness lost) or you fail over to a replica that hasn't yet heard about the latest grant and hands the "same" lock to a second holder (safety lost — replication lag, Lesson 06). A correctness-grade lock therefore wants a consensus-backed store (etcd/ZooKeeper on Raft/Paxos, Lesson 10), which costs a round of agreement per grant — slower than a single node, and now the lock's availability is capped by the consensus quorum's.
  • Clock skew makes TTL reasoning fragile. The lease "expires in 15 s" is measured on someone's clock. If the lock service and the workers disagree about how fast time passes — NTP drift, a VM live-migration that jumps the clock forward, a leap second — a holder can think its lease is still valid seconds after the service considers it expired. This is exactly why safety must not rest on synchronized clocks: the fencing token needs no clock at all, only "bigger than last time." Trust the monotonic counter, not the wall clock.

The escape hatch — often you don't need a lock at all. Ask what the lock is protecting against:

• Duplicate effect is the real fear (double charge)? Make the operation idempotent (Lesson 13): give each charge a client-chosen idempotency key and let the ledger dedupe. Then a duplicate run is harmless and the lock becomes an optimization, not a correctness requirement. This is the fencing idea generalized — the resource, not the caller, enforces once-only.
• Contention on shared state? Route each key to a single owner (consistent hashing, Lesson 04; leader per shard, Lesson 10) so there's exactly one writer by construction — no lock to acquire, because concurrency was designed out.
• Just avoiding wasted duplicate work (efficiency, not correctness)? A lease-only lock with no fencing is fine — an occasional double-run wastes CPU but corrupts nothing.

Locks come in two flavors: for efficiency (a double-run is merely wasteful → a plain lease is enough) and for correctness (a double-run corrupts data → you need fencing, or better, idempotency so the lock isn't load-bearing). Know which one you have before you reach for a lock.

Name the last trade-off — pessimistic locking vs optimistic idempotency. A lock is pessimistic: it serializes access up front so conflicts can't happen — safe, but it caps throughput, adds a SPOF, and leans on clocks. Idempotency (and single-writer routing) is optimistic: let work proceed in parallel and make duplicates harmless at the resource — faster and no shared gate, but it demands the resource support a dedup/compare-and-set guard. Reach for the lock only when you can't make the resource idempotent and can't route to a single owner. It is the last resort, not the first.

Where we landed

We took one billing job that must charge each customer exactly once and traced every way a distributed lock lets two workers charge them twice. Estimate sized the lease: heartbeat h = 5s, TTL = 3h = 15s gives a ≤15s failover on a crash, while a shorter 3s TTL recovers faster but false-evicts any live holder that pauses >3s — the flap; and no finite TTL can survive an unbounded stop-the-world pause, so the TTL is a liveness knob, never a safety guarantee. Model built the lock in three layers: an atomic set-if-absent (one winner), a lease that auto-expires so a dead holder can't block forever (but which opens a two-holder window), and the load-bearing fix — a fencing token, a monotonic number the resource uses to reject any write from a superseded grant, needing no clock and no cooperation from the frozen writer (Lesson 10's epoch, applied to data). Tracing the freeze showed a 22s GC pause expire A's lease, B acquire with token 42 and charge, and A wake at t=28s to have its token-41 write fenced out at the ledger — a double-charge turned into a rejected write. The first bottleneck was the lock itself: one shared lock serializes to 1/5ms = 200 ops/s (13.9 h for 10M charges) → use fine-grained/sharded locks; it's a SPOF that wants consensus (slower) to stay safe under replica failover; and its TTL math is only as good as the clocks — which is why fencing avoids clocks entirely. The real lesson: a distributed lock is a last resort. Prefer making the operation idempotent or routing each key to a single writer, so duplicates are harmless and no shared gate caps your throughput — and when you must lock, never trust the TTL for safety, trust the fencing token.

• Multi-region / active-active: we've kept safety by forcing one writer at a time. Now put writers on different continents and let both accept writes locally for speed. When a shopper's cart is edited in Virginia and Frankfurt at once, there's no single lock to serialize them — so you need conflict resolution: last-write-wins (and what it silently drops), CRDTs that merge cleanly (recap L06/L11), and the write-latency-vs-availability tax. The trade shifts from safety vs liveness to local writes vs global order.
