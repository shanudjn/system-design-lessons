Health Checks & Graceful Degradation: Tell "Up" From "Useful"

Lesson 75 made the record of what happened trustworthy. Now the live question: is the system even healthy enough to keep taking traffic — and who decides? A load balancer (Lesson 42) constantly asks each server "are you OK?" and routes only to the ones that say yes. That single yes/no answer — the health check — is one of the most dangerous lines of code in the whole system, because it decides whether a server gets traffic or gets pulled out of rotation. Get it wrong and you turn a papercut into a beheading: we'll open with a real outage where a recommendations service worth ~2% of revenue took down 100% of checkout — because the health check asked the wrong question. We'll estimate exactly how a shared dependency's blip becomes a fleet-wide outage in seconds. We'll model the two health checks that answer two different questions (shallow "am I alive?" vs deep "can I serve right now?", Lesson 34's liveness vs readiness), the rule for what a check may and may not touch, and graceful degradation — ranking features so you can drop the garnish to save the meal (Lesson 28). We'll trace a healthy request, a non-critical dependency dying, a critical one dying, and an overload. And we'll find the first bottleneck: a health check that depends on a shared dependency converts a partial failure into a total one, plus the per-dependency fail-open vs fail-closed choice that is really a question about which mistake you can live with. The trade under all of it: availability of the core vs completeness of the experience.

The one system we'll build

One product-page + checkout service, the same fleet from Lesson 27: 286 servers behind a load balancer, serving 40,000 requests/second. Rendering a product page pulls from several backends: the orders/checkout database (place an order, read a cart), an inventory service (stock counts), a reviews service, and a recommendations service ("customers also bought…"). Some of these are essential — no checkout DB, no sale. Some are garnish — no recommendations, the page is a hair less rich but still sells. The load balancer probes each server's /health endpoint every couple of seconds and removes any server that fails. Our job: define what /health should answer, and what the service should do when a backend is sick, so that the failure of a non-essential part never takes down the essential part. The adversary here isn't an attacker or a traffic spike — it's a well-meaning health check that's too eager to say "no."

  Says "unhealthy" twice in a row -> pulled from rotation.

The one idea the whole lesson turns on: "up" and "useful" are different questions, and a health check must answer only about this one instance's ability to serve — never about a dependency that every instance shares. The moment a check's answer depends on something shared, a single blip makes all instances answer "no" at the same time, and a mechanism designed to protect availability (route away from a bad box) instead destroys it (route away from every box at once). Everything below is about keeping the health signal honest about the right thing, and about degrading feature-by-feature instead of failing all-or-nothing.

Words we'll earn before we lean on them

  • Health check — an endpoint (e.g. GET /health) a load balancer or orchestrator probes periodically to decide whether to send an instance traffic (or restart it).
  • Liveness check (shallow) — "is this process alive and responsive?" Answered from local state only, no downstream calls. A failure means restart the process (Lesson 34).
  • Readiness check (deep) — "can this instance serve a real request right now?" May check local critical resources. A failure means remove from the traffic pool — but do NOT restart (Lesson 34).
  • Critical vs non-critical dependency — for a given request, a critical dependency is one you cannot serve the core function without (checkout DB); a non-critical one only enriches the response (recommendations).
  • Correlated failure — when many instances fail at the same moment for the same reason (a shared dependency), instead of independently. The thing health checks must not amplify.
  • Graceful degradation — serving a reduced-but-useful response when a part is unavailable, instead of failing the whole request. The opposite of all-or-nothing.
  • Load shedding / feature tiers — under stress, dropping the least-important work first (turn off recommendations to keep checkout alive, Lesson 28).
  • Fail-open vs fail-closed — when a dependency is unavailable: fail open = proceed without it (serve degraded); fail closed = refuse the request. A per-dependency choice.
  • Panic mode / fail-open LB — a load balancer that, when too large a fraction of the pool is "unhealthy," routes to everyone anyway — because a stale route beats no route (Lesson 34's fail-static).

Move 1 — Estimate: how a 2%-of-revenue blip becomes a 100% outage

Numbers first, because they show the disaster is fast and total, not gradual. The load balancer probes each of the 286 servers every 2 s, and marks a server unhealthy after 2 consecutive failed probes (~4 s). Now suppose the recommendations service has a 10-second latency blip — its p99 jumps to ~4 s (a GC pause, a deploy, a hot shard; Lesson 27's knee).

The naive deep health check. Someone wrote /health to be "thorough": it verifies the service can reach all its backends, including a GET recommendations/ping with a 5 s timeout, and returns 503 if any backend is unreachable. Watch what the blip does:

  • The recommendations service is shared by all 286 servers. During the blip, every server's probe to it hangs and fails — at the same time, because it's the same backend.
  • Within ~2 probe cycles (~4–6 s), all 286 servers have failed twice → the LB marks every one unhealthy → the healthy pool is empty.
  • An empty pool means the LB has nowhere to route: 100% of 40,000 req/s now get 502/503. Over the ~12 s it takes the blip to clear and servers to be re-added, that's 40,000 × 12 = 480,000 failed requests — including every in-progress checkout.

A feature that contributes ~2% of revenue just caused a 100% checkout outage. The recommendations service never went down for its own users in a way that mattered — it got slow for 10 seconds. The health check manufactured the outage by asking a question whose answer was the same "no" on all 286 boxes at once.

The honest health check. Now /health answers only "is this process able to serve?" — it checks local liveness and, at most, that this instance can get a connection from its own checkout-DB pool. It does not call recommendations. Recommendations is instead a request-time call with a 200 ms timeout + circuit breaker (Lesson 7); on failure the page simply omits the widget.

  • During the same blip: every request's recommendation call times out at 200 ms (or the breaker is already open, returning instantly), the widget is hidden, and the page renders in ~150 ms.
  • Health checks never touched recommendations → 0 servers removed → 0 failed requests. The blast radius is "100% of pages missing one widget for 10 s" instead of "100% of pages failing."

The scoreboard: 480,000 failed requests vs 0. Same underlying blip. The entire difference is what the health check asked and what the request did when a non-critical part was slow.

Why "check everything to be safe" is exactly backwards: a thorough deep check feels safer — surely a server that can't reach a backend shouldn't get traffic? But "thorough" quietly assumes each server's failures are independent. A shared dependency makes them correlated: all 286 fail together, so removing "the bad ones" removes all of them, and you've amplified a partial degradation into a total outage. Safety comes from the check being narrow and local — reporting only what makes this instance uniquely unable to serve — plus handling shared-dependency trouble at request time with timeouts, breakers, and degradation.

Move 2 — Model: two questions, one rule, and a feature ranking

Two checks, because there are two decisions

A load balancer or orchestrator makes two different decisions about an instance, so it needs two different answers (Lesson 34's liveness-vs-readiness, now on the serving path):

  • Liveness (shallow): "Is this process alive and its event loop responsive?" Answer from pure local state — return 200 if the process can run a trivial handler. Deps are off-limits. A failure means the process is wedged (deadlock, OOM spiral) → restart it. If liveness probed a dependency, a dependency blip would trigger a restart storm of healthy processes — the worst possible reaction (Lesson 26's reconnect storm).
  • Readiness (deep, but disciplined): "Can this instance serve a real request right now?" It may check local, instance-specific critical state: is my DB connection pool exhausted? am I still warming a cache? am I draining for shutdown (Lesson 31)? A failure means take me out of the pool — but don't restart me, because there's nothing to fix by restarting; I'll say ready again when I recover.

Swap the two and you get classic outages: a liveness check that fails on a slow dependency crash-loops warm servers; a readiness check that never fails routes traffic to a server whose thread pool is wedged (accepting connections, answering nothing — Lesson 7). Two questions, two answers, two reactions.

The one rule: what a health check may touch

Here is the rule that would have prevented the Move-1 outage, stated plainly:

A health check may only report on state that is local and unique to this instance. It must never let its answer depend on a dependency shared by the rest of the fleet.

Because: if the failing thing is unique to one instance (its own wedged pool, its own bad disk), removing that instance helps — traffic shifts to the healthy majority. If the failing thing is shared (the recommendations service, a common database, a downstream everyone calls), removing "the unhealthy instances" removes all of them, and you've converted a degradation into an outage while giving the LB nowhere to route. So classify every dependency per endpoint, and handle the shared/critical ones at request time, not in the health check.

Rank the features: core vs garnish

Graceful degradation needs a priority order — you can only "drop the least important thing" if you've decided what that is. Rank the product page's parts by how much of the goal (a completed purchase) depends on them:

FeatureTierIf its backend is down…Fail-open or -closed?
Place order / chargeCorethe sale itself; cannot proceed without itfail closed
Cart / priceCoreessential to a correct purchasefail closed
Inventory countImportantshow item, allow order, reconcile later (Lesson 32)fail open, carefully
ReviewsNicehide the reviews section, page still sellsfail open
RecommendationsGarnishomit the widget entirelyfail open

The ranking makes the trade explicit: recommendations add maybe 2% incremental revenue; checkout is the revenue. Trading a total checkout outage to keep recommendations rendering is the worst deal in the building. Degrade top-down: shed garnish first, core last (ideally never).

Fail-open vs fail-closed: which mistake can you live with?

When a dependency is unavailable you must pick a default, and the choice is a correctness/security question, not a technical one: fail open (proceed without it) when proceeding-wrong is cheap and refusing is expensive; fail closed (refuse) when proceeding-wrong is catastrophic — money moved, security bypassed, data corrupted.

  • Recommendations down → fail open. Worst case of proceeding: a slightly plainer page. Worst case of refusing: a lost sale. Open wins.
  • Payment authorization down → fail closed. Worst case of proceeding: you ship goods that were never paid for. Never fail open on money — refuse and retry (Lesson 7/13).
  • Fraud / risk check down → usually fail closed (or risk-based: fail open for a $5 order, fail closed for a $5,000 one). Proceeding without a fraud check is exactly the hole fraud walks through.
  • Auth / session service down → fail closed, always. A security check that fails open treats unauthenticated users as authenticated — the most dangerous default there is.

The rule: default to the side whose worst case you can survive. Get it backwards and you either cause needless outages (fail-closed on garnish) or hand out free goods and open security holes (fail-open on money/auth).

Move 3 — Trace the paths

Follow four situations through the fleet: a normal request, a garnish dependency dying, a core one dying, and an overload.

Path A — a healthy request

   GET /product/88f      LB routes to a READY server
   [1] server assembles page: cart + inventory + reviews + recs
   [2] each backend call has its own timeout + circuit breaker (L7)
   [3] all return -> full page in ~150 ms
   Meanwhile /health (readiness) answers only: "my process is live,
   my checkout-DB pool has free connections, I'm not draining." 200 OK.
   -> health check made ZERO downstream calls.

Path B — recommendations dies (non-critical, shared)

   Recommendations p99 -> 4 s for 10 s (the Move-1 blip).
   Request time: rec call hits its 200 ms timeout; after a few
     failures the CIRCUIT BREAKER opens -> instant "no recs" (L7).
   Response: page renders WITHOUT the widget (fail OPEN). ~150 ms.
   Health check: never calls recommendations -> stays 200 OK
     -> 0 servers removed -> 0 failed requests.
   Blast radius: 100% of pages miss one widget for 10 s. Nobody
     notices they didn't get a "you may also like."

Path C — the checkout DB dies (core)

   Case C1: ONE instance can't get a DB connection (its pool wedged).
     -> readiness on THAT box goes red -> LB removes just it ->
        its ~140 req/s shift to the healthy 285. Correct: local + unique.

   Case C2: the DB is unreachable for EVERYONE (primary down).
     -> if readiness ejects on DB-unreachable, ALL 286 go red ->
        empty pool -> total outage AND nowhere to route (the Move-1
        shape again, now on a CRITICAL dep).
     Better: readiness reflects "this box's own pool," not the shared
       DB's health. Fleet stays "ready"; requests that NEED writes
       fail CLOSED (honest 503 for checkout), while reads served from
       cache/replica degrade OPEN. The LB's PANIC MODE also helps:
       if >50% of the pool is unhealthy, route to all anyway --
       a stale route beats no route (L34 fail-static, L42).

Path D — overload: shed by tier

   Traffic spikes past capacity (L27 knee, L28 backpressure).
   Don't fail whole requests randomly. Shed by feature TIER:
     tier 3 (recs)     -> stop computing first (frees CPU)
     tier 2 (reviews)  -> then drop
     tier 1 (core)     -> protect: checkout keeps working
   Also shed by request CLASS (L28): drop retries before first-tries,
     free-tier before paid, stale-in-queue via LIFO. Never shed the
     health check itself, or you eject the fleet under load.

Path A shows the discipline: request-time calls are individually guarded; the health check stays local and silent. Path B is the payoff — a shared, non-critical blip degrades one widget instead of failing everything. Path C is the subtle one: eject a uniquely-broken instance (C1), but never eject the whole fleet for a shared failure (C2) — there's nowhere to route, so serve what you can and fail the truly-impossible parts closed. Path D shows degradation as a dial you turn under load, tier by tier. Move 4 names why the health check is the fulcrum of all of it.

Move 4 — Find the first bottleneck: the health check is a correlated-failure amplifier

The first bottleneck — a shared dependency in the health check converts a partial failure into a total one. This is the whole outage in one sentence. A health check exists to protect availability by steering traffic away from a bad instance. But that only works when "bad" is uncorrelated across instances — one wedged box out of 286. The instant the check's answer depends on something shared (a common service, a common DB, a downstream everyone calls), a single blip makes all 286 answer "unhealthy" simultaneously, the LB empties the pool, and the safety mechanism becomes the outage. The fix is the Move-2 rule: a health check reports only local, instance-unique state. Shared and critical dependencies are handled at request time — timeout, circuit breaker (Lesson 7), and degrade — never by fleet ejection. Restated as the trade: a check that's too deep couples you to shared failures; a check that's too shallow ("process is up") routes traffic to a box that accepts connections but can't actually serve (its thread pool wedged, Lesson 7). The sweet spot is narrow-but-real: check what's local and uniquely yours, nothing more.

The second wall — when a critical dependency fails for everyone, ejecting the fleet is useless. Health checks assume there's a healthy majority to route to. When the failure is genuinely fleet-wide (the primary DB is down), removing all instances doesn't route around the problem — there's no "around." Worse, the empty pool is now a second failure of its own. Two defenses: (1) keep readiness reporting this instance's own resources so the fleet doesn't self-eject on a shared outage; let individual requests fail closed only for the parts that truly can't proceed, while degrading the rest open (serve cached reads, queue writes for later — Lesson 32/33). (2) A load balancer panic mode (Lesson 42): when more than some fraction (say 50%) of the pool reports unhealthy, the LB assumes the health signal itself is lying and routes to everyone — a stale route beats no route (Lesson 34's fail-static, chosen here for exactly the same reason). The trade named: aggressive ejection (great for isolated faults) vs fail-static tolerance (survives correlated faults) — you want both, gated on how much of the fleet is affected.

The third wall — fail-open vs fail-closed is a per-dependency decision you must make on purpose. There is no global default that's right. Fail-open on recommendations saves a sale; fail-open on payment auth ships free goods; fail-open on the auth service lets anyone in. Conversely, fail-closed on a garnish feature re-creates the Move-1 outage voluntarily. So for every dependency, ask: which mistake can I survive — proceeding wrongly, or refusing? Default to the survivable side. The trade named: completeness of the experience (fail open, keep serving) vs correctness/security (fail closed, refuse) — decided dependency by dependency, never fleet-wide.

The fourth wall — degraded modes that are never exercised rot, and silent degradation hides real problems. A "serve the page without recommendations" path that only ever runs during a real incident is code you're testing in production during your worst hour. It will have the bug you didn't catch. So exercise degradation deliberately — Lesson 51's chaos engineering: regularly kill the recommendations dependency in a controlled way and confirm the page still sells. And degradation must be observable, not silent: emit a metric every time you drop a feature or trip a breaker, and alarm on the rate (Lesson 17). A system that degrades quietly looks healthy on the "is checkout up?" dashboard while quietly serving a worse product to everyone — you've traded a loud outage for a silent, invisible one, which is sometimes worse because nobody fixes what nobody sees. The trade named: the resilience of graceful degradation vs the risk that "still up" masks "quietly broken."

So the honest picture of health checks and degradation: a health check is the tiny yes/no that decides whether an instance gets traffic, and its danger is that it can amplify a correlated failure into a total outage — a recommendations blip worth 2% of revenue removing 100% of the fleet, 480,000 failed requests vs 0, decided entirely by whether the check asked about local state or about a shared dependency. You keep it honest with two checks for two decisions (liveness → restart, readiness → remove-don't-restart, Lesson 34), one rule (report only local, instance-unique state; handle shared/critical deps at request time with timeouts + breakers, Lesson 7), a feature ranking (core vs garnish) that lets you shed top-down (Lesson 28), and a per-dependency fail-open/closed choice made on the "which mistake can I survive?" question. The first bottleneck is the correlated-failure amplifier itself; around it, ejecting the fleet is useless when the failure is fleet-wide (→ fail-static + LB panic mode, Lesson 42/34), fail-open/closed must be chosen per dependency (money and auth fail closed, garnish fails open), and degraded modes rot and hide unless exercised and alarmed (Lesson 51/17). Every choice names its price.

The deepest point: "up" is not "useful," and a resilient system is one that can tell the difference about itself in real time — and admit it's sick without falling over. A brittle system has one state (working) and one failure (down); everything is all-or-nothing, so any part's failure is the whole system's failure. A resilient system has a spectrum: fully healthy, missing recommendations, missing reviews, read-only, checkout-only — each a deliberate, tested, observable step down that keeps the core alive while the edges wobble. The health check is where the system decides which step it's on, and the degradation tiers are the steps. Notice this is assembled from pieces you already own — timeouts and circuit breakers (L7), load shedding and backpressure (L28), liveness/readiness and fail-static (L34), the load balancer's routing and panic mode (L42), chaos testing (L51), observability (L17). The new idea is only the stance: design for the failure of your parts, and rank them so that when — not if — one fails, you drop the garnish and keep serving the meal. The standing tension: availability of the core vs completeness of the experience.

The four traps

  • Trap 1 — a health check that probes a shared dependency. One blip makes all instances fail together and the LB empties the pool. Keep the check local and instance-unique; guard shared deps at request time with timeouts and breakers.
  • Trap 2 — conflating liveness and readiness. A liveness check that fails on a slow dependency crash-loops healthy processes; a readiness check that never fails routes traffic to a wedged box. Liveness → restart on local wedge only; readiness → remove (don't restart) when this instance can't serve.
  • Trap 3 — getting fail-open/closed backwards. Fail-open on payments or auth ships free goods and opens security holes; fail-closed on garnish causes needless outages. Decide per dependency by which mistake you can survive.
  • Trap 4 — a degraded mode that's never tested or never alarmed. The path you only run during an incident has the bug you didn't catch, and silent degradation makes "quietly broken" look like "up." Chaos-test degradation regularly and emit a metric every time you shed or trip a breaker.

Where we landed

We taught one fleet to tell "up" from "useful." Estimate exposed the danger: with 286 servers probed every 2 s, a naive deep health check that pings a shared recommendations service turns a 10-second latency blip into an empty pool and 480,000 failed requests — a 100% checkout outage caused by a feature worth ~2% of revenue — while an honest local check yields 0 failures for the same blip. Model gave the discipline: two checks for two decisions (liveness → restart a wedged process, readiness → remove-but-don't-restart an instance that can't serve, Lesson 34); one rule (a health check reports only local, instance-unique state — shared and critical deps are handled at request time with timeout + circuit breaker, Lesson 7); a feature ranking (core checkout vs garnish recommendations) so you can shed top-down (Lesson 28); and a per-dependency fail-open vs fail-closed choice made on "which mistake can I survive?" (garnish fails open; money and auth fail closed). Tracing showed a healthy request making zero health-check calls, a non-critical blip degrading one widget instead of failing the page, a critical dependency ejecting one uniquely-broken box (good) but never the whole fleet on a shared outage (fail-static + panic mode instead), and an overload shed tier by tier. The first bottleneck: the health check is a correlated-failure amplifier — depend on something shared and one blip removes everyone; around it, ejecting a fleet-wide failure is useless (→ fail-static + LB panic mode, L42/34), fail-open/closed is a per-dependency correctness/security call, and degraded modes rot and hide unless exercised and alarmed (L51/17). The deepest point: "up" is not "useful," and resilience is having a spectrum of degraded-but-serving states instead of one all-or-nothing switch — dropping the garnish to keep serving the meal, built almost entirely from pieces you already own. The standing tension: availability of the core vs completeness of the experience.
