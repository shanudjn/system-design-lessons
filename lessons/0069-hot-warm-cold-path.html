Hot / Warm / Cold Path: One Event, Three Destinations, and the Serving-vs-Analytics Split

Lesson 68 got a single order.placed event to flow reliably through one chain of services. This lesson asks a different question about the very same event: once it's safely captured, who reads it, and how differently? Three questions land on that one order, and they could not be more unalike. A shopper refreshing a page asks "where's my order?" — a point lookup that must answer in milliseconds and be almost perfectly fresh. An ops dashboard asks "revenue in the last minute, per product?" — a live aggregate that must be fresh to within seconds. A finance analyst asks "revenue by region by day, last quarter?" — a huge historical scan that can be minutes stale but must be cheap over years of data. We'll show the mistake that ruins systems — trying to answer all three from one store — and the fix: fan the one event out into three read models, a hot serving path, a warm speed path, and a cold batch path, then put a router in front that sends each query to the shape that fits it. We'll estimate why the three questions demand three different physical shapes; model the one-write-many-reads fan-out (Lesson 37's CQRS taken to its conclusion) and what "consistent enough" means when the three views disagree; trace each query to its path and watch the three views drift and reconcile; and find the first bottleneck, which is that you are now maintaining the same logic in three pipelines that can silently disagree. The trade under every choice: freshness & per-query latency vs duplicated pipelines & cost.

The one system we'll build

One order.placed event — the reliable, effectively-once event Lesson 68 just delivered — arrives on a durable log (Lesson 9) at 2,000 orders/sec (peak ~10,000/s), each ~1 KB. Downstream, three completely different readers want that data. Our job is to serve all three well at once, without any of them getting in the others' way. The naïve instinct — "put every order in one big database and let everyone query it" — is exactly the instinct this lesson kills, because the three queries don't just want different answers; they want different machines. So we build one write that fans out into three purpose-built read models, and a read-path router that knows which one to ask.

The one idea the whole lesson turns on: there is no single store that is at once low-latency for point reads, fresh for streaming aggregates, and cheap for scans over years of data. Those are three different physical shapes with three different, often opposite, failure modes (Lesson 29 proved a big analytics scan will evict a serving store's hot cache and trip its latency knee). So you stop looking for the one perfect store. You take the one event you already captured, and you write it down three times, once in each shape — a serving store for "where's my order," a speed layer for "what's happening right now," a warehouse for "what happened over the last three years" — and you put a small router in front that reads the shape each query needs. Everything else in this lesson — the fan-out, "consistent enough," the drift between views, the duplicated-logic tax — falls out of that one move.

Words we'll earn before we lean on them

  • Serving store (hot path) — an online database tuned for fast point reads/writes of current state ("give me order O-77"). Low latency, fresh, expensive per byte, short retention. A key-value or document store (Lesson 48).
  • Speed layer (warm path) — a stream processor (Lesson 64) keeping live aggregates over recent events ("revenue in the last minute"). Fresh within seconds, bounded state, medium retention.
  • Batch / analytics warehouse (cold path) — a columnar store (Lesson 29/67) holding the full history for deep, flexible queries. Cheap per byte, huge retention, but higher latency and stale by a batch interval.
  • Read model / materialized view — a copy of the data pre-shaped for one kind of query. Each of the three paths above is a read model built from the same event.
  • CQRS (Command Query Responsibility Segregation, Lesson 37) — separating the write path (one source of truth) from the read paths (many purpose-built views). This lesson is CQRS with three read models instead of one.
  • Fan-out — one written event feeding many independent consumers, each building its own view (Lesson 15/33).
  • Freshness / staleness — how far behind the source a view is. The serving view lags ~1 s; the warehouse lags a whole batch interval.
  • Batch interval — how often the cold path re-loads new data (e.g. every 15 min). It sets the warehouse's staleness floor.
  • Read-path router — the piece that classifies an incoming query (point? live aggregate? historical scan?) and sends it to the right path.
  • Lambda architecture — run a batch layer and a speed layer in parallel, merge them at read time. Fresh and complete, but the same logic lives in two codebases.
  • Kappa architecture — one streaming pipeline; rebuild history by replaying the log (Lesson 9/29/64). One codebase, paid for in expensive replay.
  • Consumer lag — how far behind the log a consumer is (Lesson 9). Each path has its own lag.
  • Read-your-writes — a reader seeing its own just-made change (Lesson 15). Some queries need it; most historical ones don't.

Move 1 — Estimate: three questions, three physical shapes

Before choosing any technology, put the three questions side by side and read what each one demands. The demands, not the data, are what force three separate stores.

QuestionPathAccess shapeLatency needFreshness needData it touches
"Where's my order O-77?"HOT / servingpoint lookup by id~2 ms~1 s (near read-your-writes)1 row, recent
"Revenue last minute, product P-42?"WARM / speedlive windowed aggregate~10 ms~2 s1 running counter
"Revenue by region by day, last quarter?"COLD / batchlarge historical scanseconds OK~15 min stale OKbillions of rows

Why one store can't do all three (the numbers that split them apart). Suppose you serve everything from the warehouse. The columnar engine (Lesson 29) is built to scan: a point lookup "order O-77" has no fast key, so it reads far more than one row — seconds, not the ~2 ms the shopper needs (this is Lesson 12's selectivity trap in reverse). Now suppose you serve everything from the serving store — an OLTP row store. The "revenue by region, last quarter" query must read 90 days × 172.8M orders/day ≈ 15.5 billion rows; at ~1 KB/row that's a ~15 TB row-wise scan that (a) takes minutes on a store built for point reads, and (b) evicts the hot cache and pins the CPU, tripping Lesson 27's latency knee → the shopper's ~2 ms lookup becomes a timeout → Lesson 7's retry storm. The two workloads don't just prefer different stores; run together, the big one destroys the small one. Opposite shapes, opposite failure modes — exactly Lesson 29's "never share serving and analytics," now generalized to three.

Sizing each shape (they're wildly different, which is the point). HOT: keep only recent orders online — say 30 days: 172.8M/day × 30 = 5.18 billion rows ≈ 5.2 TB in a key-value/document store, indexed by order_id and by user_id so "my orders" is one fast lookup. WARM: the speed layer's state scales with keys × windows, not the firehose (Lesson 64): ~100k products × a rolling-minute of buckets ≈ tens of MB — call it ~50 MB, held in memory / on RocksDB, fresh within ~2 s. COLD: the full history — 2,000/s × 86,400 × 365 × 3 ≈ 189 billion rows ≈ 189 TB raw, compressed columnar ~3.6× to ~52 TB on cheap object storage (Lesson 20/67). Three stores whose sizes span 50 MB → 5.2 TB → 189 TB — a million-fold range — because each keeps exactly what its question needs and nothing more. The cold path is huge and cheap; the warm path is tiny and fast; the hot path is medium and fresh.

Why "keep everything hot" is the tempting wrong answer: it feels simplest to leave all 189 TB in the fast serving store so any query is instant. But fast storage is the expensive storage (Lesson 39's temperature tiers): keeping 189 TB in an in-memory-cached OLTP store costs orders of magnitude more than object storage, to serve queries that don't even need low latency. And it re-creates the failure above — the quarterly scan trampling the shopper's lookup. The right instinct is the opposite: keep only what each question needs, in the cheapest shape that meets its latency and freshness bar. Name the trade: uniform simplicity vs cost-and-isolation.

Move 2 — Model: one write, three read models, and "consistent enough"

The fan-out: CQRS with three read models

The write path is unchanged from Lesson 68: the order is captured once, durably, on the log — the single source of truth. From there, three independent consumers each build their own view. This is Lesson 37's CQRS (separate the write model from the read models), but instead of one read model we have three, each a different physical shape.

Three consumers, each idempotent, each with its own lag. (1) A serving materializer reads the log and upserts each order into the hot KV/document store, keyed for point reads. (2) A stream processor (Lesson 64) reads the same log and folds each order into windowed running totals in the speed layer. (3) A batch/ELT loader (Lesson 29/67) reads the log and appends orders into the cold warehouse, compacting them into columnar partitions on a schedule. All three are idempotent, keyed by the event's event_id (Lesson 13/68), so a duplicate delivery changes nothing and — crucially — replaying the log rebuilds any view from scratch. That replayability is the quiet superpower of this design: tomorrow you can add a fourth read model (a fraud feature store, Lesson 53) just by pointing a new consumer at the log's history — no change to the write path, no migration (Lesson 24). One source of truth, many derived views, each disposable and rebuildable.

The router: pick the path per query

The read-path router is where the design earns its keep. A query arrives; the router classifies it and routes it: a point read by id or "my recent orders" → the hot serving store; a live "what's happening now" aggregate → the warm speed layer; a large historical or ad-hoc analytical query → the cold warehouse. Sometimes the router is literal (an API gateway inspecting the query, Lesson 49); often it's just discipline in the application — the "my orders" page code calls the serving store, the ops dashboard calls the speed layer, the finance tool calls the warehouse. Either way, the routing rule is the architecture, and getting it wrong reintroduces Move 1's disaster: a point lookup sent to the warehouse is slow; a giant scan sent to the serving store is a self-inflicted outage.

"Consistent enough": the views will disagree, on purpose

Because the three views are built by three independent consumers, they are eventually consistent (Lesson 6/11) with each other and each lags the source by a different amount. At the instant an order is placed: the serving store shows it ~1 s later; the speed layer counts it ~2 s later; the warehouse includes it only at the next batch — up to 15 minutes later. So "today's revenue" read from the speed layer and read from the warehouse will legitimately differ by up to a batch interval. This is not a bug to be fixed; it is the deal you struck. The skill is defining how fresh each question actually needs to be: "where's my order?" needs near-read-your-writes (Lesson 15) — route it to the fresh hot path, and special-case the "I just ordered" moment. "Revenue by quarter?" tolerates hours of lag — the cold path is fine. Matching each query's freshness need to a path's freshness offer is the whole game; paying for more freshness than a question needs is wasted money, and offering less than it needs is a wrong answer.

Which view is "the truth" when they disagree? The one with the completeness the question demands. For a live dashboard, the speed layer is authoritative for the recent window — it's the only path fresh enough. For an audited quarterly report, the warehouse is authoritative — it's the only path complete and stable enough (the speed layer's approximate, short-window numbers get superseded by the batch layer's exact ones). The log itself is the ultimate source of truth; every view is a lens on it, correct within its own freshness and completeness bounds. Don't ask "which number is right?" — ask "which view is authoritative for this question?"

Move 3 — Trace the paths

Four scenarios: each of the three questions to its correct path, then the drift-and-reconcile that ties them together.

Path A — "Where's my order?" → the hot serving path

  Shopper opens "My Orders." Router sees: point read, recent, by user.

    query: get orders where user = U-42, status current
      -> ROUTER: point lookup of current state -> HOT serving store
      -> KV/document get on user_id index -> a handful of rows
      -> ~2 ms, data ~1 s fresh (serving materializer's lag)

    Why here: point reads on an indexed KV store are O(1)-ish (L48).
    Freshness ~1 s is close enough to read-your-writes (L15); the
    "I just tapped Buy" instant is special-cased to read the log tail.

Path B — "Revenue in the last minute?" → the warm speed path

  Ops dashboard, refreshing every 2 s. Router sees: live aggregate.

    query: revenue in the last 60 s, per product
      -> ROUTER: live windowed aggregate -> WARM speed layer
      -> read the running per-product sums (L64 tumbling/sliding window)
      -> ~10 ms, data ~2 s fresh

    Why here: the warehouse can't answer "last minute" fresh (its batch
    is 15 min stale), and the serving store has no windowed aggregate.
    The speed layer keeps exactly this running total in ~50 MB of state.

Path C — "Revenue by region by day, last quarter?" → the cold batch path

  Finance analyst, ad-hoc. Router sees: large historical aggregate.

    query: SUM(amount) by region, by day, last 90 days
      -> ROUTER: historical scan / aggregate -> COLD warehouse
      -> partition-prune to 90 of ~1,095 days, project 3 of 50 cols,
         scan columnar + aggregate + merge partials (L29/21)
      -> ~seconds, data stale by <= one batch interval (~15 min)

    Why here: 15.5B rows is a scan, not a lookup. Cheap columnar
    storage + pruning makes it fast ENOUGH and cheap; staleness of a
    batch interval is invisible to a quarterly question.
    (For a fixed dashboard, pre-aggregate into a daily rollup -> ms, L15.)

Path D — the drift, and how it reconciles

  ONE order O-77 placed at t=0. Watch it appear in each view:

    t + ~1 s :  HOT serving store shows O-77  ("order received")
    t + ~2 s :  WARM speed layer's last-minute revenue includes O-77
    t + <=15 min: COLD warehouse's daily total includes O-77 (next batch)

  So between t+2s and the next batch, "today's revenue":
    - from the SPEED layer  = fresh, but only covers the recent window
    - from the WAREHOUSE    = complete history, but missing the last <=15 min

  Reconcile: the batch layer is the eventual, exact system of record;
  the speed layer is the fast, approximate front that gets SUPERSEDED.
  Merge at read (lambda) OR run one pipeline + replay to correct (kappa).

Paths A, B, and C are the payoff of the split: each question is answered by a store built for its exact shape — the point read is milliseconds and fresh, the live aggregate is milliseconds and seconds-fresh, the deep scan is cheap and complete. None of them can trample the others, because they run on separate machinery (Lesson 29's isolation). Path D is the price: the three views drift, and you must decide — per question — which view is authoritative and how the fast-but-approximate speed layer gets reconciled against the slow-but-exact batch layer. That reconciliation choice is the fork we hit next.

Move 4 — Find the first bottleneck: the same logic in three pipelines

The split works. Its deepest cost is not storage or latency — it's that you now compute "revenue" in more than one place, and those places can silently disagree.

The first bottleneck — the duplicated-logic tax (lambda's core problem). To be both fresh and complete, the classic move is lambda architecture: the speed layer answers "the last few minutes," the batch layer answers "everything up to the last batch," and you merge them at read time. But now the definition of "revenue" — which refunds count, how currency converts (Lesson 25), which test orders to exclude — lives in two codebases: the streaming job and the batch job. Keep them in sync forever, or they drift: a fix applied to the batch SQL but not the stream operator makes the dashboard and the report show two different revenue numbers for the same day — a trust-destroying, hard-to-debug divergence. Two fixes, each a named trade: kappa architecture collapses to one streaming pipeline and rebuilds history by replaying the log (Lesson 9/29/64) — one codebase, one definition, paid for in the cost and time of replaying billions of events to backfill or correct. Or keep lambda but share the aggregation logic as one library both layers call, so there is one definition with two runners. The tension is permanent: one pipeline (simple, but replay is expensive) vs two pipelines (fresh + cheap history, but the same logic maintained twice).

The second wall — every view is another consumer with its own lag, backfill, and failure mode. Fan-out multiplies work: one event now drives three writes (serving upsert + speed update + warehouse append) = ~6,000 writes/s of derived work, three pipelines to run, three to monitor. Each has its own consumer lag (Lesson 9) — if the batch loader falls behind, the warehouse silently gets staler; if the serving materializer stalls, "where's my order?" goes stale while the customer is watching. Each new view you add is another backfill when you launch it (replay 3 years of history = Lesson 57's bulk-pipeline problem) and another thing that can break independently. The bill for "each question gets its perfect store" is paid in N pipelines to keep healthy, not one. Watch each path's lag as its own SLO; alarm when any view drifts past its freshness promise.

The third wall — the router is a real decision, and a mis-route is an outage. Classifying "is this a point read, a live aggregate, or a historical scan?" is easy at the extremes and genuinely hard in the middle ("last 7 days per product" — warm or cold?). Get it wrong and Move 1's disaster returns: a point lookup sent to the warehouse is seconds slow, and a giant ad-hoc scan sent to the serving store evicts its cache and takes the shopper's page down with it (Lesson 29's two traps, reborn as routing bugs). The router must also encode "consistent enough" per query — routing a read-your-writes-sensitive query (the customer's own just-placed order) to a stale path is a correctness bug, not a performance one. So the routing rules deserve the same care as any hot-path code: default ambiguous large queries to the isolated cold path (never the shared serving store), and route freshness-critical reads to the freshest path even when it's more expensive.

So the honest design for our one order event is: capture it once on the durable log (Lesson 9/68) as the single source of truth; fan it out to three idempotent read models (Lesson 13/37) — a hot serving store for millisecond point reads of recent state (Lesson 48), a warm speed layer for seconds-fresh live aggregates (Lesson 64), and a cold columnar warehouse for cheap deep history (Lesson 29/67) — sized to a millionfold range because each keeps only what its question needs (Lesson 39); front them with a read-path router that matches each query's latency-and-freshness need to a path's offer; accept that the views are eventually consistent with each other (Lesson 6/11) and define "consistent enough" per question (read-your-writes for "my order," hours-stale for "last quarter"); and reconcile the fast-approximate speed layer against the slow-exact batch layer with lambda (two pipelines, merge at read — beware two copies of one definition) or kappa (one pipeline, replay to correct — beware expensive replay). Every choice names its price: three shapes cost three pipelines; freshness costs money; one definition costs replay; a router that mis-classifies costs an outage.

The deepest point: freshness, query latency, and cost-per-byte form a triangle no single store can win. A store fast for point reads is expensive to fill with history; a store cheap for history is slow and stale for point reads; a store fresh for live aggregates holds only a tiny recent window. You don't beat the triangle by finding a magic store — you beat it by writing the same event down in three shapes and routing each question to the corner it lives in. This is the same instinct as CQRS (Lesson 37: one write, many read models), tiered storage (Lesson 39: match data temperature to storage cost), and lambda/kappa (Lesson 29/64: batch for complete, stream for fresh) — all three are one pattern seen from different angles. The standing tension, from the very first cache in Lesson 2 to here: freshness & per-query latency vs duplicated pipelines & cost.

The four traps

  • Trap 1 — one store for everything. Serving one database to shoppers, dashboards, and analysts feels simple until the quarterly scan evicts the serving cache and takes down "where's my order" (Lesson 29's failure, now at three-way scale). Serving and analytics have opposite shapes and opposite failure modes; isolate them onto separate machinery.
  • Trap 2 — mis-routing the query. A point lookup sent to the warehouse is seconds slow; a giant scan sent to the serving store is a self-inflicted outage. The router's classification rules are hot-path code — default ambiguous heavy queries to the isolated cold path, and freshness-critical reads to the freshest path.
  • Trap 3 — two copies of one definition (lambda drift). Coding "revenue" in both the stream job and the batch job invites them to diverge, so the live dashboard and the finance report show different numbers for the same day. Share the logic, or collapse to one pipeline (kappa) and replay to correct.
  • Trap 4 — ignoring "consistent enough." Treating all three views as interchangeable leads to reading a customer's just-placed order from a 15-minute-stale warehouse (a wrong answer) or paying serving-store prices to keep three years hot (wasted money). Define, per question, exactly how fresh it must be and route accordingly.

Where we landed

We took the one reliable order.placed event from Lesson 68 and asked who reads it — and found three questions that demand three different machines. Estimate showed why: a point lookup wants milliseconds and freshness, a live aggregate wants seconds-freshness over a tiny window, and a quarterly scan wants cheap storage over billions of rows — and run together on one store, the big query destroys the small one (Lesson 29's serving-vs-analytics split, generalized). Model chose one write and three idempotent read models (Lesson 37's CQRS to its conclusion): a hot serving store (~5.2 TB, ~2 ms, ~1 s fresh), a warm speed layer (~50 MB state, ~10 ms, ~2 s fresh), and a cold warehouse (~189 TB raw, seconds, ~15 min stale) — sized across a millionfold range — with a router matching each query's freshness need to a path's offer, and "consistent enough" defined per question. Tracing sent each question to its path and watched one order appear at t+1 s, t+2 s, and t+15 min across the three views, then reconcile (batch supersedes speed). The first bottleneck was the duplicated-logic tax — the same "revenue" definition living in the stream job and the batch job (lambda's drift), fixed by kappa's one-pipeline-plus-replay or a shared library — plus N pipelines to keep healthy (each with its own lag and backfill) and a router whose mis-classification is an outage. Every dial named its trade. The standing tension, unbroken since Lesson 2's first cache: freshness & per-query latency vs duplicated pipelines & cost.
