Media Transcoding Pipelines: One 4K Upload Into a Hundred Jobs

Lesson 20 gave us a place to put a 4K video — a durable object store. This lesson is what happens the instant the upload lands: that one 3.3 GB file is useless to almost everyone until we transcode it into a whole ladder of smaller versions — 240p for a phone on a train, 1080p for a laptop, an efficient AV1 copy to cut delivery cost — each cut into short segments a player can stream. We open on the number that decides the whole design: encoded top-to-bottom, the 4K version alone takes ~20 minutes before anyone can press play, and re-encoded across the ladder, one upload explodes into 1,200 separate jobs. We'll estimate that fan-out and the 20-minutes-to-8-seconds gap. We'll model the fix — stop treating the video as one file: cut it into keyframe-aligned segments, and every hard problem (slow encode, crashed worker, viewer waiting, wasted storage) turns into a job over a small independent chunk, arranged as a DAG and drained through priority lanes. We'll trace an upload becoming watchable, a viral burst, a cold video nobody has requested yet, and a worker dying mid-encode. And we'll find the first bottleneck: the fan-out itself — 1,200 jobs per upload flooding a shared encoder fleet, where a batch re-encode can starve the viewer who's waiting right now. The trade under all of it: storage vs compute vs time-to-first-play — you can pay in disk, in CPU, or in the viewer's patience, but you always pay.

The one system we'll build

A video platform. A creator uploads one 10-minute 4K video — a single ~3.3 GB H.264 file — into the object store from Lesson 20. Before it can be watched well on every device and network, we must produce an ABR ladder: several resolutions (2160p, 1080p, 720p, 480p, 360p, 240p) so a player can pick the one that fits the viewer's bandwidth, in two codecs (H.264 for universal compatibility, AV1 because it's ~35% smaller and cuts our delivery bill, Lesson 14/59), each cut into short segments the player streams one at a time. Plus thumbnails, plus a manifest that lists it all. Our job: turn that one upload into all those outputs fast enough that the creator's video is live in seconds, cheaply enough that we don't drown in storage or CPU, and reliably enough that a crashed machine doesn't restart a 20-minute job from zero. The adversary here isn't an attacker — it's the sheer size of the work, and the fact that the naive way to do it makes the viewer wait.

The one idea the whole lesson turns on: stop thinking of the video as one file and one job. Cut it into short, independent segments, and every hard problem becomes tractable at once. A segment is small, so it encodes in seconds (fast time-to-first-play). Segments are independent, so thousands encode in parallel (throughput) and a crashed one just re-runs (resumability). Each segment's output path is deterministic, so re-running produces the same bytes (idempotency, Lesson 13). And segments are exactly what an adaptive player streams, switching quality per segment as bandwidth changes. The segment is the unit of parallelism, priority, resumability, and streaming — one idea that unlocks four. Everything below is about producing segments, ordering the work so the waiting viewer wins, and deciding which segments to keep on disk versus make on demand.

Words we'll earn before we lean on them

  • Transcode — decode a video and re-encode it into a different resolution, bitrate, or codec. CPU-heavy; often slower than real time.
  • Rendition / variant — one output version of the video (e.g. "720p, H.264"). The set of them is the ABR ladder.
  • ABR (adaptive bitrate) — the player picks a rendition per segment to match the viewer's current bandwidth, stepping up or down without stalling.
  • Codec — the compression algorithm (H.264, VP9, AV1). Newer codecs make smaller files but cost far more CPU to encode.
  • Keyframe (IDR) / GOP — a keyframe is a self-contained frame decodable without any other; a GOP ("group of pictures") is a keyframe plus the frames that depend on it. Cuts must land on keyframes.
  • Segment / chunk — a few seconds of video (we use 6 s), starting at a keyframe so it decodes and encodes on its own. The unit everything is built from.
  • DAG (directed acyclic graph) — the job graph: probe → split → (fan-out of transcodes) → package → publish. Edges are "must finish before."
  • Fan-out — one input spawning many jobs (here 1 upload → 1,200 transcodes).
  • Priority lane / queue — separate queues so viewer-waiting work jumps ahead of background batch work (Lesson 9/28).
  • Time-to-first-play (TTFP) — time from "upload finished" to "someone can press play and it works." The number we optimize.
  • JIT (just-in-time) transcoding — make a rendition the first time it's requested and cache it, instead of pre-making everything (Lesson 48).
  • Idempotent / resumable job — safe to re-run and produces the same result; a crash resumes from the last finished segment, not from zero (Lesson 13/70).
  • Straggler / speculative execution — one slow job holding up a barrier; re-issue it on a second worker and take whichever finishes first (Lesson 29/64).
  • Packaging / manifest — stitching a rendition's segments and writing the HLS/DASH playlist the player reads.

Move 1 — Estimate: the fan-out and the 20-minutes-to-8-seconds gap

Two numbers set the whole design. First, how much work is it? Second, how long until the viewer can watch? Get these and the architecture almost writes itself.

The fan-out: one upload → 1,200 jobs. Our ladder is 6 resolutions × 2 codecs = 12 renditions. The video is 10 minutes = 600 s; cut into 6-second segments that's 600 / 6 = 100 segments. Every rendition needs every segment:

  • 12 renditions × 100 segments = 1,200 transcode jobs, plus 1 probe, 1 split, 12 package jobs, and a handful of thumbnail extractions.
  • Now scale it: at a modest steady 2 uploads/second, that's 2 × 1,200 = 2,400 transcode jobs/second arriving forever. Each segment job averages ~5 core-seconds of CPU (cheap 240p is fast; 4K and AV1 are brutal), so steady-state you need roughly 2,400 × 5 = 12,000 CPU cores just to keep pace — and a launch-day burst can multiply that 10×.

One upload is not one unit of work — it's a small storm of it. That single fact forces queues, a worker fleet, and a way to decide which of the 1,200 jobs matters most right now.

Time-to-first-play: 20 minutes vs ~8 seconds. Transcoding is often slower than real time. Suppose 4K encodes at 0.5× real time (a 600 s video takes 1,200 s of CPU) and 720p at 2× real time (600 s → 300 s). Do it the naive, monolithic way — encode a whole rendition end-to-end before publishing — and:

  • The 4K rendition alone takes 600 / 0.5 = 1,200 s = 20 minutes. Even a single 720p copy is 600 / 2 = 300 s = 5 minutes. The creator's video sits dark that whole time.
  • Now the segmented way: to let playback start, we only need segment 0 of one sane rendition. Encode just the first 6-second chunk at 720p: 6 / 2 = 3 s of CPU. Add probe + split + queue overhead and TTFP ≈ 8 seconds. The remaining 99 segments encode in parallel behind the playhead — the player only needs to stay a few seconds ahead.

The scoreboard: ~20 minutes → ~8 seconds to watchable, for the exact same total work. Nothing got cheaper; we just reordered it so the piece the viewer needs first gets made first. That reordering is only possible because we cut the video into independent segments.

Why the segment size is a real trade, not a free win: smaller segments mean finer parallelism, faster TTFP, and quicker quality switches for the player — but every segment must start with a keyframe, and keyframes are the most expensive frames to encode, so tiny segments waste bytes and bloat the manifest. Larger segments compress better and carry less overhead but parallelize coarsely and slow TTFP. 6 seconds is the common compromise. The trade named: parallelism & latency vs compression efficiency & overhead — the same tension you'll meet again in the next few walls.

Move 2 — Model: a DAG of segment jobs, drained through priority lanes

The pipeline is a DAG, and the split is where it fans out

Model the work as a directed acyclic graph of jobs — each node a unit of work, each edge a "must finish before." The shape:

  • probe → split: read the file's metadata, then cut it into 100 keyframe-aligned segments. The split is the fan-out point: one input, 1,200 downstream transcodes.
  • transcode (the fan-out): each job takes one source segment and produces one output segment for one rendition. These 1,200 jobs are mutually independent — the graph is wide and shallow here, which is exactly what lets a big fleet chew through them in parallel.
  • package (the fan-in / barrier): for each rendition, once all 100 of its segments exist, stitch them and write the HLS/DASH manifest. This is a join — it can't start until its inputs finish, which we'll see is where a single slow segment (a straggler) can hurt.
  • publish: push segments + manifests to the CDN (Lesson 14) and mark the rendition live.

Why a DAG and not a script? Because jobs fail, and a graph lets you re-run only the failed node and everything downstream of it — not the whole pipeline. The split done once feeds 1,200 transcodes; if transcode #814 dies, you re-run #814, not the split and not the other 1,199.

Two lanes, because "make this" and "make this NOW" are different jobs

The same encoder fleet serves work of wildly different urgency. Put it in separate priority lanes (Lesson 9/28) and let workers always drain the urgent one first:

LaneExample workDeadline
Interactive (highest)segment 0 of a just-uploaded video; a JIT rendition a viewer is waiting onseconds — someone is staring at a spinner
Standardthe other 99 segments of a new upload's popular renditionsa minute or two — fill in behind the playhead
Background (lowest)re-encoding the whole back-catalog to AV1; making the 240p nobody has asked for yethours/days — pure throughput, no one waiting

Within a single upload we also order the ladder: encode a middle rung (say 720p) segment 0 first so playback can start, then widen out. The trade named: latency of the waiting viewer vs throughput/fairness of batch work. Without lanes, a million-video AV1 backfill (all "background") sits in the same FIFO queue as the segment 0 a viewer needs now, and the viewer waits behind the backfill — the classic priority-inversion starvation from Lesson 28, now measured in the seconds a person will actually tolerate.

Store every variant, or make it on demand?

Here's the money question. Do you pre-transcode the full ladder for every upload and store it, or store only the source and JIT-transcode a rendition the first time it's requested (Lesson 48), caching the result?

  • Storage cost of pre-encoding everything. Our ladder is ~1.9 GB (H.264) + ~1.2 GB (AV1) ≈ 3.1 GB of variants on top of the 3.3 GB source — variants nearly double the footprint to ~6.4 GB/video. Fine for one video. But views are brutally skewed (Lesson 2): ~90% of views hit ~1% of the catalog. So for a 100M-video library, ~99M videos are cold — and if each carried our full ladder that's 3.1 GB × 99M ≈ ~300 PB of renditions almost nobody ever streams.
  • JIT instead. Keep the source (or a high-quality mezzanine) and make the rare 480p-AV1 only when someone first asks, then cache it. You spend compute on the first viewer (a few seconds if you JIT just the needed segments) and re-spend it if the cache evicts, but you don't warehouse 300 PB of cold variants.

The trade named — this is the lesson's headline: storage vs compute vs time-to-first-play. Pre-encode = pay storage, instant play. JIT = pay compute + a first-view wait, save storage. The right answer is a hybrid keyed on popularity: pre-encode the top rungs for hot content, JIT the cold tail, and tier the bytes hot/warm/cold (Lesson 39). You're not choosing one corner of the triangle — you're placing each video in it by how much it's watched.

Move 3 — Trace the paths

Follow four situations through the DAG: a fresh upload going live, a burst of uploads, a cold video's first-ever viewer, and a worker dying mid-encode.

Path A — upload → watchable in ~8 seconds

   [0s]  3.3 GB upload lands in object store (L20)
   [+1s] probe: 10 min, 4K, 30fps, H.264
   [+2s] split: 100 keyframe-aligned 6s segments (streamed, not
           whole-file -- start splitting as bytes arrive)
   [+2s] ENQUEUE seg0 -> 720p on the INTERACTIVE lane; the other
           1,199 jobs go to STANDARD/BACKGROUND by rung popularity
   [+5s] a worker encodes seg0->720p (6s / 2x = 3s CPU)
   [+6s] package writes a 1-segment manifest -> publish to CDN
   [~8s] VIDEO IS LIVE. Player can press play on 720p.
   [8s..~60s] remaining 99 segments + higher rungs fill in behind
           the playhead; ladder widens to 1080p/4K/AV1 as they finish

Path B — a burst: 10× uploads at launch

   Scheduled drop: uploads spike 2/s -> 20/s for 10 minutes.
   New transcode jobs: 20 x 1,200 = 24,000 jobs/s flooding in.
   Queue depth explodes; encoder fleet is saturated.
     - Priority lanes hold: seg0 (INTERACTIVE) still clears in
       seconds; the AV1 backfill (BACKGROUND) is what waits.
     - Autoscale the encoder fleet on QUEUE DEPTH, not CPU (L27):
       depth is the leading signal; CPU is already pegged.
     - Backpressure (L28): if even the interactive lane backs up,
       admit uploads more slowly / shed the lowest rungs first,
       and tell creators "processing" honestly.
   Result: TTFP stretches from ~8s to maybe ~30s under load, but
     never to 20 min, and NOTHING is dropped -- just deferred.

Path C — a cold video's first viewer (JIT)

   A 3-year-old video, only its 720p-H.264 rung pre-encoded.
   A viewer on a slow phone needs 240p-AV1 -- never made.
     [1] CDN miss -> origin miss -> JIT request enqueued on the
         INTERACTIVE lane (a viewer is waiting).
     [2] Transcode ONLY the segments the player needs next, from
         the mezzanine, starting at the current segment (not seg0).
     [3] First needed segment ready in ~3-5s; cache it in CDN +
         object store so the SECOND viewer pays nothing (L48).
   Trade lived out: we saved 3.1 GB x years of cold storage and
     paid ~4s of compute, once, only because someone actually asked.

Path D — a worker dies 15 minutes into a big job

   A 2-hour 4K movie: ~1,200 segments x ladder = ~14,000 jobs.
   Worker encoding segment 812->AV1 crashes at minute 15.
     Monolithic world: restart the whole 20-min+ encode from 0.
     Segmented world:
       - Job output path is deterministic:
           s3://.../vid/av1/1080p/seg00812.m4s   (idempotent, L13)
       - The queue's visibility timeout expires (L9): the message
         reappears; another worker re-runs ONLY seg812.
       - Segments 0..811 are already written -> skipped. Resume
         from 812, not from 0 (L70).
     Re-running a finished segment is SAFE (same bytes) and re-running
     the crashed one is CHEAP (one 6s chunk, ~12s of CPU).

Path A is the payoff: segment-level ordering turns 20 minutes into 8 seconds. Path B shows the lanes earning their keep — a burst delays background work, never the waiting viewer, and you scale on queue depth. Path C lives out the storage/compute trade: don't warehouse what nobody watches; make it on demand and cache it. Path D shows why segments + determinism = resumability: a crash costs one chunk, not the whole movie. Move 4 names why the fan-out is the wall all of this is built around.

Move 4 — Find the first bottleneck: the fan-out floods a shared fleet

The first bottleneck — one upload becomes 1,200 jobs, and they all land on the same encoder fleet. This is the wall the whole design bends around. Encoding is expensive and shared: every upload's fan-out competes for the same cores. Two failure modes fall out. (1) Volume: a burst of uploads multiplies an already-large job count until the queue and the fleet can't keep up (Path B). (2) Priority inversion: without lanes, the segment 0 a viewer needs right now waits in the same FIFO behind a million-video background backfill — the batch work starves the interactive work (Lesson 28). The fix is the Move-2 model: separate priority lanes so viewer-waiting jobs always jump ahead, an autoscaled worker fleet sized on queue depth (the leading signal — CPU is already pegged, Lesson 27), and backpressure that defers the lowest rungs and admits uploads honestly when even the interactive lane backs up (Lesson 28). The trade named: the latency of the person waiting vs the throughput and cost-efficiency of the batch — you protect the first by being willing to defer the second.

The second wall — storing every variant wastes a fortune on a tail nobody watches. Pre-encoding the full ladder for all 100M videos is ~300 PB of renditions, and with 90% of views on 1% of videos, almost all of it is cold (Lesson 2's skew). But the opposite extreme — JIT everything — makes the popular video's every viewer wait for an encode, and re-burns compute each time a cache evicts. Neither corner is right. The answer is a popularity-keyed hybrid: pre-encode the top rungs for hot content (pay storage where it's watched), JIT the cold tail and cache it (pay compute only when asked), and tier the bytes across hot/warm/cold storage (Lesson 39). The trade named: storage vs compute vs first-play latency — the same triangle from Move 2, now resolved per-video by how much each is actually streamed rather than globally.

The third wall — the package step is a barrier, and one straggler segment blocks a whole rendition. Packaging a rendition can't start until all 100 of its segments exist (the fan-in join). So if 99 segments finish in 3 s and segment 47 lands on a slow or flaky worker and takes 60 s, the entire rendition is stuck for 60 s — the pipeline moves at the speed of its slowest chunk. This is the classic straggler problem (Lesson 29/64). The fix leans directly on segments being idempotent: speculative execution — once most segments are done, re-issue the laggards on a second worker and take whichever output lands first. Because re-running a segment produces the identical bytes (Lesson 13), running it twice is harmless; you're spending a little extra compute to buy back tail latency. The trade named: redundant compute vs the tail latency of a join — spend the first to shrink the second.

The fourth wall — the codec/quality ladder itself is a cost you're choosing, not a given. Every rung and every codec you add multiplies both encode compute and storage. AV1 is ~35% smaller on the wire — a real CDN saving at scale (Lesson 14/59) — but costs several times more CPU to encode than H.264. A denser ladder streams more smoothly (the player has more rungs to pick from) but costs more to make and keep. So the ladder is a portfolio decision: encode the rungs and codecs your actual audience's devices and networks use, in the proportion they use them — not a maximal set "to be safe." A rung nobody selects is pure waste in both compute and storage; a codec your delivery volume can't amortize doesn't pay for its encode bill. The trade named: delivery bandwidth & playback smoothness (more/better renditions) vs the encode compute and storage to produce them — measured against who's really watching, on what.

So the honest picture of a media pipeline: one upload is a storm of work — 1,200 jobs from a single 4K file — and the naive monolithic encode makes the viewer wait ~20 minutes for what segment-level ordering delivers in ~8 seconds. The move that unlocks everything is to stop treating the video as one file and cut it into keyframe-aligned segments: independent units of parallelism (thousands encode at once), priority (segment 0 first), resumability (a crash costs one chunk), and streaming (an ABR player's native unit). Arrange the jobs as a DAG, drain them through priority lanes so the waiting viewer beats the background backfill, and place each video in the storage/compute/TTFP triangle by how much it's watched — pre-encode the hot, JIT the cold, tier the rest. The first bottleneck is the fan-out flooding a shared fleet (→ lanes + autoscale on queue depth + backpressure, Lesson 27/28); around it, storing every variant wastes a cold-tail fortune (→ popularity-keyed hybrid + tiering, Lesson 2/39), the package barrier lets one straggler block a rendition (→ speculative execution on idempotent segments, Lesson 29/64/13), and the ladder is a portfolio cost you match to your real audience (→ delivery bytes vs encode compute, Lesson 14/59). Every choice names its price.

The deepest point: transcoding is one big number you can pay in three currencies — disk, CPU, or the viewer's patience — and the whole craft is deciding, per video and per rung, which currency to spend. A hot video justifies spending storage (pre-encode the full ladder) to spend the least patience (instant play). A cold video justifies spending a little patience (a first-view JIT wait) to save storage. A launch-day burst justifies spending patience on background work (defer the backfill) to protect the patience of the person actually watching. Segmentation is what makes those trades possible — it turns one indivisible 20-minute job into a thousand tiny ones you can schedule, prioritize, resume, and place independently. Notice the whole thing is assembled from pieces you already own: object storage (L20), queues and visibility timeouts (L9), priority and backpressure (L28), autoscaling on the right signal (L27), idempotency (L13), resumable jobs (L70), the straggler/speculative-execution pattern (L29/64), popularity skew and caching (L2/48), and storage tiering (L39). The new idea is only the framing: a blob is not a unit of work — its segments are. The standing tension: storage vs compute vs time-to-first-play.

The four traps

  • Trap 1 — encoding the whole video before publishing. A monolithic encode makes TTFP equal the full encode time (minutes to tens of minutes). Segment the video and publish segment 0 first; fill in the rest behind the playhead.
  • Trap 2 — one queue for everything. A background back-catalog backfill and a viewer's segment 0 in the same FIFO means the viewer waits behind the batch. Separate priority lanes; interactive always wins.
  • Trap 3 — pre-encoding the full ladder for every video. Views are ~90/1 skewed, so most variants are never watched — you warehouse a cold-tail fortune. Pre-encode hot content, JIT the cold tail, tier the storage.
  • Trap 4 — non-idempotent, non-resumable jobs. A crash then restarts the whole encode from zero, and a straggler can't be safely duplicated. Make each segment's output path deterministic so re-runs are safe and cheap, and speculation is free of side effects.

Where we landed

We turned one 4K upload into a whole streaming ladder without melting the fleet or making the viewer wait. Estimate set the stakes: a single 10-minute 4K file fans out to 1,200 transcode jobs (6 resolutions × 2 codecs × 100 segments), and encoded monolithically the 4K rung alone takes ~20 minutes to first play — where segmenting and prioritizing segment 0 gets there in ~8 seconds for the identical total work. Model gave the structure: a DAG (probe → split → 1,200 transcodes → package → publish) whose split is the fan-out and whose package is a barrier; priority lanes (interactive / standard / background) so the waiting viewer beats the batch; and the storage/compute/TTFP triangle resolved per-video by popularity — pre-encode the hot, JIT the cold, tier the rest. Tracing showed an upload live in ~8 s, a 10× burst that defers background work but never drops it (autoscale on queue depth, backpressure), a cold video's first viewer served by JIT-and-cache, and a crashed worker that re-runs one idempotent segment instead of a whole movie. The first bottleneck: the fan-out floods a shared encoder fleet (→ lanes + autoscale + backpressure, L27/28); around it, storing every variant wastes ~300 PB on a tail nobody watches (→ popularity hybrid + tiering, L2/39), the package barrier lets one straggler block a rendition (→ speculative execution on idempotent segments, L29/64/13), and the codec ladder is a portfolio cost matched to the real audience (→ delivery bytes vs encode compute, L14/59). The deepest point: transcoding is one big number paid in three currencies — disk, CPU, or the viewer's patience — and segmentation is what lets you choose which to spend, per video and per rung. The standing tension: storage vs compute vs time-to-first-play.

Next up — we just leaned hard on "make it once, then cache it" (JIT + CDN). Now open the cache itself: when you have layers of them, what does a write do?

• Multi-level caching — write-through vs write-back (Lesson 78): a cache hierarchy (a local in-process cache, then Redis, then the CDN). On a write, do you push it all the way down synchronously (write-through: safe, slow), acknowledge fast and flush later (write-back: fast, loses data on crash), or skip the cache on writes entirely (write-around)? Plus keeping the tiers coherent so a stale layer doesn't serve yesterday's value. Trade: read/write latency vs durability & coherence.
