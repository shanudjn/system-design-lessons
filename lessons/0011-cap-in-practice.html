CAP in Practice: What a Partition Forces You to Give Up

Lesson 10 ended on a deliberate sacrifice: when the network split, the minority side stayed alive but refused every write, so the cluster would never have two leaders. We chose safety over availability without naming the law behind it. This lesson states that law — CAP — and, more usefully, shows what it actually does to real reads and writes. We take ONE concrete system: an account-balance store replicated across three zones, holding $100. Then we cut the trans-atlantic cable and watch the same partition force two completely different systems into two opposite, painful choices — one returns errors, the other returns money it doesn't have. We name the trade-off behind each, and end on the thing CAP doesn't tell you (which matters more day-to-day).

The one system we'll trace

We store one number per customer: their account balance. It's money, so a read must never lie. We keep three copies (replicas) of each balance, one in each of three zones — Virginia, Frankfurt, Singapore — so a whole zone can burn down and the balance survives. A client writes to the store; the store decides how many copies must agree before it answers. Here is the whole shape on one screen, in the healthy case and the broken one.

  ONE BALANCE (X=$100), THREE REPLICAS, A QUORUM TO ANSWER

   HEALTHY                              PARTITIONED (cable cut)
   ┌────┐  ┌────┐  ┌────┐               ┌────┐  ╳  ┌────┐  ┌────┐
   │ Z1 │──│ Z2 │──│ Z3 │               │ Z1 │ ╳  │ Z2 │──│ Z3 │
   └────┘  └────┘  └────┘               └────┘ ╳  └────┘  └────┘
   all three can talk                   {Z1}  |  {Z2, Z3}
   write reaches 2, read reaches 2      1 node|  2 nodes
   → every read sees the latest write   minority | majority

   The split forces a choice on the MINORITY side {Z1}:
     • answer anyway (maybe stale/wrong) ........ keep AVAILABILITY, lose CONSISTENCY
     • refuse to answer (return an error) ....... keep CONSISTENCY, lose AVAILABILITY
   You cannot have both while the cable is cut. That is CAP.

CAP says: when the network partitions, a system can keep every reply consistent OR keep every node available — not both. That's the whole theorem in one line. The rest of this lesson is making those three words — consistent, available, partition — concrete enough that the choice stops being abstract and becomes "errors vs. wrong money."

Words we'll earn before we lean on them

  • Replica — one full copy of the data on one node. We keep three: N = 3.
  • Consistency (the CAP "C", a.k.a. linearizability) — every read returns the value of the most recent completed write, as if there were a single copy. Not "ACID consistency" — same word, different idea.
  • Availability — every request that reaches a working node gets a non-error answer (not necessarily the latest one).
  • Partition — the network drops or delays messages so some nodes can't reach others. The cluster is split into groups that can't talk across the gap.
  • Partition tolerance — the system keeps operating while a partition is happening, instead of seizing up entirely.
  • Quorum — a minimum number of replicas that must take part. Write quorum W, read quorum R (we met these in Lesson 06).
  • CP system — under a partition, prefers Consistency: it returns errors rather than a possibly-wrong answer. AP system — prefers Availability: it always answers, accepting that answers may be stale or conflict.
  • Eventual consistency — replicas that diverged during a partition are reconciled after it heals, so they converge "eventually."

Move 1 — Estimate: is a partition even worth designing for?

Engineers love to wave CAP away with "partitions are rare." Let's put a number on "rare" and on what one costs, because that number decides whether this whole lesson matters.

Worked: the cost of one cable cut.

The store handles 15,000 requests/second, spread evenly across the three zones, so each zone serves ~5,000 req/s. A trans-atlantic link flaps a handful of times a month; say a partition that isolates one zone lasts 30 seconds before routing recovers.

• Requests hitting the cut-off zone during the partition: 5,000 req/s × 30 s = 150,000 requests.
• If we keep Consistency (refuse them): 150,000 errors, but every answer ever returned is correct.
• If we keep Availability (answer them anyway): 0 errors, but some of those 150,000 answers are stale, and any writes accepted on both sides will conflict and must be untangled later.

150,000 wrong-or-refused operations from a single 30-second blip — several times a month — is not a rounding error. A partition is not an edge case you can ignore; it's a Tuesday.

Name the first trade-off (the one you don't get to make): Partition tolerance is not optional. The network will drop packets between data centers — that's physics and backhoes, not a design choice. So a real distributed system is always "P". The honest version of CAP is therefore not "pick 2 of 3." It is: because P is forced on you, your only real choice is C-or-A, and only while a partition is actually happening. When the network is healthy you get both. CAP is a question that the network asks you, at a moment of its choosing.

Move 2 — Model: what "consistent" buys, and why a quorum gives it

We tune consistency with two dials from Lesson 06: how many replicas a write must reach (W) and how many a read must reach (R), out of N=3. The single rule that makes a read see the latest write is W + R > N.

Worked: why W + R > N means a read can't miss the latest write.

Pick W = 2, R = 2, N = 3. Then W + R = 4 > 3. A write lands on some set of 2 replicas; a later read asks some set of 2 replicas. Two sets of size 2 inside a pool of 3 must share at least one node — because 2 + 2 = 4, and 4 > 3, so they can't be disjoint (the same pigeonhole count from Lesson 10's majority proof). That shared node saw the write, so the read sees it too. Overlap guaranteed → linearizable read.

Now watch what the partition does to that arithmetic. The split is {Z1} on one side, {Z2, Z3} on the other.

  • The majority side {Z2, Z3} has 2 reachable replicas. It can still reach W = 2 and R = 2. So it keeps both consistency and availability — for itself.
  • The minority side {Z1} has 1 reachable replica. It cannot reach a quorum of 2. Here, and only here, CAP bites: Z1 must choose to answer with what it has (available, maybe stale) or return an error (consistent, unavailable).
  THE CHOICE LIVES ON THE MINORITY SIDE

   {Z1}  needs a quorum of 2, can reach only 1
     │
     ├─ CP:  "can't reach quorum" → return ERROR.  Never wrong, but down.
     └─ AP:  answer from local copy → maybe STALE.  Always up, but may lie.

   {Z2,Z3}  reaches quorum of 2 → stays consistent AND available. No dilemma.

Move 3 — Trace the paths

Path A — healthy network: you get C and A for free

Worked: Balance X = $100. A withdrawal of $40 writes X = $60 to Z1 and Z2 (W=2, acknowledged). A moment later a read asks Z2 and Z3 (R=2). Z2 holds $60; overlap is satisfied, so the read returns $60 — the latest value. Every read is correct, every request is answered. CAP only charges you during a partition; the rest of the time both properties hold. This is why "we're CP" or "we're AP" describes behavior under partition, not all the time.

Path B — partition, and we choose CP (the bank's choice)

The cable cuts. A customer's request to withdraw $80 is routed to the isolated zone Z1. Z1 tries to write to a quorum of 2 and can reach only itself. As a CP system it refuses: it returns an error like 503 / try again rather than record a balance change it can't make safely visible everywhere.

Worked: what CP protects. Suppose a fraudster fires the same "$80 withdrawal" at both sides during the 30-second split, hoping each side approves against the same $100. The majority side {Z2,Z3} reaches quorum and records X = $20. The minority side {Z1} cannot reach quorum, so it returns an error and records nothing. Net: exactly one $80 withdrawal happens; the balance is $20 everywhere after the heal. The double-spend is impossible because the side that couldn't prove consistency refused to act — the very same "minority steps down" move as Lesson 10's leader election, now applied to data instead of leadership. The cost: those ~150,000 requests on Z1 got errors for 30 seconds.

Name the trade-off: CP buys correctness and pays with uptime on the minority side. For money, identity, inventory you can't oversell, or anything where a wrong answer is worse than no answer, you choose CP and you eat the errors. The discipline is honest failure: a 503 the client can retry beats a confidently wrong balance it will act on. (This is the load-shedding "honest 429" reflex from Lesson 07, pointed at consistency.)

Path C — same partition, but we choose AP (the shopping-cart's choice)

Now the exact same cut, in a system that picks AP — it answers no matter what, with W = 1, R = 1 (a local write/read is enough). Nobody gets an error. But re-run the fraudster: each side sees its local $100, each approves an $80 withdrawal, each records X = $20 locally. The store stayed up. It also just let $160 leave a $100 account.

  AP UNDER PARTITION — both sides answer, both go wrong, reconcile later

   start: X = $100 on all replicas
   cut:   {Z1}            ╳            {Z2,Z3}
   Z1:  withdraw $80 → local $20       Z2: withdraw $80 → local $20
   heal: two writes meet. Which $20 wins? They describe DIFFERENT money.
         conflict! → reconcile (last-write-wins? merge? human?)

Worked: the reconciliation bill. After the heal the replicas hold conflicting histories. If reconciliation is naive last-write-wins, one $80 withdrawal silently overwrites the other: the bank delivered $160 of cash but the ledger shows a single $80 debit, so the bank eats $100 − $160 = −$60 per exploited account. Multiply by 1,000 exploited accounts in the window → $60,000 gone. AP didn't avoid the cost of the partition; it deferred and transformed it from "errors I can see now" into "money I lose later." For a balance, that's the wrong trade. For a shopping cart, it's the right one: let both phones add items during a partition, then merge the carts on heal (union of items) — a worst case of a duplicate line a human happily removes, and the customer was never blocked from shopping.

Name the trade-off: AP buys uptime everywhere and pays with divergence you must reconcile. The bill comes due at heal time, and how big it is depends entirely on your conflict-resolution rule: last-write-wins is cheap but loses data; a merge (set union, or a CRDT that's built to combine safely) keeps everything but only works for data whose conflicts can be merged. A bank balance can't be safely merged; a shopping cart, a "seen" flag, or a collaborative doc can. AP is the right call exactly when a stale-or-merged answer is cheaper than a refused one.

Move 4 — Find the next bottleneck: the choice CAP hides (PACELC)

Here's the trap CAP sets: it only speaks about the partition, which is rare. It says nothing about the 99.9% of the time the network is fine — yet you're still making a consistency choice then, and paying for it on every single request. That hidden choice has its own name: PACELC.

PACELC, read aloud:

• Partitioned → choose A or C (that's plain CAP, the rare case)

• Else (normal operation) → choose Latency or Consistency (the everyday case)

Worked: the everyday tax of strong consistency. To keep reads linearizable, a write must reach a quorum that may live in another region. Virginia↔Frankfurt round-trip ≈ 90 ms. So a strongly-consistent write waits ~90 ms for the remote ack — even when nothing is broken. A write that's allowed to commit locally and replicate in the background returns in ~1 ms, but a read right after might miss it (eventual). At 5,000 writes/s the throughput can be hidden behind concurrency, but the per-write latency can't: choosing strong consistency makes every writer wait ~90× longer on the happy path. That 90 ms, paid billions of times, is what most teams are actually trading when they "go eventually consistent" — not the once-a-month partition.

So the real design space isn't a one-time "CP or AP" badge. It's per-piece-of-data, and it spans both halves of PACELC:

DataPartition: A or C?Normal: L or C?Why
Account balanceC (refuse)C (wait 90 ms)a wrong number costs real money
Shopping cartA (merge later)L (1 ms)a duplicate line is harmless; blocking shopping isn't
"Last seen" / view countALapproximate is fine (Lesson 06's sharded counter)

The wall this runs into — and the subject that comes next — is that AP only works if you can actually reconcile. "Eventually consistent" is a promise you have to keep: you need a rule that merges divergent writes without losing or corrupting them. Last-write-wins quietly drops data. The honest tools are idempotency (make a replayed write a no-op) and conflict-free merges (sets, counters, CRDTs). That's why the next lesson zooms into idempotency: the machinery that makes "answer now, reconcile later" safe instead of just optimistic.

In real systems you don't usually flip one global switch. A single product mixes both: the same company keeps balances in a CP store (a database doing quorum writes / Raft — the consensus from Lesson 10) and carts in an AP store (Dynamo-style, eventual). The skill CAP teaches isn't "pick a side." It's looking at each piece of data and asking: when the cable is cut, would I rather hand this user an error or a possibly-wrong answer? — and the rest of the time, would I rather they wait or get a fast maybe-stale read?

Where we landed

We took one account-balance store, three replicas, $100, and cut the cable. Estimate showed a single 30-second partition touches ~150,000 requests several times a month — not an edge case, a Tuesday — and that Partition tolerance isn't a choice you get to make, so CAP collapses to "C or A, but only while partitioned." Model recovered the quorum rule W+R>N (overlap by pigeonhole → linearizable reads) and showed the split leaves the majority side fine and forces the dilemma onto the minority. Tracing the paths walked the healthy case (both C and A for free), the CP choice that refuses a write it can't make safe (errors, but the double-spend is impossible — Lesson 10's "minority steps down," applied to data), and the AP choice that answers both sides and pays a $60,000 reconciliation bill — right for a cart, wrong for a balance. The next bottleneck was the choice CAP hides: PACELC, the everyday latency-vs-consistency tax (~90 ms per strongly-consistent cross-region write) you pay even when nothing is broken, and the realization that AP is only as good as your ability to reconcile. The thread: a partition doesn't let you escape its cost — it only lets you choose whether to pay in errors now or in divergence later.
