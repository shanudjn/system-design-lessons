Object Storage: Storing 100 PB Durably Without Paying for 300

Lesson 19 ended with a hint: the fleet's cars stream dashcam video and photos, and someone has to keep it. This lesson takes one concrete number — 100 petabytes of files that must survive disk failures and be fetched by key — and designs the store for it. We'll estimate why a plain filesystem and the obvious "keep three copies" both buckle (300 PB of raw disk for 100 PB of data), model the object store that fixes it — a flat key → blob namespace, a separate metadata service, chunking, and erasure coding — trace the write path (PUT) and read path (GET) fragment by fragment, and hit the wall every blob store hits: a flood of tiny files that the per-object cost eats alive. The trade-off underneath: durability per raw byte — cheap storage that is slow to repair, vs simple copies that cost 2× more disk.

The one system we'll design

A million cars each upload clips and photos all day. The total already sits at 100 PB and grows. We need a service with two operations: PUT(key, bytes) stores a blob under a name, and GET(key) returns those exact bytes back. Two hard promises ride on it: durability — a blob you stored must still be there after disks die, because disks die constantly at this scale — and it must all fit on disk we can actually afford. The whole tension is on that pair of promises: keep 100 PB alive across failing hardware, without buying a mountain of extra disk to do it.

The cost of keeping data alive is measured per raw byte on disk, and the cost of finding it is measured per object. Those are two different bills, and the whole design comes from paying each one separately.

Words we'll earn before we lean on them

  • Object / blob — one opaque bag of bytes stored under a key. The store never looks inside it (unlike a database row with columns).
  • Object store — a service with a flat namespace: any key maps to a blob, with no real folders or paths in between. GET/PUT/DELETE a whole object, not a byte range in a tree.
  • Durability — the probability your bytes survive over a year, quoted in "nines" (e.g. eleven 9s = 99.999999999%). Different from availability (can you reach it right now).
  • Failure domain — a set of hardware that tends to die together (one disk, one machine, one rack, one power feed). Copies must land in different domains or they aren't really copies.
  • Replication — keep N whole copies of the blob on N failure domains. Simple; survives N−1 losses; costs N× the raw disk.
  • Erasure coding (Reed–Solomon) — split a blob into k data fragments and compute m parity fragments; any k of the k+m can rebuild the whole. Written RS(k, m).
  • Chunk — a fixed-size slice of a large object (say 64 MB) stored and coded independently, so one huge upload becomes many manageable, parallel pieces.
  • Metadata service — the small, consistent index mapping a key to where its chunks and fragments physically live.
  • Small-file problem — this lesson's final wall: costs charged per object (a metadata row, an erasure stripe) dwarf the bytes when the objects are tiny.

Move 1 — Estimate: why a filesystem and "just keep 3 copies" both fall over

Price the two things a beginner reaches for first — a normal filesystem, and triple replication — so every clever part below is earned.

Worked: the shape of the load. 100 PB of data. Say the average object is a 100 MB clip.

• Object count: 100 PB / 100 MB = 10¹⁷ B / 10⁸ B = 10⁹ → 1 billion objects.
• That's already the first problem for a filesystem: a directory tree with a billion entries. Path lookups walk the tree, inode tables have limits, one directory listing is monstrous, and permission checks add seeks — a POSIX filesystem was built for a laptop's millions of files, not a billion across thousands of machines.

At a billion objects you don't want folders and paths; you want a flat map from key to bytes. That flat map is exactly what an object store is — you give up rename, partial writes, and directory semantics, and in return the namespace scales to any size because every lookup is just "hash the key, go to that shard" (Lessons 03–04).

Worked: the naïve durability plan — keep 3 copies. The simplest way to survive dead disks is to store every blob three times on three racks.

• Raw disk needed: 100 PB × 3 = 300 PB.
• The 200 PB of extra disk is pure overhead — 2 bytes of insurance for every 1 byte of data.
• Fault tolerance you bought: you can lose 2 of the 3 copies and still have the data.

Triple replication is 3× the disk to survive 2 failures. At 100 PB that's a lot of steel. Is there a way to survive more failures for less disk? Yes — and it's the heart of every large blob store.

Name the first trade-off — durability per raw byte. Replication spends whole extra copies to buy survival. Its virtue is simplicity and fast repair (a lost copy is rebuilt by copying one surviving copy, no math). Its cost is that survival scales linearly with disk: to tolerate f failures you pay (f+1)×. Erasure coding buys the same survival for far less disk — at the price of CPU and slower repair. That is the choice the whole store turns on.

Move 2 — Model: flat namespace, metadata split, chunking, erasure coding

Four ideas, each earning its place against a number.

Model A — split the metadata plane from the data plane

The data plane holds the actual bytes: 100 PB, dumb, append-only, spread over thousands of disks. The metadata service holds only the map: key → which chunks, and each chunk → which fragments on which racks. How big is that map?

Worked: metadata is 100,000× smaller than the data. 1 billion objects × ~1 KB of metadata each (key, size, chunk list, fragment locations, checksums) = 10⁹ × 10³ = 10¹² B = 1 TB.

Compare: 1 TB of metadata vs 100 PB of data — a ratio of 10⁵ = 100,000×. The metadata fits in RAM across a small sharded cluster; the data never could.

Name the trade-off — consistency where it's small, durability where it's big. The metadata is tiny, hot, and must be correct (a wrong pointer loses an object), so it lives in a strongly-consistent, replicated store (quorums, L06/L11). The data is enormous, cold, and must be durable but only eventually consistent, so it lives in a cheap append-only plane. Splitting them lets you pay the expensive "always correct" price on 1 TB instead of on 100 PB.

Model B — chunk the big objects

A single upload can be gigabytes. Storing it as one indivisible blob is bad: you can't upload it in parallel, a failed upload restarts from zero, and no single disk may have room. So split every object into fixed chunks — say 64 MB — and store each chunk independently. A 1 GB clip becomes 1024 / 64 = 16 chunks that upload in parallel, resume from the last good chunk, and scatter across many disks.

Name the trade-off — chunk size is parallelism & repair granularity vs metadata overhead. Smaller chunks mean more parallelism and finer repair (rebuild a 64 MB chunk, not a 5 GB file), but more chunks means more metadata rows and more fragments to track. Bigger chunks cut the bookkeeping but coarsen everything. 64 MB is the usual middle — big enough that per-chunk overhead is noise, small enough to parallelize and repair fast.

Model C — erasure coding: survive more, store less

Here is the move that beats 3× replication. Take one 64 MB chunk and, instead of copying it whole, split it into k = 10 data fragments of 6.4 MB each, then compute m = 4 parity fragments (also 6.4 MB) with Reed–Solomon math. That's 14 fragments, and the magic property is: any 10 of the 14 can rebuild the original 64 MB. Store the 14 fragments on 14 different racks.

Worked: RS(10,4) vs replication, on the same 100 PB.

SchemeRaw disk for 100 PBFailures survivedOverhead
3× replication100 × 3 = 300 PB2+200%
5× replication100 × 5 = 500 PB4+400%
RS(10,4)100 × 14/10 = 140 PB4+40%

Read the last row against the middle one: to survive 4 simultaneous losses with replication you'd need 5 copies = 500 PB. RS(10,4) survives the same 4 losses on 140 PB — 500 / 140 ≈ 3.5× less disk, and still 1.6× less than plain 3× replication which only survives 2.

The disk saved vs 3×: 300 − 140 = 160 PB = 160,000 TB. At a round $20 / TB / month all-in, that's 160,000 × $20 = $3.2M / month ≈ $38M / year not spent — for more durability, not less.

Why does 14/10 give eleven-nines durability? Losing an object needs 5 of its 14 fragments gone before repair finishes. Fragments sit on 14 independent racks, and a lost fragment is rebuilt in minutes (read any 10 survivors, recompute the missing one). The window where a 5th failure could land during a repair is tiny, so the yearly odds are astronomically small — providers quote ~99.999999999% (eleven 9s). The lever is MTTR vs MTBF: fast repair (small mean-time-to-repair) shrinks the danger window, exactly the "close the gap" instinct from L06's lost update and L13's retry race.

Name the trade-off — erasure coding trades disk for CPU and repair traffic. EC wins big on storage (+40% vs +200%) and on fault tolerance, but it isn't free. Every write computes parity (CPU). Worse, repairing one lost 6.4 MB fragment means reading k = 10 other fragments — 10 × 6.4 = 64 MB pulled across the network to rebuild 6.4 MB: a 10× repair amplification. Replication's repair is a plain 1× copy. So EC is the right default for the cold, huge bulk; replication still wins for small, hot, latency-critical data where fast repair and single-fetch reads matter more than disk.

Move 3 — Trace the two paths

Two flows meet at the store: a car writing a clip, and a viewer reading one back.

Write path. Chunk, erasure-code, scatter the 14 fragments to 14 failure domains, and only when they're all durable do you commit the metadata. That commit is the single moment the object becomes real: before it, a crash leaves orphan fragments (garbage the background sweeper reclaims); after it, the object is durable. Writing the pointer last is the same "make the effect durable, then record it" discipline as L13's idempotent charge — the metadata commit is the atomic point-of-no-return. Read path. One metadata lookup gives the fragment map; fetch the 10 data fragments and concatenate (no math needed on the happy path); only if a data fragment is missing do you pull a parity and pay the decode. Every object read fans out to ~10 disks.

Name the trade-off — EC reads trade single-fetch simplicity for fan-out. A replicated read grabs one copy from one disk — lowest latency, easy tail. An EC read touches 10 fragments on 10 disks, so its latency is the slowest of the ten (the tail-amplification math from L17: one slow disk drags the whole read). You buy cheap, durable storage and pay in read fan-out and tail latency — which is exactly why hot, latency-sensitive objects often stay replicated while the cold bulk is erasure-coded.

Move 4 — Find the first bottleneck: the small-file problem

Everything above assumed 100 MB objects. Now the fleet starts uploading 4 KB thumbnails by the trillion, and every per-object cost that was noise becomes the whole bill.

Worked: what tiny objects do to the two bills. Keep 100 PB of data, but as 4 KB objects.

• Object count: 100 PB / 4 KB = 10¹⁷ / (4×10³) = 2.5 × 10¹³ → 25 trillion objects.
• Metadata: 2.5×10¹³ × 1 KB = 2.5 × 10¹⁶ B = 25 PB. The index is now a quarter the size of the data itself — and 25 trillion rows is its own distributed-systems nightmare, no longer a cozy 1 TB in RAM.
• Erasure coding a 4 KB object into RS(10,4) means fragments of 4096 / 10 ≈ 400 B. Fourteen 400-byte fragments scattered over 14 racks: every read is seek-bound, and each object drags 14 fragment pointers of metadata. The bookkeeping dwarfs the bytes.

The metadata row and the erasure stripe are fixed costs charged per object, not per byte. At 100 MB they're invisible; at 4 KB they are the system.

The fix is to stop treating a tiny object as its own storage unit. Pack many small objects, appended together, into one large (say 1 GB) container blob — and erasure-code the container, not each thumbnail. The metadata for a small object shrinks to object → (container id, offset, length): a handful of bytes you can keep in a compact in-memory index, one disk seek per read.

  • Packing kills the storage & IO overhead. One 1 GB container is one erasure stripe with 6.4 MB-scale fragments (not 400 B), and one sequential region on disk instead of a billion scattered tiny files. Reads become a single seek to an offset.
  • The index shrinks to fit in RAM. Instead of a fat 1 KB DB row per object, keep a lean key → (container, offset, len) map — a few bytes each — so even trillions stay tractable and a read is one lookup + one seek.
  • Deletes become tombstones + compaction. You can't punch a hole in a packed container cheaply, so a delete just marks a tombstone (the same trick as L15's feed deletes); a background compaction job later rewrites live objects into fresh containers and frees the old ones. Trade: cheap deletes now vs periodic rewrite traffic later — the classic log-structured bargain.

Name the last trade-off — per-object cost vs per-byte cost. A blob store's design assumes bytes dominate, so it spends structure (a metadata row, an erasure stripe, a scatter of fragments) per object and amortizes it over many bytes. Tiny objects break that assumption — the per-object structure costs more than the bytes it protects. Packing restores the assumption by making the storage unit big again (the container), at the price of an extra indirection and a compaction job. You either keep objects large, or you make them large by packing.

Where we landed

We designed one store — PUT/GET over 100 PB that survives failing disks — and made every choice pay for itself. Estimate killed the two obvious designs: a filesystem drowns in a 100 PB / 100 MB = 1 billion-entry tree, and 3× replication burns 300 PB of raw disk to survive just 2 failures. Model fixed both: a flat key→blob namespace that shards like L03/L04; a metadata plane (1 TB, consistent) split from the data plane (100 PB, durable) — a 100,000× size gap that justifies paying the expensive "always correct" price only on the small half; chunking big objects into 64 MB pieces; and erasure coding — RS(10,4) survives 4 losses on 140 PB, where matching that with replication needs 5 copies = 500 PB (≈3.5× more), saving 160 PB ≈ $38M/year for more durability. Tracing the write (chunk → code → scatter 14 frags → commit metadata last) and the read (metadata → fetch any 10 → decode only if needed) showed the durable-commit discipline of L13 and the read fan-out tail of L17. The next bottleneck was the small-file problem: 4 KB objects turn the 1 TB index into 25 PB and the erasure stripe into 400-byte splinters, because those costs are per-object, not per-byte — fixed by packing into containers with a compact in-RAM index and tombstone+compaction deletes. The thread: a blob store pays two separate bills — durability per raw byte (erasure code the cold bulk, replicate the hot bytes) and lookup per object (a tiny consistent index, packed when objects go small) — and every design choice is deciding which bill to keep down.
