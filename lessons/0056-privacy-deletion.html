Data Deletion & the Right to Be Forgotten: Erasing a User Across an Immutable, Sprawling System

For fifty-five lessons we made data spread and survive: cached in a hundred places (Lesson 48), replicated to edge KVs at 300 PoPs (Lesson 55), copied into a warehouse (Lesson 29), projected into read models (Lesson 37), and — the sharpest turn — written into an append-only event log we promised never to change (Lesson 38). Every one of those was a good idea. Together they build a trap: a user clicks "Delete my account," and her data is now in fifteen places, thirty-five nightly backups, and an immutable log by design. The law (GDPR's right to erasure, and its cousins) says you must actually make her gone — and "make her gone" now fights every durability decision you made. This lesson takes one concrete request — delete user U‑8842, "Alice," from a 200-million-user platform — and traces it end to end. We'll estimate how many copies of Alice exist and why deleting "her row" is the easy 1%. We'll model the two mechanisms that actually work — true deletion where you can rewrite, and crypto-shredding (throw away her key, Lesson 30) where you can't — plus the deletion registry and the suppression tombstone that keep her from coming back. We'll trace the trivial primary-DB delete, the immutable event log that cannot be edited, and the backups/warehouse/edge/third-party sprawl. Then the wall: you cannot delete what you cannot find, and you cannot rewrite what you made immutable — so deletion cost grows with every copy your architecture creates. The trade underneath: compliance & privacy vs immutability & derived-data sprawl.

The one system we'll design

Our platform has 200 million users. Over fifty-five lessons we built it: a primary orders database (Lesson 12/24), an append-only event log that is the source of truth (Lesson 38), read models projected off that log via change data capture (Lesson 33/37), a product/profile cache layer (Lesson 48), an edge KV replicated to 300 PoPs (Lesson 55), a data warehouse for analytics (Lesson 29), a search index (Lesson 16), tracing/logs (Lesson 17), nightly backups, and webhooks that shipped some of Alice's data to third parties we don't own (Lesson 45).

Today Alice exercises her right to erasure: "delete everything you have about me." Legally we have about 30 days to comply (GDPR gives one month). The naive engineer runs DELETE FROM users WHERE id = 'U-8842', sees "1 row affected," and calls it done. That single row is roughly 1% of the problem. Alice's data is also sitting in fourteen other systems, in thirty-five sealed nightly backups, and — worst of all — inside an event log we promised the rest of the architecture we would never rewrite. Erasure has to reach all of it, and some of it was deliberately built to resist change.

Why is this hard, in one sentence per force:

  • Sprawl — every copy you made for speed or analysis (cache, projection, warehouse, edge, export) is a new place deletion must reach. The number of copies is the number of deletes.
  • Immutability — the event log (Lesson 38), backups, and any WORM audit store were built to be un-editable. You literally cannot DELETE one user's rows out of a sealed snapshot without rebuilding the whole thing.
  • Resurrection — even after you delete Alice, a backup restore or a replayed event can bring her back. Deletion isn't a one-shot event; it's a state you must defend.
  • Retention conflict — the law that says "delete her" coexists with laws that say "keep financial records seven years" (Lesson 25). Erasure is not absolute; it yields to legal holds.

Deleting a user means reaching every copy of their personal data across a sprawling, partly-immutable system — by truly deleting it where you can rewrite, and by destroying the key that decrypts it where you can't (crypto-shredding) — while keeping a minimal tombstone so the deletion survives restores and replays. "Delete the row" is the visible tip; the lesson is the fourteen other stores, the thirty-five backups, and the immutable log underneath.

Words we'll earn before we lean on them

  • PII (personally identifiable information) — any data that identifies a person: name, email, address, device id, and often behavior tied to them. The stuff erasure must remove.
  • Right to erasure / right to be forgotten — a legal right (GDPR Article 17, and similar laws) letting a person demand you delete their personal data, usually within about a month.
  • True deletion — actually removing the bytes: DELETE the row, overwrite the record, drop the document. Works only where the store lets you rewrite.
  • Crypto-shredding (crypto-erase) — encrypt each user's data with a per-user key; to "delete" the user, destroy their key. The bytes remain but are now unreadable noise. The one method that works on immutable stores (Lesson 30).
  • DEK (data encryption key) — the per-user key that encrypts one user's data. Small (32 bytes), stored in a key store / KMS, and the single thing crypto-shredding destroys.
  • Data map / personal-data registry — the inventory of every system that holds PII. You cannot delete what you cannot find; this is the map that says where to look.
  • Deletion orchestrator — the job that fans the deletion out to every registered store, idempotently and with retries until each one acknowledges (Lesson 13/32/46).
  • Tombstone / suppression list — a tiny record that "this user was deleted," so a backup restore or replayed event can't silently re-create them. Holds a hashed id, not the PII.
  • Legal hold / retention obligation — a rule that forces you to keep certain data (tax, financial, litigation) even against an erasure request. The exception that makes erasure not absolute.

Move 1 — Estimate: how many copies of Alice exist, and why the row is 1%

Two counts decide the shape of this problem: how many places hold Alice, and how expensive it is to reach the immutable ones. Get the second wrong and you'll try to edit a backup — which is where designs die.

1. The copy count. Alice's data lives in roughly 15 systems (primary DB, event log, ~3 read models, cache, edge KV, search index, warehouse, logs/traces, plus exports). Nightly backups keep 35 daily snapshots (a 35-day retention window), so the backup tier alone holds 35 more copies of her. The primary-DB row you'd DELETE is 1 of ~50 copies — about 2%. "1 row affected" is a rounding error against the real surface. The work is the other ~49 copies, and a third of them are immutable.

2. Why you can't just rewrite the immutable ones. Suppose the primary DB is 10 TB. A nightly backup is a sealed 10 TB snapshot; you cannot surgically remove Alice from it. To truly edit her out you'd restore it, delete her, and re-seal — 10 TB of work per backup, × 35 backups = 350 TB moved for one user. At even 100,000 erasure requests a month (a modest 0.05% of 200M users) that's 350 TB × 100,000 = 35 exabytes of restore-edit-reseal per month. That's not "expensive," it's arithmetic that says impossible. Rewriting immutable stores per user does not scale — you need a method whose cost per deletion is O(1), not O(data).

3. The size of the thing crypto-shredding actually deletes. Give every user one 256-bit (32-byte) DEK. All 200M keys together are 200,000,000 × 32 B = 6.4 GB — a rounding error you can keep in a fast key store. Deleting Alice becomes "delete one 32-byte key," and her ciphertext everywhere — event log, all 35 backups, the warehouse — turns to noise at once, without touching a single one of them. Crypto-shredding converts an O(exabytes) hunt-and-rewrite into an O(32 bytes) key delete. That single trade is the whole reason the problem is tractable.

Name the trade-off — compliance & privacy vs immutability & derived-data sprawl. Every earlier lesson bought performance and auditability by making data durable and copied: immutable logs you can replay, backups you can restore, projections and caches and edge replicas everywhere. Erasure is the bill for all of it. You pay in two currencies: reach (deletion must touch every copy the sprawl created) and rewritability (immutability was a feature you now have to defeat). Crypto-shredding pays both at once — but shifts the burden onto key management: now the key is the thing that must be perfectly findable and perfectly destroyable.

Move 2 — Model: two deletion mechanisms, one registry, one tombstone

Deletion is not one operation. It's a small kit of four parts, each answering a question the naive DELETE ignores: where is she, how do I erase each place, and how do I keep her gone?

1. The data map — you can't delete what you can't find

The first and hardest part isn't code, it's an inventory: every system that stores PII, what field, and how to delete it there. New table, new cache, new export? It joins the registry, or erasure silently misses it. A deletion is only as complete as your map of where personal data went — and derived-data sprawl means the map grows every time someone adds a projection or a pipeline. This is why privacy is an architecture property, not a feature you bolt on: an untracked copy is an undeletable copy.

2. True deletion vs crypto-shredding — pick by "can I rewrite this store?"

True deletion works wherever the store is mutable: the primary DB row, read models (Lesson 37 — re-derive them without Alice), the search index, a cache entry. You issue a real DELETE/overwrite and the bytes are gone. Simple, complete, auditable — where it's allowed.

Crypto-shredding works wherever the store is immutable or unreachable: the append-only event log (Lesson 38), sealed backups, WORM audit stores, and anything already flushed to slow tiers. You encrypted each user's PII with their DEK at write time; to erase, you destroy the DEK. The event stays in the log (chain intact, history honest — "an event happened") but its personal payload is now undecryptable noise. The rule is mechanical: rewrite where you can (true delete), destroy the key where you can't (crypto-shred). One dial — is this store rewritable? — chooses the mechanism.

The catch that makes or breaks crypto-shred: "delete the key" only counts if every copy of the key is gone. A key sitting in a key-store backup, a config file, or a warm cache means the ciphertext is still readable — so it's not deleted. Crypto-shredding doesn't remove the deletion problem; it moves it from "reach every copy of the data" to "reach every copy of the key" — a far smaller, more controllable surface (6.4 GB of keys vs petabytes of data), which is exactly why it wins. Keep keys in a store with short backup retention and explicit crypto-erase, and the key surface stays small.

3. The tombstone — stop the resurrection

Delete Alice today; tomorrow a backup restore for an unrelated outage brings her row back, or a webhook you sent last week gets replayed and re-imports her. Deletion is a state to defend, not an event. So you keep a suppression tombstone: a tiny record — hash(U-8842) + deletion timestamp + status — that every re-materialization path checks. A restore or replay that tries to re-create a tombstoned user is dropped. The paradox: to prove someone is forgotten, you must remember that you forgot them — so you keep the fact of deletion (a hashed id, no PII), never the data. That same tombstone is your audit trail: proof to a regulator that erasure happened, and when.

4. The orchestrator — idempotent fan-out with retries

Fifteen stores plus a key-delete plus third-party forwards won't all succeed on the first try. So deletion runs like a saga (Lesson 32) or a durable job (Lesson 46): fan out to each registered store, each step idempotent (Lesson 13 — deleting an already-deleted user is a no-op, not an error) and retried until it acknowledges. The request is complete only when every store has confirmed — then you record completion on the tombstone.

The four properties that make erasure real, named: completeness (a data map so no copy is missed), irreversibility (true-delete or key-destroy so the bytes can't be recovered), durability of the deletion (a tombstone so restores/replays can't resurrect), and provability (an audit record you can show a regulator). Miss completeness and you leak a copy; miss irreversibility and you only hid the data; miss durability and she comes back; miss provability and you can't prove any of it happened.

Move 3 — Trace the paths

Follow Alice's deletion through three tiers: the trivial mutable store, the immutable log that refuses to be edited, and the backup/warehouse/edge/third-party sprawl.

Path A — the primary DB and read models (the easy 1%)

  DELETE FROM users, orders WHERE user_id = 'U-8842'   -> a few ms, bytes gone.

  The read models (L37) are PROJECTIONS off the log (L33 CDC). Two options:
    (a) emit a "user-deleted" event -> the projector removes Alice's rows, or
    (b) re-derive the projection from the (now crypto-shredded) log; her
        entries decode to noise and are dropped on rebuild.
  Search index (L16): DELETE the doc. Cache (L48): evict / let TTL expire.

  This tier is genuinely easy: every store here is MUTABLE. This is the part
  the naive engineer does -- and then stops. It's ~1-2% of the copies.

Path B — the immutable event log (the part you cannot rewrite)

  Alice generated ~5,000 events over her lifetime, scattered through an
  append-only, hash-chained log (L38). You want event #4,412 gone.

  NAIVE:  "just delete event #4,412 from the log."
  REALITY: the log is immutable BY DESIGN. Deleting one event breaks the
           hash chain and invalidates every event after it, and every read
           model and replay that depends on the log. You'd be un-building
           Lesson 38 to satisfy Lesson 56.

  CRYPTO-SHRED:  at write time, each event's PII fields were encrypted with
                 Alice's DEK. To erase:  destroy Alice's DEK (32 bytes).
     -> event #4,412 STILL EXISTS (chain intact, "an event happened at T"),
        but its personal payload is now undecryptable noise. History stays
        honest; the person is gone. One key delete erases ALL 5,000 events
        at once, without touching the log.

  This is why you encrypt-per-user at write time even before anyone asks to
  be deleted: crypto-shred only works if the data was BORN encrypted.

Path C — backups, warehouse, edge, and the data you already gave away

  BACKUPS (35 sealed snapshots): you cannot edit one user out of a snapshot.
    -> crypto-shred covers them: Alice's ciphertext is in every backup, but
       the key is gone, so it's unreadable. Each backup also AGES OUT on its
       own retention clock (<=35 days), after which the ciphertext is gone too.

  WAREHOUSE (L29, 3.65 PB, partitioned by day): Alice is smeared across
    thousands of daily Parquet files. Per-row deletes are ruinous.
    -> crypto-shred, OR a scheduled partition-rewrite job (L46) that rebuilds
       affected partitions without her on the next compaction.

  EDGE KV (L55, 300 PoPs): propagate a delete / let the short TTL expire.
    Eventually consistent -- gone from all PoPs within seconds-to-minutes.

  THIRD PARTIES (L45): you already SHIPPED some of Alice's data out via
    webhooks. You no longer hold that copy. You must FORWARD the erasure
    request to them (a contractual/legal duty) and record that you did.
    You control your reach; you can only REQUEST theirs.

  Every one of these copies exists because an earlier lesson made data spread
  or survive. Deletion is the bill for durability + sprawl.

Path A is the 1% everyone does. Path B is where the design is decided: you cannot delete from a store you built to be immutable, so you delete the key instead — which only works if the data was encrypted per-user before anyone asked. Path C is the reminder that sprawl is unbounded: every backup, warehouse partition, edge replica, and export is another copy erasure must reach, and the ones you already handed to third parties you can only ask them to delete.

Move 4 — Find the first bottleneck: you can't delete what you can't find, or rewrite what you made immutable

There's no clever runtime that makes this cheap the way isolates made cold starts cheap (Lesson 55). The wall is structural, and it has two faces that crypto-shredding pushes against but never fully removes.

1. The reach wall (derived-data sprawl). Deletion cost is proportional to how many copies your architecture made. Every cache (Lesson 48), projection (Lesson 37), warehouse table (Lesson 29), edge replica (Lesson 55), and export (Lesson 45) is one more place a delete must land — and one more place a missed registry entry silently leaks Alice forever. The number of deletes is the number of copies, and copies are what all our performance work produced. The only scalable defense is to make deletion O(1) regardless of copy count — encrypt per-user everywhere, so a single key-destroy erases every copy at once. Then reach stops mattering, because you never chase the copies; you kill what makes them readable.

2. The immutability wall. The event log (Lesson 38), backups, and WORM audit stores were deliberately un-editable — that was the point. Erasure directly contradicts that guarantee. You do not resolve it by weakening immutability (that un-builds Lesson 38); you resolve it by separating the fact from the payload: keep the immutable event ("something happened at T"), encrypt the personal payload with the user's key, and let key-destruction remove the person while the honest, replayable history survives. Immutability and erasure only coexist because encryption lets you delete the meaning of a record without deleting the record.

Two more walls sit behind those. Key management becomes the crown jewel: crypto-shred is only as good as your ability to destroy every copy of a key — a key in a backup, a cache, or a config that outlives the delete leaves the data readable (Lesson 30's key hierarchy is now load-bearing for privacy, not just secrecy). And erasure is not absolute: a legal hold or a retention obligation (financial records kept seven years, Lesson 25; data under litigation) can force you to keep specific data against the request — so real systems do partial erasure, deleting everything not legally pinned and recording why the rest remains. The deepest point: privacy-by-deletion is really privacy-by-encryption. You don't chase petabytes of copies across fifteen systems and thirty-five backups — you make the data born-encrypted per user, and reduce "forget this person" to "destroy this 32-byte key, everywhere it exists." The hard part was never the DELETE; it was the sprawl and the immutability we built on purpose.

The four traps

  • Trap 1 — "DELETE the row" is deletion. One row is ~1‑2% of the copies. Erasure must fan out to every store in the data map — read models, caches, search, warehouse, edge, backups, exports — or Alice persists in fourteen places you didn't look. Deletion completeness is a property of your registry, not your SQL.
  • Trap 2 — trying to rewrite immutable stores per user. Editing one user out of an append-only log breaks the hash chain and every downstream replay; editing 35 sealed backups is exabytes of restore-and-reseal. Use crypto-shredding: encrypt per-user at write time, then destroy the key. Rewrite where mutable, key-destroy where not.
  • Trap 3 — forgetting the resurrection path. A backup restore or a replayed event/webhook silently re-creates a deleted user. Without a suppression tombstone, deletion doesn't stick. Deletion is a state you defend, not a one-shot command — every re-materialization path must check the tombstone.
  • Trap 4 — crypto-shred, but a copy of the key survives. A DEK left in a key-store backup, a config, or a warm cache leaves the ciphertext readable — so the data is not deleted. Crypto-shred just moves the target from data to key; keep the key surface tiny (short retention, explicit crypto-erase) and destroy it everywhere.

Where we landed

The right to be forgotten is the bill for fifty-five lessons of making data durable and copied. Estimate counted the copies — Alice lives in ~15 systems and 35 backups, so the primary-DB row is ~1‑2%, and truly rewriting the immutable tier is exabytes of restore-and-reseal per month (impossible), while destroying one 32-byte key erases every copy at once (6.4 GB of keys for all 200M users). Model gave the kit: a data map (you can't delete what you can't find), two mechanisms chosen by "is this store rewritable?" — true deletion where mutable, crypto-shredding where not — a suppression tombstone so restores and replays can't resurrect her, and an idempotent orchestrator to fan out with retries. Tracing walked the trivial mutable delete (the 1%), the immutable event log where you destroy the key because you can't touch the log, and the backup/warehouse/edge/third-party sprawl where crypto-shred plus retention age-out plus forwarded requests do the work. The first bottleneck is structural: you cannot delete what you cannot find (sprawl) or rewrite what you made immutable (the log, backups) — so privacy-by-deletion becomes privacy-by-encryption: born-encrypted per user, deletion collapses to destroying one key everywhere it exists — bounded by key management as the new crown jewel and by legal holds that make erasure partial, not absolute. Delete the meaning, not the record; keep the proof, not the person. The standing tension: compliance & privacy vs immutability & derived-data sprawl.
