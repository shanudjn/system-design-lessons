Multi-Tenancy & Noisy Neighbors: Many Customers on One Shared System

Every lesson so far designed a system for one workload. But almost every SaaS runs one shared system for thousands of paying customers at once — that's the whole business model: pack many tenants onto the fleet you already built (Lesson 27's 286 servers, 40k req/s) and the database you already sharded (Lesson 3), so each tenant costs pennies to serve. The catch is a single word: shared. A shared connection pool, a shared cache, a shared queue — every one of them is a resource that all tenants draw from the same pot. So the instant one tenant misbehaves — a runaway export, a bad query, a traffic spike, an abusive script — it can drain that pot and starve everyone else. That's the noisy neighbor: one customer's problem becomes all customers' problem, through a resource they didn't know they were sharing. This lesson takes one concrete system — a B2B SaaS with 50,000 tenants on that shared fleet and one Postgres cluster — and defends it. We'll estimate how much damage one greedy tenant does through a shared connection pool (a 100% blast radius from 0.002% of the tenants); model the isolation spectrum (shared table → schema → database → a whole cell per tenant) and the three fairness controls that tame the pot — per-tenant rate limits (Lesson 8), concurrency bulkheads (Lesson 7), and fair queuing; trace a normal request, the same spike with and without isolation, and a tenant that has genuinely outgrown the pool; and hit the first wall — a rate limit counts requests, not work, so one cheap-looking query can still burn the fleet. The trade running underneath: density & cost efficiency vs isolation & fairness.

The one system we'll design

A B2B SaaS — say a project tracker. 50,000 tenant companies share one platform: the same fleet of app servers (Lesson 27) and one Postgres cluster whose app-side connection pool holds 300 connections. Every request carries a tenant_id; rows are tagged with it (WHERE tenant_id = ?). Total traffic is 40,000 req/s. This is the cheapest possible design — one stack, all tenants — and that cheapness is exactly why it's dangerous: everything is shared, so everything is a channel through which one tenant can hurt another. The question this lesson answers is not how to serve a tenant (we know how). It's how to stop one tenant from taking down the other 49,999.

  ONE SHARED STACK, MANY TENANTS  (every box is a resource they all draw from)

     tenant A  tenant B  ... tenant Z ...   (50,000 companies, one tenant_id each)
         \        |            /
          \       |           /
        +--------------------------+
        |   shared app fleet       |   286 servers, 40k req/s   (Lesson 27)
        +--------------------------+
                    |
        +--------------------------+
        |  shared connection pool  |   300 connections   <-- the pot everyone dips into
        +--------------------------+
                    |
        +--------------------------+
        |    shared Postgres       |   rows tagged WHERE tenant_id = ?   (Lesson 3)
        +--------------------------+

  The pool is FINITE and SHARED. If one tenant grabs most of the 300 connections,
  the other 49,999 wait. One tenant's spike is now everyone's outage. That is the
  NOISY NEIGHBOR, and it happens through a resource nobody chose to share on purpose.

Multi-tenancy means many independent customers share one system's resources; a noisy neighbor is one tenant whose demand on a shared resource degrades service for the others. The whole discipline is: get the cost savings of sharing, without letting the sharing become a channel for one tenant to harm the rest. You do that by putting limits and walls around each tenant's slice of every shared pot.

Words we'll earn before we lean on them

  • Tenant — one customer (a company, an account) whose data and traffic are logically separate from every other customer's, even though they run on the same system. Identified by a tenant_id.
  • Multi-tenancy — one running system serving many tenants at once. Its opposite is single-tenancy (a private stack per customer): more isolation, far more cost.
  • Noisy neighbor — a tenant whose load on a shared resource (CPU, connections, cache, a queue) steals capacity from the others.
  • Isolation — how well one tenant's behavior is walled off from another's. More isolation = a smaller blast radius when one tenant misbehaves (Lesson 36).
  • Density — how many tenants you pack onto one unit of infrastructure. High density is cheap; it trades directly against isolation.
  • Quota — a hard cap on how much of a resource one tenant may consume over time (storage, rows, API calls/day). A fixed ceiling.
  • Fair share — the slice of a shared resource a tenant is entitled to right now. Can be static (a fixed cap) or dynamic (its cut of whatever's free).
  • Fair queuing — instead of one shared line where a flood from one tenant blocks all others, give each tenant its own line and serve them in rotation, so no tenant can monopolize the server.
  • Bulkhead — a per-tenant cap on in-flight work (Lesson 7): a tenant may hold at most N of the shared connections/threads at once, so it can never drain the whole pool.

Move 1 — Estimate: how much damage does one greedy tenant do?

Two facts collide. First, tenant usage is wildly skewed (Lesson 2's Zipf, yet again): the average tenant does 40,000 / 50,000 = 0.8 req/s, but that average is a lie — most tenants do almost nothing while a handful of big enterprises do thousands of req/s. Second, the pool is finite and shared. Put those together and you can price the blast radius of one bad actor.

The pool's normal headroom. 300 connections; a normal query holds one for ~5 ms. By Little's Law (Lesson 13), throughput = concurrency ÷ hold-time = 300 / 0.005 s = 60,000 queries/s of capacity — comfortably above the 40,000 req/s the whole platform needs. In steady state everyone is served in single-digit milliseconds. So far, sharing looks like a pure win.

Now one tenant misbehaves. At 09:00 a single enterprise tenant kicks off a nightly bulk export: 200 heavy report queries, each a full-table scan holding a connection for ~2 seconds (Lesson 12's un-indexed scan). Those 200 queries grab and hold 200 of the 300 connections for two seconds at a stretch. That leaves 300 − 200 = 100 connections for everyone else.

The blast radius. Remaining capacity for the other 49,999 tenants collapses to 100 / 0.005 s = 20,000 queries/s — but demand is still 40,000 req/s. Demand now exceeds capacity by 20,000/s, so the queue in front of the pool grows without bound (Lesson 28), latency leaves the knee (Lesson 27), and requests start timing out across the board. One tenant — 1 / 50,000 = 0.002% of the customer base — just degraded 100% of the platform. It didn't attack anything. It didn't exceed any per-request rule. It simply drew hard from a pot everyone shares.

Name the trade-off — density vs isolation. The reason the blast radius is 100% is the same reason the platform is cheap: everything is shared. Maximum density (one pool for all 50,000) gives maximum cost efficiency and zero isolation — any tenant can drain the shared pot. Maximum isolation (a private pool per tenant) gives each tenant a wall no neighbor can cross, at 50,000× the infrastructure. Every multi-tenancy decision is a point on the line between these two: how much money you'll spend on walls to shrink the blast radius. The rest of this lesson is how to buy a lot of isolation without paying for a private stack per tenant.

Move 2 — Model: the isolation spectrum and the three fairness controls

Two questions to answer. First, how separate is each tenant's data and infrastructure? — that's the isolation spectrum. Second, given a shared resource, how do you divide it fairly so no tenant can take more than its share? — that's the three controls.

The isolation spectrum: shared → siloed

  FOUR PLACES TO PUT THE WALL  (top = cheap/dense/leaky, bottom = costly/isolated)

  model                    what's shared              density   isolation   cost/tenant
  -----------------------  -------------------------  --------  ----------  -----------
  1. shared table          one table, tenant_id col   HIGHEST   LOWEST      pennies
     (WHERE tenant_id=?)    everything shared                    a bad query hurts all
  2. schema per tenant      same DB, separate schema   high      low-mid     low
     (table per tenant)     still one engine/pool                one hot tenant, one DB
  3. database per tenant    own DB, shared hardware    mid       high        higher
                            noisy neighbor bounded to its own DB
  4. cell per tenant        whole stack, own cell      LOWEST    HIGHEST     highest
     (Lesson 36)            nothing shared -> 1/N blast radius, physically

  You don't pick ONE for all tenants. Small tenants share a table (cheap);
  a giant enterprise gets its own database or cell (isolated). Match the wall
  to the tenant -- density where you can afford it, isolation where you can't.

The spectrum decides where data lives. But the incident in Move 1 happened at the shared connection pool, which even models 1–3 keep sharing. So you also need controls that carve up a live shared resource in real time. There are three, and they answer three different questions:

1. Per-tenant rate limit (Lesson 8) — "how fast may this tenant ask?" Give each tenant_id its own token bucket, sized by its plan (e.g. 500 req/s sustained, burst 1,000). Requests past the bucket get an honest 429 (Lesson 7). This is the key move Lesson 8 hinted at: the limit is keyed on tenant, not on one global counter — 50,000 independent buckets, so a flood from one drains only its own.

2. Concurrency bulkhead (Lesson 7) — "how much may this tenant hold at once?" A rate limit counts arrivals per second; a bulkhead caps in-flight work. Cap each tenant at, say, 30 of the 300 connections simultaneously. Now the Move-1 export — 200 queries wanting connections — can only ever tie up 30, no matter how many it fires. The rest queue inside that tenant's own slice. This is the control that directly stops the pool-drain, because the drain was about concurrency (2-second holds), not arrival rate.

3. Fair queuing — "when the resource is scarce, whose request goes next?" One shared FIFO lets a tenant that dumped 10,000 requests block a tenant with 1 waiting behind them (head-of-line blocking). Instead, give each tenant its own sub-queue and serve them round-robin (or weighted by plan). A tenant with a huge backlog waits on its own queue; a quiet tenant's lone request is served on the next rotation. No tenant's flood delays another's trickle.

Name the trade-off — a static cap wastes idle capacity; work-conserving fairness reclaims it but must give it back instantly. The simplest fair share is a fixed cap: every tenant gets 30 connections, full stop. Easy to reason about, but if 49,999 tenants are idle, their 30-slot allowances sit unused while a busy tenant is throttled at 30 — you've left most of the pool empty. A work-conserving scheme (weighted fair queuing) instead lets a tenant borrow the idle capacity of others — until another tenant shows up, at which point it must reclaim its share immediately. That's strictly better utilization, but harder: the whole risk is a borrower that won't let go fast enough, which reopens the noisy-neighbor door. Static caps trade utilization for simplicity and safety; work-conserving fairness trades simplicity for utilization, and lives or dies on how fast it can preempt a borrower.

Move 3 — Trace the paths

Four runs: a normal request (the cheap common case), the Move-1 spike without isolation (the outage), the same spike with the bulkhead (contained), and a tenant that has genuinely outgrown the shared pool (route it out).

Path A — a normal request (small tenant, the 99% case)

  GET /projects   (tenant_id = B, a 5-person company)

   1. Edge checks tenant B's rate-limit bucket -> has tokens, allow. (Lesson 8)
   2. App borrows 1 of 300 pool connections -> within B's 30-conn bulkhead. (Lesson 7)
   3. Query: SELECT ... WHERE tenant_id = B  -> indexed, ~5 ms, release connection.
   4. Return. B used 1 connection for 5 ms out of a 300-connection pool. Invisible to all.

  This is why sharing pays: 49,999 small tenants like B cost almost nothing each,
  because each touches the shared pot briefly and lets go.

Path B — the spike WITHOUT isolation (Move 1 replayed: the outage)

  tenant E (enterprise) fires 200 x 2-second full-scan report queries

   1. No per-tenant bulkhead -> E grabs connections as fast as it can.
   2. 200 of 300 connections held for ~2 s each. Only 100 left for everyone.
   3. Platform capacity: 100 / 0.005 = 20,000 q/s  <  40,000 q/s demand.
   4. Shared queue grows unbounded (L28); latency past the knee (L27); timeouts.
   5. RESULT: all 49,999 other tenants degraded. Blast radius 100%, from 0.002% of tenants.

  E didn't break a rule. It just drew hard from a pot with no per-tenant wall.

Path C — the SAME spike WITH a per-tenant bulkhead (contained)

  tenant E fires the same 200 report queries -- but now capped at 30 connections

   1. E's bulkhead = 30 of 300 connections. It grabs 30; query 31+ queues in E's OWN slice.
   2. Pool left for everyone else: 300 - 30 = 270 connections.
   3. Their capacity: 270 / 0.005 = 54,000 q/s  >  40,000 q/s demand.  -> nobody starves.
   4. E's export still runs -- just serialized through 30 connections, so it takes longer.
      The pain of E's greed is paid BY E (a slow export), not by the other 49,999.

  Same load, same pool. One per-tenant wall moved the blast radius from 100% to ZERO.
  The noisy neighbor is now only noisy in its own room.

Path D — a tenant that has genuinely outgrown the shared pool (route it out)

  tenant E keeps growing: it legitimately needs 5,000 req/s, sustained.

   Bulkheading E to 30 connections now HURTS E -- it's a real, paying, heavy tenant,
   not an abuser. The fix isn't a tighter wall; it's a BIGGER ROOM:

   1. The tenant ROUTER (tenant_id -> location) moves E down the isolation spectrum:
        give E its own database (model 3) or its own cell (model 4, Lesson 36).
   2. E now has a private pool it cannot share -> it can use all of it, harming no one.
   3. The shared pool serves only the 49,999 small tenants -> back to comfortable headroom.

  Routing (tenant_id -> shard/DB/cell) is the knob that lets you place each tenant at the
  RIGHT point on the spectrum: dense for the small, siloed for the large. (L3/L4/L36)

Path A is why multi-tenancy is cheap; Path B is the disaster that "just share everything" invites; Path C is the per-tenant wall that fixes it for pennies; Path D is what you do when a tenant deserves more room, not less. But look again at Path C. The bulkhead capped E at 30 connections — 30 requests in flight. What if E stays within 30 connections but each of those 30 requests is a query that scans 100 million rows? The wall counts slots, not work. That's the first bottleneck.

Move 4 — Find the first bottleneck: a limit counts requests, not work

Every control in Move 2 meters the number of something — requests per second, connections held, position in a queue. None of them meters how expensive each of those things is. And tenant workloads vary in cost by orders of magnitude: one request lists 10 rows from an index; another runs an unindexed aggregate over the tenant's entire 100-million-row history (Lesson 12's selectivity trap). Both are "one request." Both fit inside a 30-connection bulkhead. One costs 5 ms; the other costs 40 seconds of CPU.

Worked: the cheap-looking request that isn't. Tenant E, now well-behaved on request count (well under its 500 req/s and 30-connection caps), sends just 10 requests, each an un-indexed report over its 100M rows: 20 GB scan ÷ 500 MB/s ≈ 40 s of work each (Lesson 12). Ten of them held for 40 s = 10 connections tied up for 40 seconds. E is inside every count-based limit and still monopolizing capacity — because the limits never priced the work per request. Counting requests told you nothing about the CPU and I/O each one would burn.

The fix: meter cost, not count. Charge each tenant against a budget denominated in the resource that's actually scarce — CPU-seconds, rows scanned, or bytes read — not requests. A cost-based limiter (a token bucket whose tokens are CPU-milliseconds, Lesson 8 generalized) drains fast for a 40 s scan and slowly for a 5 ms lookup, so a tenant's expensive traffic is throttled even when its request count is tiny. Pair it with per-tenant query guardrails: statement timeouts, row-scan limits, forced LIMITs — so no single request can run away in the first place.

Name the trade-off — measurable-but-wrong vs accurate-but-costly limits. Request count is trivial to measure and enforce at the edge, but it's a poor proxy for load, so it lets expensive tenants through. Cost (CPU-seconds, rows) is the right thing to limit, but you often can't know a request's cost until after you've run it — so cost-based limiting is reactive (meter what a tenant spent, throttle its next request) and needs real accounting plumbing. The deeper lesson: fairness is only as good as the unit you meter. Meter the wrong unit and a tenant games it for free — so meter the resource that is actually scarce, even when it's harder to measure.

Past the metering wall, three more walls decide whether your multi-tenant design holds:

  THE THREE WALLS AFTER THE METERING WALL

  1. The SHARED RESOURCE is still one blast surface (L36).  Even with perfect fairness,
        a bug or a poison query in the ONE shared DB/cache/pool can hurt every tenant on it.
        -> SHUFFLE-SHARDING: split the resource into N partitions, assign each tenant a random
           SUBSET of k. Two tenants are fully co-victim only if their whole subsets coincide.
           100 partitions, k=2 -> C(100,2)=4,950 combos -> a given pair fully overlaps with
           probability ~1/4,950 ~ 0.02% (vs 100% when everything is shared). The AWS trick.

  2. The HOT TENANT problem (L3/L19).  Tenant usage is Zipf; one tenant is 100x the rest, so a
        single tenant's DATA (not just traffic) becomes a hot shard no bulkhead can fix.
        -> Route the whale to its own database/cell (Path D); don't force one wall on all sizes.

  3. The NOISY-NEIGHBOR you can't rate-limit: BACKGROUND work.  Rate limits guard the request
        path, but tenants also share async workers (L5/L9), cron jobs, and the cache (L2 -- one
        tenant's giant scan evicts everyone's hot keys).
        -> Per-tenant queues/quotas on the async tier too, and per-tenant cache budgets. Every
           shared pot needs a per-tenant wall, not just the front door.

The four traps

  • Trap 1 — a limit that counts requests but not their cost. A tenant inside every per-second and per-connection cap can still run 10 full-table scans and monopolize the fleet. Count-based limits are a proxy for load, and a leaky one. Meter the scarce resource itself — CPU-seconds, rows, bytes — and add per-query guardrails (timeouts, row caps) so one request can't run away.
  • Trap 2 — one global limit instead of one per tenant. A single shared rate limit or a single shared FIFO queue re-creates the shared pot: one tenant's flood fills it and every other tenant hits the wall or waits behind the flood (head-of-line blocking). Keep the counter, the bucket, and the queue per tenant_id, so one tenant's excess drains only its own allowance.
  • Trap 3 — forcing one isolation model on every tenant. Bulkheading a giant, paying tenant to a tiny slice punishes a good customer; sharing a table with an abusive one endangers the rest. Usage is Zipf — match the wall to the tenant: dense (shared table) for the 49,999 small ones, siloed (own DB or cell) for the few whales, chosen by the tenant router.
  • Trap 4 — walling the front door but leaving the back doors open. Rate-limiting the HTTP path while the shared async workers, cron jobs, and cache have no per-tenant limits just moves the noisy neighbor somewhere you're not looking — one tenant's batch job starves everyone's background processing, or its scan evicts everyone's cache. Every shared resource, sync or async, needs its own per-tenant wall.

Where we landed

Multi-tenancy is the SaaS business model — pack 50,000 tenants onto one shared fleet (Lesson 27) and one database (Lesson 3) so each costs pennies — and its single risk is the word shared: every shared resource is a channel through which one tenant can starve the rest, the noisy neighbor. We took a B2B SaaS with a 300-connection shared pool and defended it. Estimate priced the danger: normal load fits in 60,000 q/s of headroom, but one tenant's 200-query, 2-second-each export grabs 200 of 300 connections, dropping everyone else's capacity to 20,000 q/s below 40,000 demand — a 100% blast radius from 0.002% of the tenants, with no rule broken. Model gave the isolation spectrum (shared table → schema → database → cell per tenant, trading density for isolation) plus three controls that carve a live shared pot: per-tenant rate limits (Lesson 8, one bucket per tenant_id), concurrency bulkheads (Lesson 7, a cap on in-flight slots), and fair queuing (a line per tenant, not one shared FIFO). Tracing showed the cheap normal request, the spike without isolation (the 100% outage), the same spike with a 30-connection bulkhead (270 left → 54,000 q/s → zero impact, the greed paid by the greedy), and a genuine whale routed out to its own database. The first bottleneck — a limit counts requests, not work — bites when a tenant stays inside every count-based cap yet sends 10 full-table scans at 40 s each; the fix is to meter the scarce resource itself (CPU-seconds, rows, bytes) plus per-query guardrails, then to shuffle-shard the shared resource (Lesson 36) so no bug has a 100% blast radius, route hot tenants out (Lesson 3/19), and put per-tenant walls on the async tier and cache too — not just the front door. The real lesson: sharing is what makes multi-tenancy cheap, and the only thing that makes it safe is a per-tenant wall around every shared pot, sized by the resource that's actually scarce.
