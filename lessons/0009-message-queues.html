Message Queues: The Log, Exactly-Once & Ordering

In Lesson 05 we pushed a slow bulk job onto a queue and let workers drain it. That bought us asynchrony, and we met its sharp edges in passing — at-least-once delivery, duplicates, dead-letter queues. This lesson takes ONE high-volume event stream — every redirect on our URL shortener emits a click event — and traces it through a log-based message queue end to end. We'll see why a log is not the same shape as a mailbox, why "exactly-once delivery" is a polite fiction that real systems quietly replace with something honest, and why the simple act of counting in order forces a trade you can't dodge. We name the trade-off behind every choice.

The one stream we'll trace

Every time someone follows a short link, the redirect server emits a tiny event — a fact that already happened: {code:"a9Z", ts:…, country:"IN", paid:true}. Two downstream jobs consume the same stream: an analytics consumer that increments a per-link click counter, and a billing consumer that meters paid customers' usage. Neither can keep up if it has to run inline on the redirect's hot path, so the events go into a message queue in the middle. Here is the whole shape on one screen.

  ONE EVENT STREAM, TWO INDEPENDENT READERS

  redirect servers          THE LOG (per-key, append-only, kept 7 days)
  (producers)        partition 0:  [e0][e1][e2][e3][e4][e5]►   append ──►
      │  emit                         ▲committed            ▲head
      ├──────────────►  partition 1:  [.][.][.][.][.]►
      │                partition 2:  [.][.][.][.]►
      ▼                       …  (20 partitions total)
                                 │                    │
                  ┌──────────────┘                    └───────────────┐
                  ▼ consumer group "analytics"        ▼ consumer group "billing"
            count clicks per code                meter usage per paid customer
            (its OWN offset per partition)       (its OWN offset, reads same data)

  The log is kept, not consumed-away. Each group remembers HOW FAR it has read.

A message queue is a buffer in the middle that lets producers and consumers run at different speeds and survive each other's outages. A log-based queue (think Kafka) is a specific shape: an append-only sequence of events, kept for a retention window, where each reader tracks its own position — so the data isn't deleted when one reader reads it, and a second reader (or the same reader after a crash) can re-read from any point. That last property is the whole reason this lesson exists.

Words we'll earn before we lean on them

  • Event — an immutable record of something that already happened (a click), not a command to do something.
  • Log — an append-only, ordered sequence of events. New events go on the end; nothing is edited.
  • Offset — a position in the log: "reader X has consumed up to event #4,217." Just a number.
  • Partition — one independent sub-log. The stream is split into many so it can be read in parallel.
  • Consumer group — a set of workers that share the partitions of a stream and track one set of offsets together.
  • Commit — to durably record "I've processed up to this offset," so a restart resumes from there.

Move 1 — Estimate: how much stream, how many partitions?

Sizing comes first, because it decides the shape of everything downstream. Two numbers drive a log: the throughput (events/sec) and the retention (how long you keep them). From those fall the partition count and the storage bill.

Worked: sizing the click stream.

Peak redirects = 100,000 clicks/second. One event each, ~200 bytes on the wire.

• Ingest rate = 100,000 × 200 B = 20,000,000 B/s = 20 MB/s.
• Events per day = 100,000 × 86,400 s = 8.64 billion events/day.
• Retention = 7 days. Storage = 20 MB/s × 86,400 s/day × 7 = 20 × 10⁶ × 604,800 ≈ 12.1 TB for the full window (before replication).

Partitions. One consumer instance can process about 10,000 events/sec (it does a counter update and a network write per event). To absorb 100,000/s we need at least 100,000 ÷ 10,000 = 10 consumers reading in parallel — and a partition can only be read by one consumer in a group at a time, so we need ≥ 10 partitions. Round up to 20 for headroom and future growth: each partition then carries 100,000 ÷ 20 = 5,000 events/s, a comfortable half of one consumer's ceiling.

Name the trade-off: the partition count is a dial between parallelism and order. More partitions = more consumers can run at once = more throughput — but ordering is only ever guaranteed within a single partition, never across them. So every partition you add buys speed and spends global order. We'll feel the bite of that in Move 3.

Move 2 — Model: the log, the offset, the consumer group

A traditional queue (think a mailbox or SQS) deletes a message once a worker acknowledges it — the message is gone, and a second team that wanted the same data is out of luck. A log does the opposite: it keeps every event for the whole retention window and hands each reader a bookmark.

 Traditional queue (mailbox)Log (this lesson)
After readmessage deletedevent stays for the retention window
Positionthe broker tracks per-message ackeach consumer group tracks one offset per partition
Re-read / replaynot possible once ackedrewind the offset and re-read
Two independent readersneed two copies / fan-outtwo groups read the same log at their own pace

The single most important variable in the whole design is the committed offset: the last position a consumer group has durably said "done" up to. A crash doesn't lose your place — you resume from the committed offset. But when you commit, relative to when you do the work, is the entire game of delivery semantics. Watch this gap.

  ONE PARTITION, ONE CONSUMER — the order of two steps decides everything

     read e7 ─► DO THE WORK (update counter) ─► COMMIT offset=7
               └───────────── the danger gap ─────────────┘
                  a crash anywhere in here changes the semantics:
                    commit BEFORE work  → crash loses e7   (at-most-once)
                    commit AFTER  work  → crash repeats e7 (at-least-once)

Move 3 — Trace the paths

Path A — delivery semantics (the order of two steps)

There are exactly three things that can happen to an event when a consumer can crash mid-flight. They differ only by where you put the commit.

Worked: the billing consumer crashes. It reads click e7 for a paid customer and must (1) add a $0.001 charge and (2) commit offset 7.

At-most-once — commit first, then charge. The worker commits offset 7, then crashes before the charge lands. On restart it resumes at offset 8. The charge is lost — the customer used the link free. Fast, no duplicates, but it drops work on failure.

At-least-once — charge first, then commit. The worker adds the $0.001 charge, then crashes before committing. On restart it re-reads offset 7 and charges again — the customer is billed twice for one click. Nothing is ever lost, but duplicates happen on every crash/retry.

Real systems almost always choose at-least-once, because losing data silently is worse than handling a duplicate you can see. But then you owe a defense against the duplicate. That defense is the thing everyone calls "exactly-once" — and it is worth saying plainly what that phrase really means.

Worked: there is no exactly-once delivery. Picture the worker finishing the charge and sending "commit offset 7" to the broker. The network drops the acknowledgement. Did the broker record the commit or not? The worker cannot tell. Its only two moves are: assume yes (and risk having lost the commit → at-most-once) or assume no and resend / re-read (→ at-least-once). Across an unreliable network you must pick one; "exactly-once on the wire" is not on the menu. This is the same two-generals impossibility we keep bumping into.

So how do systems advertise "exactly-once"? They give you at-least-once delivery + a way to make the duplicate harmless — sometimes called effectively-once. Two honest implementations:

• Idempotent effect — tag each event with a unique event_id and have the consumer record processed IDs (a dedup set / a unique key on the charge row). A re-delivered e7 finds its ID already present and skips the second charge. (This is exactly the idempotency key from Lessons 05 and 07, applied to the consumer.)
• Transactional commit — write the output and the new offset in one atomic transaction, so either both land or neither does. A crash can never leave "charged but not committed." This is what brokers mean by built-in "exactly-once" — it works only when the output store can share the transaction.

Name the trade-off: delivery semantics is a dial between lost work and duplicate work. At-most-once never duplicates but drops on failure; at-least-once never drops but duplicates on failure. There is no third native option — "exactly-once" is at-least-once with the duplicate neutralized by idempotency or a shared transaction, and you pay for it in dedup storage or a tighter coupling to the output store.

Path B — ordering (and what it costs)

Some work doesn't care about order: incrementing a click counter is commutative — +1 then +1 gives the same total in any order. But suppose we also stream link-lifecycle events — created → disabled → re-enabled for a code. Apply those out of order and the link ends up in the wrong final state (disabled when it should be live). Order matters per code, and a log only promises order within one partition.

  PARTITION BY KEY  →  all events for one code land in one partition, in order

   hash("a9Z") % 20 = 3   ┌► partition 3:  created ─ disabled ─ enabled   ✓ in order
   hash("a9Z") % 20 = 3   │
   every "a9Z" event ─────┘   (same key → same partition → consumed in sequence)

   RANDOM partitioning      → "a9Z" events scatter across partitions,
                              consumed in parallel → final state can be WRONG

The fix is to partition by key: choose the partition from hash(code) % 20, so every event for code a9Z is pinned to the same partition and read in the order it was produced. We get per-key ordering for free. But pinning a key to a partition is exactly the move that creates a hot partition.

Worked: the celebrity link. A single viral code is being clicked 30,000 times/second during a campaign. With partition-by-key, all 30,000/s hash to one partition. That partition's lone consumer tops out at 10,000 events/s, so it falls behind by 20,000 events every second while the other 19 partitions sit half-idle. Ordering forced the hot key onto one lane, and one lane can't be widened. Random partitioning would have spread those 30,000/s evenly — at the cost of the per-code ordering we needed.

Name the trade-off: ordering vs load spread. Partition-by-key buys per-key order and pays with hot partitions (the same hot-key problem from Lessons 03 and 06, wearing a new hat). Random partitioning buys even load and pays with lost ordering. You only get to keep one for any given key — so order only the keys that truly need it, and let the commutative ones (like our click count) spread freely.

Move 4 — Find the next bottleneck: lag, poison, and rebalance

The log happily buffers everything; the danger is on the read side. Three walls show up in order.

Consumer lag — the gap between head and committed

Worked: a producer spike opens a gap. A campaign pushes producers to 150,000 events/s while consumers max out at 20 partitions × 5,000/s = 100,000/s. Lag grows at 150,000 − 100,000 = 50,000 events/sec. After one minute of spike the backlog is 50,000 × 60 = 3,000,000 events — that's the consumer lag. The log stores it fine (3M × 200 B = 600 MB, trivial against 12 TB), but end-to-end latency balloons: an event now waits behind 3M others. When the spike ends and producers fall to 80,000/s, drain rate = 100,000 − 80,000 = 20,000/s, so clearing 3M takes 3,000,000 ÷ 20,000 = 150 seconds. Lag is the single number to alarm on: if it only ever grows, consumers are permanently too slow and no buffer saves you.

The poison message — one event that blocks a whole partition

In a log you can't just "skip" a bad message the way a mailbox can hide one. If event e7 is malformed and the consumer throws every time it tries, it can't advance the offset past 7 without either losing order or looping forever — and every event behind it in that partition is now stuck. One bad event has stalled 5,000 events/second.

The fix is the dead-letter queue (DLQ) from Lesson 05, applied here: retry e7 a bounded number of times (say 3); if it still fails, write it to a separate DLQ topic for humans to inspect, then commit offset 7 and move on. The partition unblocks; the poison is quarantined, not lost.

  POISON HANDLING — bound the retries, quarantine, then advance

   e7 fails ─► retry 1 ─► retry 2 ─► retry 3 ─► still failing?
                                                   │
                                       ┌───────────┴───────────┐
                                       ▼                       ▼
                              copy e7 → DLQ topic     commit offset 7, read e8
                              (humans inspect)        (partition flows again)

The rebalance storm — and the next course

When a consumer crashes or you add one, the group must reassign partitions — a rebalance. During it, consumers briefly stop. If a flaky consumer flaps in and out, the group rebalances over and over and barely makes progress — a small coordination problem: who owns which partition, and who decides? That question — electing a coordinator, agreeing on membership without two leaders disagreeing (split-brain) — is its own subject, and it's exactly where we go next.

Where we landed

We took one 100,000-events/sec click stream and ran it through a log. Estimate sized it — 20 MB/s, 12 TB over 7 days — and set 20 partitions, naming the parallelism-vs-order dial. Model drew the log against a mailbox: events kept, each group tracking its own committed offset, with the danger gap between doing work and committing. Tracing the paths walked the three delivery semantics (at-most-once loses, at-least-once duplicates, "exactly-once" = at-least-once + idempotency or a shared transaction — there is no exactly-once on the wire), then ordering: partition-by-key buys per-key order and pays with a hot partition. The next bottleneck was the read side — consumer lag as the one number to alarm on, the poison message that blocks a partition until a DLQ quarantines it, and the rebalance storm that opens the door to coordination. The thread through all of it: a log is just an ordered list of facts plus a bookmark per reader — and almost every hard question is really "what happens to the bookmark when something crashes?"
